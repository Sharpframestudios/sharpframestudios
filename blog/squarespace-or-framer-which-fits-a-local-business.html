Squarespace or Framer: Which Fits a Local Business?

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01Two Good Tools for Different Jobs

Squarespace and Framer both let a small business build a good-looking website without writing code, and both host the site for you. The question is not which is better in general. It is which one fits the way you run your business, who will edit the site, and what you need it to do.

One disclosure first. Sharp Frame Studios designs and builds websites for a living, and we have written about Framer before, so you should weigh our view with that in mind. This post sticks to what the two companies publish, and it names the cases where Squarespace is the more sensible choice.

As always with software pricing, the figures below were read from each company’s pricing page in November 2026 and may change. Check the current page before you commit.

02The Editing Experience

Squarespace is built around a drag-and-drop editor. Its pricing page names Fluid Engine as its newest editing experience on version 7.1 sites, and lists the drag-and-drop editor as part of every plan. For an owner who wants to change a photo, a price or an opening time without calling anyone, that is the draw. The structure is guided, and it is hard to break the layout.

Framer works more like a design canvas. Its help center describes the canvas as the place to design pages and interactions. That gives a designer more freedom over spacing, animation and layout, and it is why Framer sites often look less like a template. The tradeoff is that a site with many custom details can be harder for a beginner to edit confidently, unless the designer sets up the pieces you will change in a simple way.

A practical way to think about it: Squarespace asks you to work within its structure and rewards you with simplicity, while Framer gives a designer room and asks the owner to rely on that designer for bigger changes. If you will update the site yourself every week, lean toward the first. If you update it a few times a year and want a distinctive look, the second becomes more attractive.

03Design Freedom and Content

Think too about who will keep the site healthy. On either platform, someone has to update hours, remove expired offers and check that forms still reach the right inbox. A site that depends on a single person who has since moved on tends to go out of date within a season, so name an owner for it and write down where the logins live.

Both platforms offer templates and both can be customized. Squarespace sites tend to share a recognizable look because many owners stay close to the templates. That is not a flaw for a plumber or a tax preparer, whose customers want clarity rather than novelty.

Framer’s plans show how it thinks about content. According to Framer’s pricing page, the Basic plan includes 30 pages, 2 CMS collections and 1,000 CMS items, and the Pro plan includes 150 pages, 10 collections and 2,500 items. CMS means the content system for repeating items such as blog posts, menu items or team members. For most local business sites, 30 pages is plenty, but a business with a large directory or many locations would need to look at the higher plan.

Squarespace has its own blogging and page tools, and Sarasota-area businesses with a steady stream of events or posts can run them well on it. On both platforms, a site you never update will look stale however well it was designed.

04Selling and Booking Online

If you sell products, Squarespace has the clearer answer. According to its pricing page, online stores with Stripe or PayPal checkout are available on all four of its plans. Transaction fees differ by plan: the Basic plan lists a 2 percent fee on online store transactions, while the Core, Plus and Advanced plans list none. Payment processors charge their own card fees on top, which the page lists separately.

Framer is a different story. We checked Framer’s pricing page and its help center, and neither lists a built-in online store. A shop on a Framer site would usually mean connecting a third-party service or adding custom work. That is workable for a handful of products, but it is not the one-stop setup Squarespace offers.

For appointments, Squarespace points to a scheduling product on its site, which the company now calls Acuity Scheduling. Framer sites commonly use an outside booking tool embedded on a page. Either way, check whether you want a booking tool that works with the platform you pick, since a restaurant, salon or fitness studio may rely on it daily.

Be realistic about volume as well. A restaurant that takes a few catering orders a week can use a form or an ordering link, while a business shipping dozens of orders a day needs inventory, shipping and tax tools that match. Ask what you will do on the busiest day of your year, such as the first week of season, and choose the setup that still works then.

A good rule: if selling is the core of the business, start with a platform built for it. If the site is mainly a brochure that leads to calls, forms and bookings, both fit.

05What They Cost

Based on Framer’s pricing page, there is a free plan, a Basic plan at $10 per month and a Pro plan at $30 per month. The page says prices are monthly and billed according to the billing cycle you choose at checkout, and it mentions that a complimentary domain is included on yearly billing. We could not confirm the separate yearly amounts, so look at the page before you compare.

Squarespace’s pricing page lists four plans, with two figures for each depending on whether you pay monthly or annually: Basic at $25 or $19 per month, Core at $39 or $29, Plus at $65 or $49, and Advanced at $139 or $99. The lower figure belongs to annual billing. The page offers a 14-day free trial, and says annual plans include one year of free domain registration on eligible domains, after which the domain renews at the standard rate.

Notice what these numbers leave out. They are the platform fees, not the cost of a website. They do not include design work, writing, photography, setup, email, a booking tool or the time you spend. Scope drives the price of a real project, and the platform subscription is a small part of it. For how we think about that, see our pricing section.

06Which One Fits Which Business

Some typical matches, offered as rules of thumb rather than rules:

  • A one-person service business that wants a clean site it can edit alone, such as a bookkeeper in Venice or a pet sitter in Lakewood Ranch, often does well on Squarespace.
  • A business that sells products online, from a boutique on Main Street to a small food producer, will find the store tools more mature at Squarespace.
  • A restaurant, studio, clinic or design-minded retailer that wants a distinctive look and mostly updates hours, menus and photos, is a good fit for Framer, especially with a designer who sets up the editable parts.
  • A business with a content-heavy site, such as many services, locations or articles, should compare the page and CMS limits on both before choosing.

Wherever you land, you should own your domain, keep your content in a form you can export, and avoid being tied to a person who is the only one who can log in. Our guide to Framer versus WordPress for a small business website makes similar points about owning your site, and what Framer is and why designers use it explains the tool in more detail.

07Making the Decision

Before choosing, write down four things: what you need the site to do in its first year, who will update it and how often, whether you will sell or take bookings directly, and how much you want it to stand apart from competitors. Those answers narrow the choice faster than any feature list.

Neither platform makes a site show up on Google by itself, and no one can promise a ranking on either one. What helps is clear pages, accurate business details, fast loading and content that answers the questions customers ask.
