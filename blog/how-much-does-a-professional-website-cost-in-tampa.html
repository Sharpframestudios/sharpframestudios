How Much Does a Professional Website Cost in Tampa?

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01Why Published Ranges Vary So Much

Ask three sources what a small business website costs and you may get three very different answers. That is not because anyone is lying. A one-page site built by the owner on a template and a custom site for a restaurant with online ordering are different products that happen to share the word website.

The figures below come from published guides, not from Tampa surveys, and each of the companies behind them sells website services. Read them as a rough map of the market, not as quotes. Prices also change, so check current numbers before you budget.

For Tampa business owners the main takeaway is that scope, meaning what you need the site to do, drives price far more than location or fashion. Two businesses on the same street can reasonably pay very different amounts because one needs five simple pages and the other needs bookings, photography and a menu that changes weekly.

02What the Published Ranges Say

Wix, a website builder, breaks costs into three routes. According to Wix, building the site yourself on a builder plan runs from about $17 to $200 a month, which it estimates at $204 to $2,400 in the first year. Hiring a freelancer adds designer or developer fees that it puts at $500 to $10,000 or more on top of the platform subscription, and it lists small agency work at $3,000 to $10,000 or more for fully customized sites.

WebFX, a marketing agency, gives a wider spread. It reports that the average cost to build a small business website ranges from $1,000 to $48,000, depending on complexity, features and who builds it. Its component figures include design at $2,000 to $15,000, with basic small business design at roughly $2,000 to $5,000, and small agencies at $6,000 to $12,000.

These sources disagree at the edges, which is normal. A simple, well-scoped site from a small studio or freelancer can land near the lower or middle part of these ranges, while a large or complex build climbs quickly. Neither guide is a substitute for a written proposal based on your actual needs.

03The Ongoing Costs People Forget

The build is only the first bill. A website has recurring costs, and they are worth asking about before you sign. According to WebFX, domain names typically cost $0.99 to $15 a year to register and $10 to $60 or more to renew, and web hosting runs $10 to $200 a month, with small businesses usually on shared hosting at roughly $10 to $20 a month. Wix says its plans include hosting for the life of the site, which is one reason builder plans are priced as monthly subscriptions.

Beyond those, plan for business email, a small maintenance or support budget, and occasional updates when your services or prices change. Photos, copywriting and logos may be separate items. If your site takes payments or bookings, the payment processor and any booking tool may charge fees as well.

Hosting deserves a sentence of its own for a Gulf Coast business. If your site goes down during a storm or a busy weekend, customers cannot find your hours or your phone number, so ask any provider what happens when something breaks, who is responsible for fixing it, and how quickly they usually respond.

A useful habit is to write down the total cost over three years, not just the launch price. A cheaper build with a large monthly fee can cost more over time than a higher one-time price on a platform you can run cheaply, and the reverse can also be true.

04What Moves the Price Up or Down

When two quotes differ, the difference is almost always in scope. These are the factors that most often explain it.

  • Number of pages, and how much of the content is new. A five-page site is a smaller job than a thirty-page one.
  • Custom design versus a lightly adapted template.
  • Features such as online ordering, reservations, appointment booking, a store or a members area.
  • Who writes the words and who provides the photography.
  • Integrations with tools you already use, such as a calendar, a payment system or an email list.
  • Moving content from an old site and keeping its address structure in place.
  • Training, handover and how much support you want after launch.

To make the list concrete, imagine two Tampa businesses. A one-person accounting practice may need a home page, a services page, an about page, a contact page and a short set of frequently asked questions. A neighborhood restaurant may need the same skeleton plus a menu that staff can edit, photo galleries, reservation or ordering links and seasonal notices. The second site is not better, only larger in scope, and the price should reflect that.

Each item is a question to ask. A proposal that lists pages, features and what you must supply is easier to compare than a single number. Our guide on hiring a web designer has a longer list of questions on ownership, scope and support.

05Spending in Proportion to the Business

There is no single right budget. A solo tradesperson who mainly gets work by referral may need a clean, credible, fast site with a clear phone number and a few good photos. A medical practice, a restaurant group or a firm competing for high-value clients has more to gain from a deeper build and more to lose from a weak one.

One way to think about it is to estimate what a single new customer is worth to you over a year, and how many customers you expect the site to help you win or keep. That is a rough exercise, and it does not guarantee a result, but it puts the cost in context. It also helps to decide what you do not need yet. A site can start small and grow.

Tampa Bay is a large and competitive market, and customers compare several options before calling. A site that loads quickly on a phone, states what you do, and shows how to reach you often matters more than an elaborate design. Our piece on what Tampa businesses need from a website looks at that in more detail.

06How to Compare Quotes Fairly

When quotes arrive, put them side by side and read them for what is included, not just the total. Check who owns the domain and the site account, how many rounds of revisions are included, what happens if you supply content late, and what support costs after launch. If a quote is much lower than the rest, find out what has been left out. If it is much higher, ask what the extra covers.

Ask for the proposal in writing, with the deliverables, the dates and the payment schedule spelled out. Most projects are paid in stages, such as a deposit, a midpoint and a final payment at launch, and you should know what triggers each one. It is also reasonable to ask for two or three references you can contact, and to check that their sites still work well on your phone.

Be careful with anyone who promises search rankings as part of a website price. No one can guarantee a position on Google, and a fixed promise of that kind deserves questions. A good website gives search engines and customers clear information, and beyond that the results depend on many things outside a designer's control.
