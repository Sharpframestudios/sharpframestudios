Restaurant Online Ordering: Toast, Square, ChowNow or Delivery Apps

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01Three Things People Mean by Online Ordering

When a restaurant owner says “online ordering,” the phrase can mean three different things, and the costs are not alike. The first is a delivery marketplace such as DoorDash, Uber Eats or Grubhub, where an app brings you customers and you pay a commission on every order. The second is ordering software that runs on your own website, often tied to your point-of-sale system, such as Toast or Square. The third is a dedicated ordering service such as ChowNow, which sits somewhere in between.

Most restaurants end up using more than one. The useful question is not which single tool wins, but which orders you are willing to pay a percentage on and which ones you would rather keep. This article compares the published fee structures as of late 2026, explains what each option does and does not give you, and finishes with a simple way to decide.

A note on sources and timing. These prices change often, so treat every figure as a snapshot and confirm it with the company before you sign. Some of the pricing write-ups we found were published by companies that sell competing ordering products, and we say so where it matters. Nobody can tell you your exact cost without your menu, your average order and your volume.

02What the Delivery Marketplaces Charge

According to Food On Demand, a trade publication, in a story dated April 1, 2026, Uber Eats raised its marketplace fees in March 2026. It reports that the Lite plan went from 15 to 20 percent, the Plus plan stayed at 25 percent but rose to 30 percent for orders from Uber One members, and Premium stayed at 30 percent. Pickup orders rose from 6 to 7 percent when in-store pricing is validated, and the self-delivery rate stayed at 15 percent.

The same report lists DoorDash partnership plans called Basic, Plus and Premier at 15, 25 and 30 percent, with a 6 percent rate for qualifying U.S. pickup orders. For Grubhub it lists Basic, Plus and All-access plans at 5, 15 and 20 percent marketing commission, with an extra 10 percent when Grubhub handles the delivery. Grubhub adds no pickup fee, according to the report, but processing fees still apply.

Those are advertised commissions, and they are not the whole bill. Sauce, a company that sells commission-free ordering and so has a stake in this comparison, says card processing usually adds 2 to 3 percent and that owners report real costs closer to 30 to 40 percent once marketing and placement fees are included. Treat that 40 percent as something owners say rather than a measured number. It does explain why so many restaurants feel that delivery apps are expensive.

What you get for the fee is real: customers who open an app looking for dinner, and drivers you do not have to employ. What you usually do not get is the customer’s email address or phone number, and the app decides how your menu is displayed.

03What Ordering on Your Own Website Costs

Ordering on your own website avoids the marketplace commission, but it is not free. You pay for the software and for card processing, and you handle pickup or delivery yourself. Three examples show the range.

  • Toast: according to a January 2026 pricing write-up from Sauce, Toast lists a pay-as-you-go starter plan at $0 a month with processing of roughly 3.09 to 3.69 percent plus 15 cents a transaction, and a subscription plan from about $69 a month per terminal with roughly 2.49 percent plus 15 cents. The same write-up puts the online ordering add-on at about $75 a month. These are a competitor’s figures, so check Toast’s own pricing page.
  • Square: NerdWallet, in an article updated in February 2026, lists Square’s processing as starting at 2.6 percent plus 15 cents, against 2.49 percent plus 15 cents for Toast. It describes Square as more cost-effective for cafes, quick-service restaurants and bars. Online card rates are often different from in-person rates, so ask for the online figure specifically.
  • ChowNow: Capterra lists ChowNow as starting at $229 a month, with tiers shown at $229, $319 and $409 a month. Sauce’s write-up gives a lower range, so the two sources disagree. The company’s own quote is the number to trust.

The pattern is that these tools trade a share of every order for a monthly fee plus processing. At low volume a flat monthly fee can cost more than a commission would. At higher volume it usually costs less. That break-even point is the center of the whole comparison.

04A Simple Way to Compare, With Made-Up Numbers

Here is an illustration, not a prediction. Say your restaurant takes 100 online orders in a month at an average of $35, which is $3,500. At a 25 percent marketplace commission, the fee is $875 before any processing charge. Through your own website, with an ordering add-on of about $75 and card processing of roughly 3 percent, the same $3,500 costs about $75 plus $105, or roughly $180.

The gap is large, but the arithmetic does not tell the whole story. A marketplace order may be one you would never have received at all, because that customer found you inside the app. An order from a regular who would have phoned you anyway is a different case: paying 25 percent to take an order you already had is the expensive kind.

A better question is how many of your app orders come from new customers and how many come from people who already know you. You can get a rough answer by reading through a month of orders and asking your staff which names they recognize. Then run the same math with your own average ticket and volume. If the numbers only work at high volume, that is worth knowing before you commit to a contract.

05Owning the Customer Relationship

The strongest argument for ordering on your own site is not the fee. It is that the customer belongs to you. With your own system you usually receive the name, email address and order history, with the customer’s permission, and you can tell them when the stone crab special returns or when you have reopened after a storm. On a marketplace the app typically keeps that information, and the same customer sees your competitors one tap away.

That matters in a market like Sarasota, where business swings with the seasons. A list of local regulars and returning winter residents is worth something in September, when traffic is slow, and again when the snowbirds come back. If you have to close for a hurricane, a message to your own list is a faster way to say you are open again than waiting for people to check an app.

A site that takes orders also has to be a good site. The menu should read well on a phone, the hours and the order button should be easy to find, and the page should load quickly. Our guide to what a restaurant website needs in 2027 and what it can skip covers those basics.

06A Practical Setup for a Local Restaurant

For many small restaurants the sensible setup is a mix: keep one or two delivery apps for reach, and send your regulars to your own ordering page. A few ways to make that happen:

  • Put your direct ordering link first on your website, your Google profile and your Instagram bio, with the app links second.
  • Print the web address or a QR code on takeout bags and receipts, so a customer who ordered through an app sees where to order next time.
  • Watch your menu pricing. Food On Demand notes that Uber Eats’ lower pickup rate applies when in-store pricing is validated, so how you price across channels can affect your fee. Read each platform’s current terms before you change anything.
  • Ask every provider about contract length, setup fees and what happens to your customer data if you leave.
