Website Subscription vs One-Time Build: Comparing the Two Pricing Models

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01Three Offers, Three Different Units

A business owner pricing a website this fall will run into three kinds of offers. The first is a website builder such as Squarespace or Wix: a monthly fee, and you build the site yourself. The second is a done-for-you site with nothing down and a monthly fee for as long as the site stays online. The third is the traditional arrangement, where you pay a designer once for the build and then cover small running costs.

They are hard to compare because the prices come in different units. One is $17 a month, one is $150 a month, and one is a single invoice in the thousands. A fair website builder cost comparison puts all three on the same footing by picking a period and adding up everything paid inside it.

This post uses three years. It then looks at two things a total does not show: who owns what, and what happens when you leave.

Every price below comes from a named page that we read in November 2026. Most of those pages belong to companies that sell websites, so treat the figures as real examples and not as market averages. Prices in this area change often, so check the current page before you budget.

02What the Monthly Models Charge

Start with the builders. A Wix blog article by Cecilia Lazzaro Blasbalg, which states that its plans and pricing are current as of November 2026, lists the Light plan at $17 per month, Core at $29 and Business at $39. The article says those are prices for a yearly subscription, paid in full at the time of purchase and before tax.

For Squarespace, an article by NoJoke Agency, a Swiss studio that builds on the platform, reports annual-billing prices of $19 per month for the Basic plan and $29 for Core, or $25 and $39 when billed month to month. The same article says Squarespace emailed customers in July 2026 about a price rise on annual plans that applies at each customer’s next renewal. So the price you start at is not fixed for the life of the site.

Both sources describe the same domain arrangement. A custom domain is free for the first year on an annual plan, and after that it becomes its own bill. NoJoke Agency puts the Squarespace renewal at about $20 a year.

Done-for-you subscriptions cost more, because someone else does the building. Hello Web Designs, in an article by Josh Blalock dated July 22, 2026, describes its own plan as $150 per month with $0 down, covering the build, up to 20 pages, hosting and unlimited edits. The article explains the model plainly: the designer covers the cost of the build and recoups it over time through the monthly payments.

Other sellers sit lower. RateGather advertises a five-page site with hosting for $33 per month and no setup fee. AgentSite lists a Starter plan at $49 per month billed yearly, which it states as $588 paid once for the year, for one site of up to eight pages. Five pages, eight pages, and twenty pages with unlimited edits are different products, so read what is included before comparing the monthly numbers.

03What a One-Time Build Charges

Published ranges for a paid build are wide. A Liquid Web article by Mohammed Noufal, last modified on May 20, 2026 according to the page, puts a small business site built by a freelancer at $500 to $10,000 or more upfront, and an agency or custom site at $3,000 to $30,000 or more. A WebFX pricing guide, modified on May 15, 2026, gives the same two ranges. Neither page explains in detail how the ranges were produced, so read them as broad estimates.

A more useful number comes from Hello Web Designs, because that company prices the same site both ways. Next to its $150 monthly plan, its article lists a lump-sum option at $3,500 one time, followed by $25 per month for hosting, or $50 per month if you want edits included.

That second part matters. A one-time build is never the only payment. Liquid Web’s table shows $15 to $150 or more per month in running costs for a freelancer-built small business site. We went through those bills line by line in our post on what a website really costs each year after launch.

04The Three-Year Math

Here is what 36 months costs under each model. The prices are the published ones above. The multiplication is ours.

  • A builder you run yourself: $612 on Wix Light at $17 per month, or $1,044 on a $29 plan such as Wix Core or Squarespace Core. Add the domain from year two onward, and your own hours.
  • A done-for-you subscription: $1,188 at RateGather’s $33 per month, or $5,400 at Hello Web Designs’ $150 per month.
  • A paid build at Hello Web Designs’ lump-sum price: $3,500 plus $25 per month for hosting comes to $4,400. With the $50 edits plan it comes to $5,300.

The interesting comparison is the last two, because the same company sells both. At three years, the $150 subscription and the paid build with edits are $100 apart. The two lines cross at month 35, when each has cost $5,250.

After that they separate. The subscription keeps costing $1,800 a year. The paid build with edits costs $600 a year. By the end of year five the subscription has cost $9,000 and the paid build $6,500, or $5,000 with hosting only.

So the honest answer depends on two things: how long you expect to keep the site, and how much cash you have now. Picture a pressure washing business starting up in Bradenton with one truck and a thin first season. Paying $150 a month instead of $3,500 upfront may be the right call there, even though it costs more by year five.

An established shop in downtown Venice that plans to keep the same site for six or seven years is in a different position. It pays less in total by paying upfront. Seasonal businesses on the Gulf Coast should also remember that a monthly fee bills in August exactly as it does in February, when the snowbirds are here and the register is busy.

The builder is the cheapest column by a wide margin, and for some businesses it is the right one. What the total leaves out is the time it takes to plan, write, design and maintain the site yourself.

05Ownership: What Is Actually Yours

A website is several separate things: the domain, the words and photos, the design, and the accounts everything lives in. Under each pricing model, ask who holds each one.

The domain is the most important. Hello Web Designs says in its article that you own your domain either way, and AgentSite says the domain connects in your name. With a builder’s free first-year domain, the domain is registered through the builder. The Wix article says its voucher is valid only for a new domain registered through Wix. Wherever it is registered, make sure the account is in your name. Our post on where to buy and keep your business domain explains why.

The design is where the models differ most. RateGather’s monthly page does not address who owns the site, but it offers a separate $397 one-time website for people who want to own it outright, which suggests the monthly version works differently. Hello Web Designs’ article does not say who owns the design if you cancel, and it advises readers to ask any provider exactly what happens to the site itself if they leave. AgentSite says you own your site and content and can still download the pages as HTML files after you stop paying.

Builders have their own version of this. You own your text and photos, but the design lives on the platform. Squarespace’s help page on exporting, last updated on October 9, 2026, says a site exports as an .xml file in WordPress format, and it lists style settings and custom CSS among the things that do not export. Leaving a builder means carrying your content out and rebuilding the design elsewhere.

That applies to a paid build too, if it was built on one of those platforms. The difference is that the platform account is yours, and the site stays online for as long as you pay the platform’s plan, whether or not you ever speak to the designer again.

06Exit Terms to Read Before You Sign

Subscription terms vary more than the prices do. Hello Web Designs’ article says some $0 down offers lock a customer into a 12 or 24-month contract, while its own service can be canceled at any time. RateGather states no contract, no minimum term and no cancellation fee. AgentSite says the first year is paid upfront and the site stays live to the end of the paid year if you turn off renewal.

Since no two are alike, ask every provider the same questions and get the answers in writing.

  • Is there a minimum term, and what do I owe if I leave before it ends?
  • What happens to the live site the day after I cancel?
  • Can I buy the site outright later, and at what price?
  • Whose name are the domain, the hosting and the platform accounts in?
  • Can the monthly price rise, and how much notice do I get?
  • What counts as an edit, and how quickly are edits made?

The same questions apply to a one-time build. Ask what is handed over on launch day: the logins, the files, and the accounts.

Neither model is the better one in general. A subscription suits a business that needs to protect cash now and wants edits handled without a separate invoice. A paid build suits a business that expects to keep its site for years and wants the lowest long-run total and the clearest ownership. A builder suits an owner with more time than budget.
