How to Choose a Domain Name for Your Business

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01A Domain Name Is Hard to Change Once People Use It

Your domain name is the address customers type, the ending of your email addresses and the text printed on your truck, your menu and your business cards. Changing it later is possible, but every printed piece, every listing and every link pointing to the old address then has to be updated. That is why it is worth an hour of thought before you register anything.

Most small business owners find the choice harder than it looks, because the name they want is often taken. This article walks through the rules of thumb that hold up, how to check availability, and how to stay out of trademark trouble. None of it is legal advice, and if a name matters a great deal to your business, a trademark attorney is the right person to ask.

02Start With the Rules of Thumb That Hold Up

Network Solutions, a long-running domain registrar, advises keeping a domain name short and memorable so customers can remember and share it easily. It also says hyphens make a name harder to say, remember and type, and that numbers cause confusion because people will not know whether to type a numeral or spell the word out. These are practical points more than strict rules, and they match how people actually use an address.

The best check is the phone test. Say the address out loud to a friend, the way you would on a call or in a radio ad, and ask them to type it. If they have to ask whether the word has one L or two, or whether it is plural, you will lose some visitors at that step. A spelling twist looks clever on a sign and fails the moment someone tries to find you.

A few more habits help in practice:

  • Choose a name you can say in one breath, and avoid strings of words that run together into something unintended when read without spaces.
  • Pick words your customers already use. A Venice landscaper is more likely to be searched for as a landscaper than by a coined word nobody has heard.
  • Avoid a name so narrow that it boxes the business in. A name built around one service or one neighborhood becomes awkward when you add a second service or open in Bradenton.
  • Check that the name does not read badly in capital letters or in an email address, where there are no spaces to help.

03Which Ending to Use: .com or Something Else

Network Solutions says .com remains the most widely recognized and trusted ending, and that newer endings such as .online, .store, .shop and .tech can work when the .com is not available. For a local business that wants customers to type an address from memory, a .com is still the safest default, because it is what people tend to try first.

If the .com you want is taken, look closely at what is on it before deciding. A parked page or an unrelated business in another state is different from a direct competitor in your market. Adding a hyphen or a number to dodge the conflict usually creates the problems described above, and a slightly different word is normally a better fix.

Google's own search documentation is relaxed about this choice. It says keywords in the name of a domain alone have hardly any effect beyond appearing in breadcrumbs, and that Google Search does not care which ending you use. It also advises that when you pick the name of your site, you should do what is best for your business. So treat the name as a branding and memory decision. No one can guarantee a position on Google, and a keyword-stuffed address is not a shortcut to one.

04Check Availability Beyond the Registrar's Search Box

A registrar will tell you whether an exact address is free, but that is only the first check. Before you commit, look at the name the way a customer or a competitor would.

  • Search the name on Google and on the map listings for your area, and see whether another business already trades under it or something close to it.
  • Check whether matching social media handles are available. Network Solutions suggests securing matching handles where possible so your branding stays consistent across platforms, which matters if you plan to post regularly.
  • Look at similar spellings and the plural or singular form of the name, because customers will mistype and a competitor holding the near-miss address can catch that traffic.
  • Decide on a business email address at the same time, since an address ending in your own domain looks more established than a free webmail account.

Also think about what happens when you register. Make sure the domain is registered in your name or your company's name, with an email address you control, and not in the name of a freelancer or a past employee. If a domain is registered to someone else, they control it, and getting it back can be slow. 05Avoid Trademark Trouble Before You Print Anything

A domain name is not the same thing as a trademark. Owning an address does not give you the right to use a name that another business already holds as a mark for similar goods or services. The United States Patent and Trademark Office describes a trademark search as a search you complete to make sure your mark is available and that no other trademark conflicts with it. Its free Trademark Search system, at tmsearch.uspto.gov, is where to start.

Run your proposed name there before you register it, and try variations of the spelling. Pay attention to businesses in the same line of work as yours, since conflicts usually turn on similar names used for similar services. A search will not settle every question, because common-law rights can exist without a federal registration, so a plain web search belongs alongside it.

The stakes are real, though they are not usually dramatic for small local businesses. ICANN, the organization that coordinates the domain system, notes that all registrars must follow the Uniform Domain-Name Dispute-Resolution Policy, a process that lets trademark owners file complaints about abusive registrations without going to court. Losing a domain that way after you have built a customer base on it is an expensive lesson. If you are building a brand you hope to grow, an hour with a trademark attorney costs far less than a rebrand.

The reverse applies too. Do not register someone else's brand name, or a close variant of it, hoping to resell it or to catch their customers. That is the behavior these dispute processes exist to stop.

06What a Domain Costs and How to Register It

A domain is an annual fee, not a one-time purchase. According to Domain Name Wire, Verisign, the registry that runs .com, announced that its wholesale fee for each .com registration and renewal would rise 7 percent, from $10.26 to $10.97 per year, effective November 1, 2026. That is the price registrars pay, not what you pay, and registrars set their own retail prices. Watch for the gap between a low first-year offer and the renewal price, which is often higher.

When you register, turn on automatic renewal and put a calendar reminder a month before the expiration date. Use an email address you check, since that is where renewal notices go. A lapsed domain can be picked up by someone else, and a small business that loses its address can lose its email and its website on the same day.
