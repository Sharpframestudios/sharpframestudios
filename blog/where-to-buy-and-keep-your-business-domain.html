Where to Buy and Keep Your Business Domain

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01Why the Registrar Matters More Than It Seems

A domain name is the address of your website and, usually, of your business email. The company you register it with is called the registrar, and it is the place that renews the name, holds the account login and decides how you move the domain if you ever want to leave. Most owners pick one in about five minutes and never think about it again, until a renewal notice goes to an old email address.

That is the usual way a small business gets into trouble. A website built years ago by a nephew, a former employee or a long-gone freelancer may have its domain registered under that person’s account. Everything works until the card on file expires, and then the website and the email tied to it can stop working on the same day.

This post covers where to register a domain, what to compare, and the handful of settings that keep it safe. If you are still choosing the name itself, our posts on how to choose a domain name and on which extension to use come first.

02Registrar, Host and Designer Are Three Different Things

People often use one word for all of it, so it helps to separate the pieces. The registrar holds the registration of the name. The host is the company that stores and serves the website files. The designer is whoever builds the site. One company can do all three, but they do not have to, and keeping them separate is often safer.

According to ICANN, the registered name holder has certain rights, including the right to transfer the domain to another registrar. The key word is holder. The name should be registered in your business’s name, with an email address you control, in an account you can log into. If a web company registers it for you, ask for the account details in writing, or ask that the domain be placed in an account you own.

A simple check takes a minute. ICANN points owners to whois.icann.org to look up the current registrar and the contact details on file for a domain. If the name or email shown is not yours, that is worth fixing now.

03What to Compare When You Choose

Price is the first thing most people look at, and it is the least reliable. Many registrars advertise a low first-year price and charge more at renewal, so the number to compare is the renewal price. According to a March 2026 comparison by DomainDetails, typical annual .com renewals ran about $10.46 at Cloudflare, $11.08 at Porkbun, $18.68 at Namecheap, between $18.99 and $25.00 at GoDaddy, and $20.00 at Squarespace. The same article notes that prices change, so check the registrar’s own page before you buy.

Price differences of a few dollars a year matter far less than the following:

  • Whether privacy protection is included. The same DomainDetails comparison lists it as free at Cloudflare, Porkbun, Namecheap and Squarespace, and as an added yearly cost at GoDaddy. Privacy protection keeps your home address and phone number out of public lookups.
  • Whether the registrar supports two-step login on the account, so a stolen password alone is not enough.
  • Whether support is easy to reach by chat or phone when something goes wrong, which matters more for a small office than a low price.
  • Whether the account is simple enough that you, or someone you trust, will actually log in once a year.
  • Whether the registrar lets you move the domain out without fuss. A registrar that makes transfers difficult is telling you something.

There is no single best choice for every business. A shop owner who wants to call someone may happily pay more for a registrar with phone support, while a technical owner who manages everything alone may prefer the cheapest at-cost option.

04Auto-Renew, Reminders and What Happens if a Domain Expires

Turn on auto-renew, and keep the card on file current. ICANN’s guidance for domain owners recommends the same: track the expiration date, consider auto-renewal and keep payment and contact information up to date. A card that expires in March is the most common reason a good domain lapses.

Reminders also depend on a working email address. According to ICANN, registrars must send two renewal reminders, about a month and about a week before expiration. If those go to a former employee’s inbox, nobody will see them. Use a shared address such as an office mailbox rather than one person’s name.

If a renewal is missed, there is sometimes a way back, but it is not guaranteed and it can cost more. ICANN describes two paths. Some registrars delete the name immediately, after which a 30-day redemption period applies and a fee may be charged to restore it. Others offer an auto-renew grace period of 1 to 45 days where you can still renew. After those windows, the name can be released to anyone, auctioned or kept by the registrar.

ICANN also warns that email, websites and other services tied to the name may stop working once it expires. For a Lakewood Ranch dental office or a Venice plumber, that means patients and customers cannot email you and the website shows an error. A few dollars a year does not seem like much until the name is gone.

05Locks, Transfer Codes and Moving Registrars

Most registrars put a transfer lock on a domain by default. The lock stops someone from moving the name to another registrar without your approval. ICANN says a locked domain may be denied a transfer, and that registrars must give name holders a readily accessible and reasonable way to remove the lock.

To transfer a domain, you generally need an authorization code, sometimes called an auth code or transfer code. ICANN describes it as a unique code created by the registrar to identify the domain holder and help prevent unauthorized transfers. Keep that code private, the way you would a password, and only request it when you are ready to move.

There are timing rules to plan around. According to ICANN, registrars may deny a transfer within 60 days of the initial registration or of a previous transfer. Registrars must deny a transfer during the 60-day lock that follows a change of registered name holder, so ICANN suggests finishing a transfer before you change contact information. If you are moving close to expiration, ICANN also notes that transferring during the auto-renew grace period can mean paying a renewal fee and a registration fee, so it is better to renew first.

None of this is difficult, but it explains why a domain cannot be moved in an afternoon in an emergency. Do the work while the domain is healthy.

06A Short Checklist Before You Move On

Whether you are registering a new name or reviewing an old one, these steps take about half an hour and prevent most problems:

  • Look up your domain and confirm that the registrant is your business and the email on file is one you read.
  • Log into the registrar account yourself. If you cannot, ask whoever set it up to add you or transfer the account.
  • Switch on auto-renew and check that the payment card does not expire this year.
  • Turn on two-step login for the account.
  • Confirm privacy protection is on, and that the transfer lock is enabled.
  • Write down the registrar name, the account email and the renewal date, and keep it where a business partner or family member could find it.
  • Consider registering for several years at a time if the registrar allows it, so there is less to forget.
