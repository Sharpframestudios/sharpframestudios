Dental Practice Websites: What New Patients Check First

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01A New Patient Arrives With Questions, Not Curiosity

Someone looking for a dentist is rarely browsing. A crown came loose, a child needs a first checkup, or a couple just moved to Lakewood Ranch and left their old dentist up north. They open a dental practice website with a short list of questions and leave as soon as they have answers, or as soon as it is clear they will not get them.

The list is fairly predictable. Do you take my insurance? Can you see me soon, and what if it hurts tonight? What do other patients say? How much paperwork is there before the first visit?

Some of them are hesitant to begin with. According to the American Dental Association’s Health Policy Institute, the top reasons adults give for avoiding the dentist include affordability, fear, and not being able to find a convenient location or appointment time. A website can at least answer the cost question honestly and make the appointment easy to request.

02Insurance and Payment: Answer It Before They Call

Insurance is usually the first filter. Zocdoc, the appointment booking service, analyzed hundreds of thousands of dental bookings made on its platform in 2023 and reported that nearly nine out of ten were booked using a commercial insurance plan and in-network. That is one company’s own data, not a picture of every patient, but it suggests that whether the plan is accepted is among the first things people want to know.

Many dental sites answer with a single line such as “we accept most major insurance.” That sentence tells a visitor nothing. A better insurance section does three things:

  • Lists the plans you are in-network with by name, in plain text that a person can search on the page, not only as a strip of logos.
  • Says what happens if a plan is not on the list, for example whether you will file an out-of-network claim on the patient’s behalf.
  • Explains the options for someone with no dental coverage at all, such as an in-house membership plan or payment arrangements, if you offer them.

That third point matters more on the Gulf Coast than in most places, because so many patients here are retired. Medicare.gov states that in most cases Medicare does not cover dental services like routine cleanings, fillings, tooth extractions, or items like dentures and implants. A retiree in Venice or on Longboat Key may be paying out of pocket, or may have dental benefits through some other plan and not be sure what they include. A short paragraph written for that person, saying what you accept and inviting a call to check, is worth the space.

03Emergencies and Hours: The Person Who Needs You Tonight

The second kind of visitor is in pain. The American Dental Association estimates that there are 2 million visits to hospital emergency departments for dental pain in the U.S. every year, and says an emergency department visit for dental pain can range from $400 to $1,500, compared with $90 to $200 for a visit to the dentist. Some of those people simply could not tell, at nine at night, whether a dentist would see them.

The ADA’s consumer site, MouthHealthy, tells the public that most dentists reserve time in their daily schedules for emergency patients, and advises calling the dentist with as much detail as possible. If your practice does hold time for urgent cases, your website should say so in words a frightened person can find in a few seconds.

In practice that means a few specific things:

  • A phone number at the top of every page that dials when tapped on a phone.
  • A plain statement of whether you see same-day emergencies, and whether that includes people who are not yet patients.
  • What to do after hours: an on-call number, a voicemail that is checked, or an honest instruction to go to urgent care or the emergency room for swelling, bleeding or trauma.
  • Hours that are correct, including the Friday you close at noon and the holiday weeks.

Hours deserve a second look in this region. If the office runs a longer schedule from November through April when seasonal residents return, the site should show it, and it should change back in May. A storm closure belongs on the homepage the same day. Our post on getting a website ready for snowbird season covers that routine in more detail.

Plenty of non-urgent visitors are also looking after you have gone home. In the same Zocdoc analysis, 48 percent of patients booked dental appointments between 5 p.m. and 9 a.m., and 60 percent booked on a mobile device.

04Reviews, Photos and the Florida Advertising Rule

After the practical questions comes the personal one: will I be comfortable here? People answer it by looking at faces, rooms and what other patients wrote. Zocdoc reported that dental providers with one to three office photos on their profiles received twice the bookings of those with none, and that providers with 10 to 29 reviews received nearly three times the bookings of those with one to nine.

For the website, this argues for real photographs of the dentist, the team and a treatment room in place of stock images, and for a clear link to your reviews where they actually live, so a visitor can read them unedited.

Florida also regulates what a dentist’s advertising can say, and a website is advertising. The copy of Florida Administrative Code Rule 64B5-4.002 published by Cornell Law School’s Legal Information Institute, which we read in November 2026, says that all advertising in any media must identify the Florida licensed dentist who assumes total responsibility for it, either by license number or by the dentist’s commonly used name together with the address and telephone number on file with the Department. The same rule lists kinds of advertising that are not allowed, including advertising that:

  • Contains laudatory statements about the dentist or group of dentists.
  • Relates to the quality of dental services provided as compared to other available dental services.
  • Is intended or is likely to create false, unjustified expectations of favorable results.
  • Is likely to appeal primarily to a layperson’s fears.

The rule also says the term “sleep dentistry” may not be used unless the licensee holds a valid general anesthesia permit. A headline such as “the best dentist in Sarasota” sits uneasily next to that language. We are designers, not attorneys, and rules are amended, so have the dentist or the practice’s attorney check the current text with the Florida Board of Dentistry before the site goes live. The same caution runs through our post on med spa websites and what not to claim.

05New Patient Forms and Privacy

The last thing a new patient checks is how much effort the first visit will take. Completing paperwork at home is a real convenience. It is also the part of a dental site that carries the most risk if it is built casually.

A November 2022 ADA News article put the test as three questions: is the practice transmitting protected health information through its website, storing it on a server connected to the website, or collecting it on the website? If the answer to any of them is yes, the article said, the website needs to be HIPAA-compliant. It described HIPAA-compliant web forms as a good first step, said the data collected in those forms should be encrypted, and noted that a covered dental practice with a website is required to prominently post its Notice of Privacy Practices there.

What that means for design is a clear split between two kinds of form. A general contact form asks for a name, a phone number and a preferred time, and tells people not to include medical details. A medical history or intake form is a different thing and belongs in a tool built for health information, usually the patient forms feature of the practice’s own management software or a forms service that will sign a business associate agreement. The website links to it and does not try to copy it with an ordinary form that emails answers to the front desk.

Tracking tools are part of the same conversation. Guidance from the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services on online tracking technologies says that a website letting users schedule appointments without entering credentials may involve protected health information, and that regulated entities must have a business associate agreement with tracking technology vendors that receive it. The HHS page also notes that a federal court in Texas vacated a portion of that guidance in June 2024, so this is an area to review with whoever handles compliance for the practice, not one to guess at.

Whatever forms you use, keep them short. The W3C’s forms tutorial, updated in March 2026, advises asking users to enter only what is required to complete the process, and labeling every field clearly. Our guide to contact page best practices covers the general form in more depth.

06A Ten-Minute Check of Your Own Site

You can test all of this yourself. Take out your phone, open your site as if you had never heard of the practice, and try to answer five questions without calling the office:

  • Is my insurance plan accepted, and what happens if it is not?
  • Can I be seen today for a broken tooth, and what do I do after hours?
  • Who is the dentist, and what does the office look like?
  • Where can I read reviews that the practice did not select?
  • What do I need to fill in before the first visit, and is it clear where that information goes?

Any question that takes more than a couple of taps is a place where a new patient may give up. Fixing it is usually a matter of clearer writing and better placement, not a new website.
