Website Redesign in Siesta Key: 12 Signs Your Business Has Outgrown Its Site

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01Where a Siesta Key Website Actually Gets Read

Hardly anyone opens a Siesta Key business website at a desk. It gets opened on a phone, outdoors, in glare, by somebody with sand on their hands and one bar of signal, standing in a rental kitchen or on Ocean Boulevard deciding where the afternoon goes. That one fact should drive most of what follows, and on a lot of local sites it has driven nothing at all.

The resident base here is small and older. The Census Bureau counted 5,454 people living on Siesta Key, 53.6 percent of them 65 or over. Those residents matter, but they are not the volume. Sarasota County recorded 2,894,100 visitors in fiscal year 2024, and Visit Sarasota County's own economic impact report found that 85 percent of visitors spent time on the beach. Your buyer is a stranger on a short clock.

Demand is not the problem. Tripadvisor named Siesta Beach the number one beach in the country for 2025, fourth in the world, and the three and a half miles of quartz sand bring the searchers whether you publish anything or not. The problem is that every rental manager, charter captain, kayak outfitter, chair service and restaurant on the key is being compared inside the same handful of search results, by the same person, in the same minute.

That comparison is getting less forgiving. In March 2026, the peak month of the year, county lodging occupancy slipped to 81.5 percent while the average nightly rate hit a record $402.36, and visitation from October through March ran 4.4 percent behind the prior season. Fewer people, paying more, choosing more carefully. A site that merely exists loses to one that answers.

Here are twelve signs a Siesta Key site has stopped keeping up, grouped by the kind of failure rather than listed at random, because they tend to arrive in clusters.

02Signs One to Three: It Fails the Sand Test

The sand test is simple. Hand your phone to somebody standing in full sun on Crescent Beach, on cellular, and ask them to book. Most local sites fail it in the first ten seconds.

Sign one: it is slow on a phone that is not on Wi-Fi

Large untouched photographs, a booking widget that loads after everything else, four tracking scripts and a font that arrives late all add up to a page that is technically working and practically unusable. Visitors do not diagnose this. They go back and tap the next result, and you never see it in your analytics as anything other than a short session.

Sign two: it needs two hands and good aim

Menus that only open on hover, links spaced a few pixels apart, a date picker built for a mouse, text at a size that disappears in bright light. Someone holding a drink, a child's hand or a paddleboard is operating your site with one thumb. Buttons should be large, well separated and high contrast, and the type should be readable on a screen washed out by the sun rather than on a calibrated monitor indoors.

Sign three: the action is below the fold and buried in a menu

Call, book and check availability should be visible without scrolling, on every page, in that order of prominence for whichever one your business actually depends on. If your phone number lives only on a contact page, you have added a step to the single thing you want people to do.

03Signs Four to Six: It Cannot Take the Booking

Siesta Key runs on short decision windows. A family decides on a sunset charter at two in the afternoon, a couple picks a restaurant while they are walking to it, a guest books a week in a rental three days out because a plan fell through. Every hour a request sits unanswered is an hour a competitor can take it.

Sign four: availability lives somewhere you cannot see

If the site says call for availability, it is asking a visitor to do work on your behalf at the exact moment they were ready to commit. Real-time availability, or at minimum an honest calendar you maintain, converts far better than a form that promises a reply.

Sign five: bookings arrive as email and get reconciled by hand

This works until the week it does not, and the week it does not is usually March. Double bookings, a missed deposit, a party that turns up for a boat that already went out. When the booking system is a person checking a phone, capacity is capped by that person's attention, not by your actual inventory.

Sign six: money, weather and cancellation terms are vague

Deposits, balances, refund windows and what happens when a storm or a red tide bloom closes the day are the questions visitors ask before they enter a card number, and they are the ones most often missing. Put the policy in plain language on the booking page, not in a PDF or a confirmation email. Clarity here removes more hesitation than any amount of photography.

04Signs Seven to Nine: It Ignores What Visitors Actually Ask

Look at your own inbox and voicemail for a week. The questions that keep arriving are the ones your site should have answered, and on Siesta Key they are remarkably consistent.

Sign seven: nothing about getting there or parking

The key is reached by two bridges from mainland Sarasota, lots fill early in season, and the free Route 77 Siesta Islander trolley runs daily from 8am to 10pm between the Village and the south end. A visitor deciding whether you are worth the drive needs that information from you, not from a forum post. A short, specific paragraph about arrival is one of the highest-value pages a local business can add.

Sign eight: it is not clear where on the key you are

Siesta Key Village, Crescent Beach, the Midnight Pass Road stretch and Turtle Beach are different experiences and different drives. Sites that say Siesta Key and stop leave a stranger guessing. Name the section of the island, give a landmark, and say what parking near you is like. Someone weighing you against an option in the Village will make that decision on geography.

Sign nine: the local rules and rhythms are missing

Sea turtle nesting season runs May 1 through October 31, which shapes beach lighting and evening events. County beaches and parks are smoke free. Golf carts are legal on roads posted at 35 miles per hour or below. If you rent property, run a restaurant patio or sell an evening experience, saying so is not filler. It reads as competence, and it quietly answers the question of whether you know what you are doing.

05Signs Ten to Twelve: It Cannot Keep Up With the Season

A tourist-economy website is a living document. The moment maintaining it becomes a chore you put off, it starts working against you, and on a barrier island the drift shows fast.

Sign ten: you cannot change hours or prices yourself in under a minute

Seasonal hours, storm closures, a captain out sick, a summer schedule that differs from January. If any of that requires emailing someone who built the site three years ago, the site will be wrong for most of the year. Wrong hours are worse than no hours, because a visitor who drives to a closed door does not come back and does tell people.

Sign eleven: the proof is stale

Reviews from four seasons ago, photographs taken before the last renovation, a crew page with people who have moved on, no sign of the beach as it looks now. Visitors are actively checking whether you are still operating and still good. Recent images, current reviews pulled forward, and a genuine sense of this year all do more for conversion than a redesigned hero section.

Sign twelve: you cannot tell what the site is doing

If you are not tracking phone taps, booking starts, completed bookings and direction requests, you cannot distinguish a traffic problem from a message problem, and you will spend money on the wrong one. Most Siesta Key businesses that think they need more visitors actually need a higher completion rate on the visitors they already get. That is a measurable question, and it should be answered before anything gets redesigned.

06What to Do With All This

Not every one of these signs calls for a rebuild. If the branding still fits, the structure has room and the site loads quickly, three or four targeted fixes will usually recover more revenue than a full project would. Speed, a visible booking path and accurate hours are cheap to address and pay back inside a single season.

A redesign is the right call when the problems are structural rather than cosmetic. That usually means the site cannot take a booking properly, cannot be updated by the person who knows what changed, or is built in a way that makes adding a new service, a second location or a real availability calendar a rewrite instead of an addition. Patching around those limits costs more over two seasons than replacing them once.

Whichever path you take, sequence it the same way. Decide the single action the site exists to produce. Fix speed and the mobile path to that action first. Write the arrival, parking and policy answers your inbox has been handling. Then measure, so the next decision is based on what happens rather than what you assume.
