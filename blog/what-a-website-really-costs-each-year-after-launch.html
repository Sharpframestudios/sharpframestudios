What a Website Really Costs Each Year After Launch

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01The Bill That Arrives After Launch

Most conversations about website pricing stop on launch day. The design is approved, the site goes live, and the invoice for the build is paid. Then, somewhere between month three and month twelve, the smaller bills start arriving: a domain renewal, a platform plan, an email subscription, a license nobody remembers buying.

None of these is large on its own. Together they are the real answer to the question people type into Google, which is how much does a website cost a year. This post covers only those ongoing costs. It does not cover what it costs to design and build the site in the first place.

Every figure below comes from a named source that we read in November 2026. Prices in this area change often, so treat the numbers as a snapshot and check the current page before you budget.

02The Domain: Small, but Never Optional

Your domain name is rented by the year, and the rent does not stop. According to a GoDaddy blog article published in April 2026, domain renewal typically runs from $10 to $20 or more per year. WebsiteSetup gives a similar range, saying most standard domains cost between $10 and $25 per year to renew.

Two details are worth knowing. First, the price you paid in year one is often not the price you pay in year two. A Findstack article from September 2026 describes very cheap first-year domains as a loss leader, and says the first-year price tells you almost nothing about what the domain costs to keep. Look for the renewal price before you buy, not the promotional one.

Second, the underlying price of a .com is rising. According to the same Findstack article and a May 2026 report on webhosting.today, Verisign, the company that runs the .com registry, raised its wholesale price from $10.26 to $10.97 per year effective November 1, 2026. That is the price registrars pay, not the price you pay, but the webhosting.today report notes that retail renewal prices have historically followed wholesale increases with a lag.

The practical advice is simple. Keep the domain in an account you control, turn on automatic renewal, and keep the card on file current. Our post on where to buy and keep your business domain goes through that in detail.

03Hosting or a Platform Plan

Every website has to live somewhere, and you pay for that in one of two ways. Either you pay a hosting company and run software such as WordPress on it, or you pay a website platform a single subscription that includes the hosting.

For traditional hosting, the GoDaddy article puts shared hosting, the kind most small sites use, at $5 to $15 per month, and managed hosting, where the host handles more of the upkeep, at $20 to $150 or more per month. Introductory hosting offers behave like introductory domain offers, so ask what the plan costs when it renews.

For platforms, the subscription replaces the hosting bill. Here is what two companies’ own pricing pages showed when we read them in November 2026:

  • Squarespace listed four website plans, with the entry plan, called Basic, shown at $19 or $25 per month depending on the billing period, and the next plan, Core, at $29 or $39. The page said paying annually saves money and that an annual plan includes a custom domain free for the first year, which then renews at the standard rate.
  • Framer listed a Basic plan at $10 per month and a Pro plan at $30 per month under yearly billing, with a free custom domain included on yearly plans.

Those are starting points, not recommendations. The plan you need depends on things like online sales, the number of pages and how many people edit the site. Notice also the pattern with free domains: the first year is included, and after that the domain becomes its own line on your budget.

04Email, Security Certificates and Small Licenses

A business email address on your own domain is usually billed separately from the website, and it is billed per person. According to Microsoft’s plans and pricing page, Microsoft 365 Business Basic was listed at $7.00 per user per month, paid yearly, when we checked. Google Workspace is the other common choice, but its pricing page did not display dollar amounts in a form we could verify, so we are leaving its number out. Three mailboxes cost three times what one costs, which is why a shared address such as info@ is sometimes handled as an alias instead of a paid seat. Our comparison of Google Workspace and Microsoft 365 explains the options.

The security certificate, the thing that puts https and the padlock in front of your address, is a cost that has largely gone away. Let’s Encrypt, a nonprofit certificate authority, states on its own site that anyone who owns a domain name can obtain a trusted certificate at zero cost. Most platforms and many hosts include a certificate automatically. If a company is charging you a separate annual fee for a basic certificate, it is fair to ask why.

Licenses are the cost people forget. A WordPress site often depends on paid add-ons for forms, bookings, galleries or backups. According to WebsiteSetup, individual premium plugins usually cost between $30 and $200 per year, and a site that uses several can spend $100 to $500 per year on them. Booking tools, review widgets and stock photo subscriptions belong on the same list. Once a year, write down every subscription connected to your site and cancel the ones nothing uses.

05Maintenance and Content: The Cost of Someone’s Time

The costs so far are subscriptions. The next two are labor, and this is where budgets differ the most.

Maintenance means software updates, backups, security checks and fixing things that break. How much of it you need depends on how the site was built. A site on a hosted platform has most of the technical upkeep handled by the platform, so the work that remains is mostly content. A WordPress site needs regular updates to its core software, theme and plugins, and someone has to do them.

If you pay for that, published ranges are wide. WebsiteSetup says basic plans covering updates, security and backups start around $50 to $150 per month. A Network Solutions article updated in October 2026 puts basic plans at $15 to $100 per month and standard plans at $100 to $500 per month. The GoDaddy article puts monthly retainers at $100 to $1,500 or more. The spread reflects what is included: a plan that only clicks update is not the same service as one that includes design changes and a person who answers the phone.

Content is the other labor cost. Hours change, prices change, staff leave, a new service is added. On the Gulf Coast there is a seasonal layer on top of that: winter hours when the snowbirds return, summer hours when they leave, and a notice on the homepage when a storm closes the shop for a few days. Someone has to make those edits, whether that is you on a quiet Tuesday or a designer you pay by the hour or by the month.

Before you sign a care plan, ask three questions. What exactly is included each month? How quickly are small changes made? What happens to the site, the domain and the accounts if you stop paying?

06Adding It Up

So what does a year look like? Published totals vary because the sites vary. WebsiteSetup estimates $360 to $3,000 per year for a small business website. The GoDaddy article estimates $600 to $6,000 per year for a small to medium business site. At the low end of those ranges is an owner who does the upkeep personally and pays only for the domain, the hosting or platform plan and email. At the high end is a business paying a professional for ongoing maintenance and regular new content.

A simple way to build your own number is to list five lines and fill in each one:

  • Domain renewal, at the renewal price rather than the first-year price.
  • Hosting or platform plan, at the price it renews at.
  • Email, multiplied by the number of people who need a mailbox.
  • Licenses and add-ons, including booking and review tools.
  • Time, either yours or someone else’s, for updates and content.

A seasonal business should budget for all twelve months. The domain, the plan and the mailboxes bill in August exactly as they do in February, even if half your customers are up north.
