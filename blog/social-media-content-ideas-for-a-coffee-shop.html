Social Media Content Ideas for a Coffee Shop

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01Start With the Four Things a Coffee Shop Really Has

A coffee shop has an unusual advantage on social media: most of what makes it interesting happens in front of a counter, every few minutes, in good natural light. People, drinks, a room and a routine are four things you already have, and nearly every useful post idea comes from one of them. The trick is to stop looking for a clever concept and start pointing the phone at what is already going on.

It helps to sort your ideas into those four buckets before you plan anything: the regulars, the baristas, the drinks, and the events. If a post does not fit one of them, ask whether it belongs on your account at all. A coffee shop in Sarasota or Bradenton does not need a trending audio clip that has nothing to do with coffee. It needs a reason for someone nearby to walk in this week.

This post gives you a handful of ideas in each bucket, a few notes on what the published guidance for cafes says about frequency and format, and a simple way to keep it all manageable when you are also running a line of customers at 7:30 in the morning.

02Regulars: Let the People Who Already Love You Do the Talking

Regulars are the strongest content a small cafe has, because they are proof that real people choose you more than once. A short caption such as “Tom has ordered the same cortado every weekday since we opened” says more than any slogan, provided Tom agrees to it. Always ask first, and never post a close-up of a customer who has not said yes.

A few ways to bring regulars into your posts without making anyone uncomfortable:

  • Ask a few regulars for their usual order and post a simple card or photo of the cup with a first name and the drink, never a face unless they offer.
  • Repost a customer’s own Story or photo of your cafe, with their permission and a thank-you.
  • Run an occasional “regular of the month” feature that is really about the drink, the corner table or the story behind the habit.
  • Ask an open question in the caption, such as what people order on a rainy morning, and answer the comments.

According to a cafe Instagram guide from Zest Coffee, an Australian coffee supplier, photos of real customers enjoying your food and drinks help create a sense of fear of missing out, and reposting customers’ Stories encourages more of them. That fits what most owners notice on their own: people like to be seen in a place they are proud to go.

03Baristas: Make the Team the Face of the Shop

People return to a cafe partly because of the person behind the counter. A new face can make a shop feel unfamiliar, and a familiar one makes it feel like a neighborhood place. Posts that introduce your team do double duty: they warm up the account and they give staff a small bit of recognition.

Zest Coffee’s guide suggests featuring baristas, chefs and servers to make the cafe feel relatable and to show that you value your team. Keep it light. A photo of the morning crew with a line about who likes which drink, or a short clip of someone practicing latte art, is enough.

Ask each staff member what they are comfortable with before you post. Some people love the camera and some would rather stay behind the grinder, and either choice is fine. A hand pouring milk into a cup can be a perfectly good post, and it needs no face at all.

If you have a seasonal or part-time team that changes with the tourist months, a quick “meet the winter crew” post once the snowbirds return can be a friendly way to reset. Returning part-time residents often like to see that their favorite spot is staffed and ready, and our guide to getting your website and social ready before the season covers the wider checklist.

04Drinks: One Standout Item at a Time

Menus are long, and posts are short. Instead of describing the whole menu, pick one drink and give it a full post. The guide from Zest Coffee recommends highlighting one standout menu item rather than posting generically about the whole cafe, and that is easy to put into practice: a single iced drink in a clear cup, shot beside a window, usually looks better than a collage of everything you sell.

A few formats that work for drinks:

  • A short pour or stir clip of a signature drink, with the name and price in the caption.
  • A step-by-step Reel of how a seasonal drink is made, with each step on screen for a second or two.
  • A simple “what’s in the cup” post that lists the ingredients so people know what to order.
  • A new-item announcement that includes the date it is available, so no one arrives expecting something that is not there yet.

Perdiem, a company that makes ordering software for cafes, lists ideas such as recipe and craft videos of a signature latte and a “day in the life” clip of opening, serving and closing. It also recommends keeping tutorial Reels to about 30 seconds, which is the company’s own suggestion and not a rule from Instagram. Shorter is usually kinder to someone watching on a phone in line.

Good light does most of the work. Place the drink near a window, wipe the cup, and take the photo before the foam settles. Our post on food photography on a phone covers the basics, and nearly all of it applies to coffee.

05Events: Give People a Date to Show Up

Events turn an account from a scrapbook into a reason to visit. A cafe does not need a stage or a sound system. A Saturday morning latte art demo, a local artist hanging work on your wall, a book club that meets on Tuesday evenings, or a morning set aside for dog owners can all be an event, and each one gives you at least three posts: the announcement, a reminder, and a thank-you with photos afterward.

Hold a few events that are tied to the place you serve. Gulf Coast shops can plan around the Sarasota farmers market, a school break, or the weeks when winter residents arrive, and partner with nearby businesses such as a bookstore, a florist or a yoga studio. A partnership can put you in front of their customers, and yours in front of theirs.

Be clear and specific in every announcement: the date, the start and end time, whether it is free, and whether people need to sign up. If the event is outdoors, say what happens in the rain. During hurricane season, have a plain note ready for storm closures, and post updates as soon as you know them. People remember the shops that kept them informed.

Perdiem also describes customer challenges, such as asking followers to recreate a latte art design under a branded hashtag. If you try something like this, keep any prize small and the rules short, and check Instagram’s current promotion rules before you run it.

06How Often to Post, and Which Format to Use

The best schedule is the one you can keep on your slowest week. Zest Coffee’s guide suggests posting quality content to the grid every two to three days, sharing three or four Stories a day, and favoring selective, high-quality posts over daily posts that can feel spammy. That is the guide’s advice for cafes, not a requirement from Instagram, and a small team can start well below it. Three good posts a week beats seven rushed ones.

For Reels, Buffer’s guide to Instagram Reels length, first published in January 2025, says Reels can run up to 20 minutes when filmed in the Instagram app and notes Adam Mosseri’s comment that there is no single perfect length. Buffer says clips under 90 seconds have the highest chance of reaching new audiences. For a coffee shop, most clips need only 10 to 30 seconds.

Stories suit the unplanned moments: the morning rush, a new bag of beans arriving, the first cup of a new seasonal drink. The grid suits the photos you would be happy to have as the first impression. If you are still deciding where to spend your time, how often a business should post on social media goes through it platform by platform.

07A Simple Weekly Routine, and Where the Website Fits

Here is a rhythm that fits around a busy counter. On Monday, post a drink. On Wednesday, post a person, whether that is a barista or a regular who said yes. On Friday, post the weekend event or the weekend hours. During the week, share two or three Stories when something interesting happens. Take ten minutes on Sunday to look at what is coming, such as new items, holidays and local events, and jot down what you will shoot.

Social media is also not a replacement for the basics. Your hours, address, menu and any online ordering link should live somewhere you control, and a simple website is the natural home for them. If you have not decided whether you need one, read whether you still need a website if you have Instagram and Google. Nobody can promise that posting will lead to a certain number of visitors or a place on any search results page, so judge your efforts by the things you can see: more regulars saying they found you online, more people mentioning a post at the counter, and steady growth in the people who follow you from the neighborhood.
