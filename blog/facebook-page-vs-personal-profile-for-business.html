Facebook Page vs Personal Profile for Business

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01What a Profile Is and What a Page Is

Facebook gives you two different kinds of presence, and many small business owners start on the wrong one. A personal profile is you: your friends, your family photos, your vacation pictures. A Page is a public home for a business, a brand or an organization, and it is built to be run by a team rather than one person.

The difference shows up in small details. According to Buffer's help center, a personal profile shows a Friends tab under the main photo, while a Page does not. Buffer also says a Page shows the word Page in its intro section. If you are not sure which one you have, those two clues will tell you in a few seconds.

It is common in Sarasota, Venice and Lakewood Ranch for a one-person business to begin on a personal profile because that is the account the owner already has. A massage therapist, a boat detailer or a house cleaner posts a few jobs, neighbors comment, and it feels like it is working. The trouble tends to come later, when the business outgrows that account.

A useful test is to picture someone else taking over. If a bookkeeper, a part-time assistant or a family member had to post for the business next month, could they do it without logging in as you and seeing your private messages? On a profile the answer is no, and on a Page the answer is yes.

02Why the Page Matters for a Business

The first reason is the rules. Jon Loomer, a long-time Facebook marketing writer, quotes the Facebook Help Center as saying it is against the Facebook Terms to use your profile to represent something other than yourself, and that you could permanently lose access to your account if you do not convert it to a Page. Sprout Social makes the same point, saying that using a profile for business content is a violation that can result in deletion of the account. Both are secondhand summaries, so check Meta's current terms yourself before relying on them.

The second reason is reach and room to grow. Sprout Social says a profile is capped at 5,000 friends, while a Page has no friend limit and people follow it without sending a request. That matters less on day one and more after a good busy season, when strangers want to follow you and you would otherwise have to approve each one.

The third reason is tooling. Sprout Social says a Page gives you access to Page Insights, which reports on performance and audience, and lists boosting posts as a Page feature. It also says several people can manage a Page with their own logins. Buffer notes that it cannot connect to personal profiles at all, only to Pages and groups, so scheduling tools generally expect a Page.

There is also a plain professional reason. A customer who searches for your business and finds a personal profile with a Friends tab, a cousin's birthday post and your lake weekend photos is looking at a very different impression than a Page with your hours, services and recent work.

03What Stays Private and What Goes Public

The cleanest way to think about it is that the profile is where you live and the Page is where you work. Your personal profile can stay private to friends, and your Page is public by design. Customers do not need to be your friends to message the business, and you do not need to accept a stranger as a friend just to quote a job.

A Page also keeps your personal life out of the business account in the other direction. When you post as the Page, comments and replies appear under the business name, not under your own. Customers see a business answering, which is what most of them expect.

This also helps with the snowbird season. Seasonal customers who left in April often check Facebook from their northern homes for your summer hours or a reopening date. A public Page lets them find that update without sending a friend request and waiting for you to notice it.

Here are a few things worth keeping on the personal side:

  • Family and friends photos, which belong on a profile with the audience set to friends.
  • Personal opinions that you would not want attached to the business name.
  • Private conversations with people you know personally, who should not be mixed in with customer inquiries.

04How to Move From a Profile to a Page

Older guides describe a tool that converts a profile directly into a Page. The sources disagree on whether that option still exists. Jon Loomer describes selecting friends and followers to move to the new Page and says past usage stats do not transfer and the new Page starts with no likes or followers. Buffer's help center says a profile cannot be converted into a Page, and Locable says Facebook no longer converts a profile into a Page automatically. Because Meta changes this, look at what your own account offers before you plan around it.

If conversion is not offered, the fallback is a fresh start, which Locable lays out in steps. You create a new Page and complete its details, repost key photos and posts from the profile to the Page, announce the change, update your website links and social icons, and post a final notice on the old profile pointing people to the Page.

The website step is easy to forget. If your site, email signature or printed cards link to the old profile, change every one of them to the new Page address. Our guide to setting up a Facebook Business Page covers the name, category, hours and action button once the Page exists.

05Keeping the Two Separate Day to Day

Once the Page exists, the habit that keeps things clean is posting from the right place. Before you publish, check which name appears at the top of the post box. Many owners accidentally comment on a customer's post as themselves when they meant to answer as the business.

Access is the other piece. A Hootsuite article from March 2021 describes giving someone full control of a Page or limiting them to specific tasks, with examples such as accessing insights, creating ads, posting content and managing community activity. Meta's options may have changed since then, but the idea still applies. If you bring in a part-time helper or a studio, give them only the access the job needs, and never hand over your personal login.

A short monthly check keeps the setup healthy. Open the Page and confirm the hours, phone number and website address are current, look at who has access and remove anyone who no longer works with you, and scroll through the last few posts to make sure nothing personal slipped in. It takes about ten minutes and prevents the slow drift that leaves a business Page looking neglected.

Hurricane season is a good test of this setup. If a storm closes your shop for a few days, a Page lets you post an update as the business from a phone, and a second person with access can do it if you are without power. With a personal profile, the business has one point of failure, which is you.

None of this changes how Facebook decides who sees a post. No one can guarantee reach or a position in search, and a Page does not promise either. What it gives you is the right container, so the work you put into posts, reviews and photos belongs to the business and can be handed off when needed.
