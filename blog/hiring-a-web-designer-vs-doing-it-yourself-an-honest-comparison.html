Hiring a Web Designer vs Doing It Yourself: An Honest Comparison

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01The Question Is Not Only About Dollars

When a small business owner weighs hiring a web designer against building the site alone, the comparison usually starts and ends with price. A website builder shows a monthly fee of a few dozen dollars, and a designer shows a project quote. Set side by side, the builder looks like the obvious winner.

That comparison leaves out the most expensive ingredient, which is your own time. Every hour you spend choosing a template, writing page copy and fixing a menu that breaks on a phone is an hour not spent with customers. The honest question is which route costs less once you count the hours, the risks and the result you end up with.

This article walks through both sides fairly. Doing it yourself is the right call more often than a design studio might like to admit, and it is worth knowing when.

02What Doing It Yourself Really Costs

Start with the visible costs. According to a comparison published by Website Builder Expert in August 2026, Squarespace plans run from $19 a month on the Basic plan to $99 a month on the Advanced plan when billed annually, and higher when billed monthly. The same article notes that after the first year a custom domain starts at around $20 a year and professional email renews at $7 to $22 per user per month. Other builders such as Wix have their own tiers, and prices change often, so check the current numbers before you decide.

Those fees are real but modest. The larger cost is time. Wix, which sells a builder itself, says in its own guide that a small business site built by the owner takes roughly 20 to 40 hours spread over a few weeks, and that a project typically runs from four weeks to three months from concept to launch. Its guide lists incomplete content, late decisions about structure and several people giving feedback in separate rounds as the usual reasons projects drag on.

Here is a simple way to think about it. Suppose your time is worth $40 an hour, whether that is what you bill customers or what you would pay someone to cover your shift. Thirty hours of website work is then about $1,200 of your time, before you have paid a builder subscription or fixed anything after launch. The number is only an example, so swap in your own hourly figure and your own honest estimate of the hours.

There is also the cost of the season you build in. A Sarasota-area restaurant, contractor or salon owner who starts a site in January is competing for hours with the busiest weeks of snowbird season. A project that was supposed to take a few weeks can easily stretch into the summer, and a half-finished site helps no one.

03When Doing It Yourself Is the Right Call

A do-it-yourself site is a good fit in more situations than people expect. If any of the following describe you, a builder and a free weekend may serve you well.

  • You are testing a new idea and do not yet know whether the business will last, so a simple one-page site with your hours, services and contact details is enough.
  • You enjoy this kind of work, and you already have clear photos and a good sense of what you want to say.
  • Your customers find you mainly by referral, and the site is a place to send them for your phone number and address rather than a tool for winning new work.
  • Your needs are small enough that a template can carry them: a handful of pages, no custom features, no complicated booking or payment.

In those cases, the money you save is real and the trade-offs are acceptable. Just decide up front how many hours you will give the project, and set a date to publish. Sites that are never finished cost their owners the most.

04Where DIY Quietly Costs More

The trouble tends to show up after launch, in places that are hard to see when you are building.

Accessibility

Templates are not automatically accessible to people who use screen readers or keyboard navigation, and the owner is the one responsible for the choices made on the page, such as descriptions for images and readable text. The Independent Florida Alligator reported in February 2026 on a wave of accessibility lawsuits against small businesses in Gainesville, including restaurants and a bakery. According to that report, one bakery settled for about $6,500 plus attorney fees, and the owner said the site, which was built from Squarespace templates, had problems he had not noticed until then. Missing alternative text on images was among the issues cited. This is one local example and not a forecast for any particular business, and this article is not legal advice, but it shows why the details of a template deserve attention.

Copy and structure

Builders make it easy to arrange blocks and hard to know what those blocks should say. Many self-built sites describe the owner and skip the visitor: they list services without saying who they are for, or leave out the one thing a first-time customer needs, such as a price range, a service area or how to book. A designer, or a friend who has hired several, will usually ask those questions early.

Maintenance

A site is never really finished. Hours change, seasonal offers come and go, and after a hurricane warning you may need to post a closure notice quickly. If updating the site is a chore you dread, it will fall behind, and an out-of-date site can be worse than a simple one that is accurate.

Ownership and portability

Sites built inside a hosted builder generally live inside that builder. If you outgrow it, moving your pages to another platform can mean rebuilding much of the work. That is not a reason to avoid builders, only a reason to know what you would do if the business changes.

05What Hiring a Designer Does and Does Not Fix

Hiring a professional shifts most of the hours from you to someone who does this every week. You still have work to do, mainly supplying photos, answering questions and reviewing drafts, but the time is far smaller and the decisions are made with more experience behind them.

It does not remove every risk. A designer cannot fix a business that has no clear offer, and no one can guarantee that a new site will rank at any position on Google or bring in a set number of calls. Quality also varies widely between designers. Our guide to hiring a web designer lists questions worth asking before you sign, including who owns the finished site and what happens after launch.

On cost, project prices vary a great deal, and the scope of the work drives the number more than anything else. Our breakdown of what a professional website costs in Sarasota gives sourced market ranges, and the pricing section of our site explains how we think about scope.

06A Simple Way to Decide

Write down three numbers before you choose. First, the hours you can honestly spare over the next two months. Second, what those hours are worth to you. Third, how much of your new business you expect to come through the site rather than through word of mouth.

If the site is a business card and your hours are scarce, a simple builder page may be all you need. If customers compare you with competitors on their phones before calling, or the site needs to book, sell or reassure, the case for professional help grows stronger. Many owners land in the middle: they build a first version themselves, then bring in help once the business has proven itself.
