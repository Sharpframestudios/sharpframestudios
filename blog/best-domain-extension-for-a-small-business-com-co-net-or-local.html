Best Domain Extension for a Small Business: .com, .co, .net or Local?

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01Start With What the Extension Actually Is

The extension is the ending of your web address: the .com in sharpframestudios.com, or the .net, .co or .org you might see on other sites. Choosing one feels like a big decision, but for most small businesses the real question is narrow. Will a customer type your address correctly, and will they believe the site belongs to a real business?

Those two things, memorability and trust, are what this post compares. It does not cover how to pick the name itself, which we handle in our guide to choosing a domain name for your business. Here the name is settled and you are choosing what goes after the dot.

One caution before the details. Any claim that an extension will help you rank higher on Google is worth treating carefully, and we come back to what Google has said about it below. No one can guarantee a position in search results, whatever domain you buy.

02What Google Says About Extensions and Ranking

Many owners worry that the wrong extension will hurt them in search. According to a July 2015 Search Engine Journal report on a Google Webmaster Central answer, Google said that when it crawls, indexes and ranks URLs, it makes no difference whether a domain ends in .com or .sucks. In other words, Google said it does not favor one generic extension over another.

The same report describes one exception: country-code extensions such as .ca and .uk are used for geotargeting, meaning Google treats them as relevant to searchers in those countries. The report also says Google treats city and region extensions like .london the same as any other, so a searcher in London is not more likely to see a .london site. That statement is from 2015, so check Google's current documentation if search is central to your plans.

For a business in Sarasota or Bradenton, the practical takeaway is simple. You can choose an extension for how customers will perceive and type it, and you do not need to pick one in hopes of a search advantage. If you are already worried about visibility, our post on why a new website may not show up on Google covers the more common causes.

03How .com Compares on Trust

The most useful evidence we found is a survey run by GrowthBadger. Its write-up says 1,500 people were shown a made-up brand with a randomly chosen extension and asked to rate, on a scale of 1 to 5, how trustworthy they would expect the site to be based purely on its address.

According to GrowthBadger, .com scored highest at 3.5, followed by .co at 3.4, with .org and .us at 3.3 each and .biz at 2.9. The author notes that .com does not win by a huge amount. That is a single survey from a company with its own interests, not a law of nature, but it points the same way as common advice: .com is the safe default and the gaps between the mainstream choices are small.

The low score for .biz is worth noticing if you are tempted by a cheap or available option. Wix, which sells domains, describes .biz and .business as lighter trust signals than .com and suggests them mainly for businesses that get customers through referrals. For a business that depends on strangers finding it, that is a reason for caution.

04The Typing Problem With Everything Except .com

Trust is half the story. The other half is what happens when a customer hears your address on the radio, reads it on a truck, or is told it by a neighbor at the pool. Wix calls .com the default for small businesses because it is the extension people type automatically, so other extensions risk sending some visitors to whoever owns the .com version of your name.

That is the main trade-off with .co. It was originally the country code for Colombia, and according to Nominus it was relaunched globally in 2010 as a business extension. It is shorter and can look modern. But Wix warns that people often add an m out of habit, and says .co suits brands found through search and social media more than those spread by word of mouth.

This matters in a retiree-heavy area, where many customers read an address off a card or a mailer and type it in by hand. Seasonal residents who come back each winter may also remember only half of what they saw last year, so the simplest address is the easiest to recall.

Think about how your customers learn your name. A landscaper in Lakewood Ranch who gets most work by referral and yard signs is helped by an address nobody mistypes. A new online shop promoted through Instagram links is less exposed, because people click instead of typing. If you do choose something other than .com, Wix suggests also registering the matching .com and redirecting it, so the stray visitors still arrive.

05When .net, .org, Local or Newer Extensions Make Sense

Wix says .net still reads as technical, which makes it a sensible fallback for IT services and similar businesses, and notes that it is far easier to find an available name there than in .com. For a pool service or a restaurant, it may simply look like a second choice.

Wix says .org signals mission over profit, so it suits nonprofits, associations and clubs, and can leave visitors unsure what a for-profit business does. A Gulf Coast neighborhood association or a charity fits well. A plumber does not.

Local extensions need the most thought. Wix describes country-code extensions as showing local customers that you are one of them, but they can feel wrong when you sell across borders. For a single-city business, Wix recommends a .com with the business name, since a city name in the address stops fitting if the business grows. That is relevant here: a name with Sarasota baked in is awkward if you later serve Venice and North Port.

The newer extensions, such as .tech or .ai, tend to cost more to renew. Wix puts standard domains at roughly 10 to 50 dollars a year depending on the extension, and says the renewal price matters more than the first-year promotion. Check that number before you commit.

06A Simple Way to Decide

Start by checking whether the .com version of your exact business name is available. If it is, take it. If it is not, try adding a short, natural word before giving up on it, such as the type of service, rather than jumping to an unusual extension.

If the .com is truly unavailable, look at who will be typing the address and where they will meet it. Referral and print-heavy businesses should lean toward an address people can spell from memory. Online-first brands have more room to experiment. In every case, avoid hyphens and numbers, say the address out loud to a friend, and ask them to write it down.

Consider three typical cases. A Sarasota dentist with many retired patients who still look things up by typing is best served by a plain .com that is easy to say over the phone. A family restaurant on Siesta Key that is found mostly through maps and social posts has more flexibility, but a .com still avoids confusion on printed menus. A consultant who sells mainly to technical clients can reasonably use .net if the .com is taken.

Two practical habits protect you whichever you choose. Turn on auto-renew, because losing a domain you have advertised for years is painful, and register the domain under the business owner's own account, not a helper's. Your domain also shapes your business email, so it appears on every message you send.
