Google Business Profile Photos: Sizes and What to Upload

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01The Picture That Speaks Before You Do

Search for a plumber, a bakery or a hair salon on Google Maps and look at what you actually take in first. It is rarely the description, and often not even the star rating. It is a photo: a storefront, a plate of food, a van in a driveway, or a dim, tilted picture that a customer took four years ago.

That photo comes from the business’s Google Business Profile, the free listing that appears in Google Search and Maps. Owners usually come to the subject with one narrow question, which is the Google Business Profile cover photo size. The answer is short, and it is in section 03. The more useful questions are which photos to add and why the one you picked is not always the one Google shows.

Everything described below as Google’s own rule was read on Google’s help pages in November 2026. Platform details change, so check the help page before a big upload.

02Three Kinds of Photo, and One Surprise

Google’s help page on managing Business Profile photos and videos names three types an owner can add. The logo helps customers recognize the business. The cover photo is the one you set at the top of the profile as the picture that best represents you. Business photos are everything else: the pictures that show particular features of the place and the work.

The surprise is in the cover photo paragraph. Google says plainly that choosing a cover photo does not guarantee it will appear as the first image for your business. The same page explains that if the cover photo you select is low in quality, or if other sources suggest it is not the best picture to represent the business, a photo submitted by a user may be chosen instead.

That changes how to think about the whole job. You are supplying Google with a set of clear, honest pictures and stating a preference among them. The stronger the set, the more likely the first image a stranger sees is one you would have picked.

Two practical notes from the same page. Photos and videos show on Google only after the Business Profile is verified, so a brand-new listing may show nothing for a while. And the page says the profile highlights the logo for businesses that have their basic information filled in, such as a phone number and hours. If you are still working through that stage, our posts on profiles for businesses with no storefront and writing the description cover it.

03Sizes: What Google Publishes and What It Does Not

Google publishes one set of photo guidelines, and it applies to every photo type. According to the help page, photos look best on Google when they meet these standards:

  • Format: JPG or PNG.
  • File size: between 10 KB and 5 MB.
  • Recommended resolution: 720 pixels tall by 720 pixels wide.
  • Minimum resolution: 250 pixels tall by 250 pixels wide.
  • Quality: in focus and well lit, with no significant alterations or excessive use of filters.

Notice what is missing. The page gives no separate dimensions for the cover photo and no aspect ratio for it. The widely repeated cover photo size comes from marketing guides. Semrush, a search marketing software company, says in a guide by Cecilia Meis dated July 14, 2025 that the ideal cover photo is 1024 by 576 pixels, a 16:9 landscape shape, with a minimum of 480 by 270. The same guide suggests 1200 by 900 pixels, a 4:3 shape, for images attached to posts.

Those are reasonable working numbers, and they are not Google’s. BizIQ, a marketing agency, makes the distinction in a guide updated November 3, 2026: it describes the per-slot sizes as recommendations from the local search community rather than figures Google hands out. Semrush adds that Google may crop the cover photo depending on the browser or where the listing appears.

A sensible reading of all this: shoot the cover photo wide, in landscape, at 1024 by 576 pixels or larger, and keep the important part of the picture in the middle so that a crop to a square or a narrower strip does not cut off the sign or the front door. Keep every file under 5 MB.

Video has its own short list. Google’s guidelines ask for clips up to 30 seconds long, up to 75 MB, at a resolution of 720p or higher.

04What to Upload, Type by Type

Google has a second help page, titled Tips for business-specific photos on your Business Profile, that reads like a shot list. For most categories it suggests at least three photos. Here is how that list translates for a local business.

  • Exterior. Google says these help customers recognize the business as they approach from different directions, and suggests at least three. Take one from each direction a driver would arrive, and one from the parking lot if the entrance is around the side. In a plaza off Tamiami Trail where six storefronts look alike, this is the photo that gets people to the right door.
  • Interior. These give a feel for the ambiance and decor, and Google asks that they truthfully show what it is like to stand inside as a customer. Shoot from where a customer would stand, at eye level, with the lights on and the counters cleared.
  • Products. Google suggests at least three photos of representative or popular items. Pick what you actually sell most, not the most expensive thing in the case.
  • Photos at work. For service businesses, Google suggests at least three pictures that represent the services offered. A pool technician testing water, a roofer on a tile roof, a groomer mid-trim. A business that travels to its customers has no dining room to show, so these carry most of the weight.
  • Food and drink. Google suggests at least three, noting that they help customers plan where to eat. Our guide to restaurant food photography on a phone covers light and angles.
  • Team. Google suggests at least three photos showing management and employees, to present a more personal side of the business. For a trade where a stranger comes to the house, a clear picture of the people who will knock on the door is reassuring in a way that no logo is.

Hotels and inns get two extra categories on the same page: at least one photo of each common area, and at least three of the most popular guest rooms.

05What to Leave Out

The quality line in Google’s guidelines ends with a plain sentence: “the image should represent reality.” The tips page for business-specific photos uses nearly the same guideline and adds AI to the list of things not to use excessively, alongside filters and significant alterations.

Google’s broader advice for media on Maps, on a page titled Tips for posting media to Maps, is more specific. It says to use media you captured yourself, at the location, and to avoid screenshots, stock photos, collages and heavily edited images. It also advises against adding borders or text. Small corrections are fine: the page gives fixing rotation and improving lighting as examples of adjustments that help.

In practice that rules out several things owners are tempted to upload.

  • A promotional graphic with a phone number and a discount across it. That belongs in a post or on your website, not in the photo gallery.
  • A stock picture of a smiling family, a generic kitchen or a beach that is not yours.
  • A screenshot of a review or of your own website.
  • A rendering or an AI-generated image of a remodel that has not been built.
  • A photo so brightened and saturated that the dining room no longer looks like the dining room.

The reason goes beyond policy. Someone who drives over from Lakewood Ranch because of a photo should arrive at the place in the photo.

06After You Upload: Waiting, Customer Photos and Upkeep

New uploads are not instant. Google’s help page says it can take 24 to 48 hours for photos and videos to show on the profile. In the meantime each item carries a status. Pending means it is still uploading or processing, or the profile is not verified. Not approved means the item was flagged for violating a policy. Live means customers can see it on Search and Maps.

Customers add photos too, and their pictures sit in the same gallery as yours. You cannot curate them like an album, but Google’s help page says an owner can request the removal of customer-added photos, and it links to the steps for reporting ones that break the rules. A blurry but honest picture of your patio is not a violation. The practical answer to an unflattering customer photo is a better, more recent one of your own.

Recency matters for a simple reason: the help page says the newest media appears first on the profile. A business that changes through the year should let its photos change as well. Around Sarasota that might mean a new exterior shot when the storm shutters come down in the fall, the holiday window in December, and the patio set for the winter season when seasonal residents are back and deciding where to eat.

One caution to end on. Good photos make a profile clearer and more convincing to the person looking at it. They do not buy a position in the results, and no one can guarantee where a business appears on Google.

The same pictures are worth using twice. The exterior, the team and the work photos you take for Google are usually the ones a website needs most, so keep the full-size originals in one folder.
