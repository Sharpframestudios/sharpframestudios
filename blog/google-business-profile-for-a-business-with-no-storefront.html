Google Business Profile for a Business With No Storefront

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01What Counts as a Service-Area Business

Plenty of Gulf Coast businesses never see customers at a counter. A mobile pet groomer, a house cleaner, a pressure washing crew, a mobile notary and a lawn service all travel to the customer. Google has a name for this, and setting your profile up under that name avoids most of the problems people run into later.

Google's help page on service areas describes a service-area business as one that visits or delivers to customers directly but does not serve customers at its business address. Each service-area business can have only one profile covering its whole area. Google also describes a hybrid business, which serves customers at its address and also travels to them. According to that page, a hybrid business needs permanent on-site signage to be treated like a storefront, and without it should be listed as a service-area business.

That distinction matters for a home-based operator in Lakewood Ranch or Venice. If customers never come to your house, you are a service-area business, and the profile should say so. Google also says plainly that you should not use a business profile for a location that does not exist in the real world.

02Setting Up the Profile and Hiding Your Address

The setup itself is short. You search for your business name, choose a category, and then answer a question about whether customers visit you at a location. Google's sign-up help page walks through these steps, and the category choice deserves a few minutes because it tells Google what kind of business you are.

Google's guidelines say the business name should reflect your real-world name. Adding extra information such as service descriptions, taglines, phone numbers, URLs or hours to the name is not permitted, and categories should not be used as a way to add keywords. A name like Smith Cleaning is fine. A name stuffed with city names and services puts the profile at risk of suspension.

If you do not serve customers at your address, Google's service area page says to remove your address from the profile. That is the step that keeps a home address from appearing on a public map. It is worth doing deliberately, because a profile that shows your kitchen-table address to strangers is a privacy decision you can avoid.

The same guidelines say P.O. boxes and mailboxes at remote locations are not acceptable, and that a rented virtual office where you do not actually operate is not eligible. So the answer for a business without a storefront is not to borrow a mailing address. It is to list the profile as a service-area business with the address hidden.

03Choosing Your Service Areas

After the address question comes the service area. Google's help page says you can set up to 20 service areas, based on cities, postal codes or other areas you serve. It also says you cannot set your service area as a radius around your business, and that your overall area should not be more than about two hours of driving time from where the business is based.

For a business based in Sarasota, that might look like a short list of places you actually work: Sarasota, Siesta Key, Lakewood Ranch, Bradenton, Venice and North Port. Listing every town from Tampa to Fort Myers is tempting, but it goes against the guidance and does not describe where you would realistically show up on a Tuesday afternoon.

Two details from the same help page are easy to miss. Each service-area business can have only one profile for its whole area, so opening a separate profile for every town you serve works against the rules and invites trouble. And Google says businesses selling age-restricted products such as alcohol, cannabis or weapons need a storefront, so they cannot rely on a service area alone.

A useful rule is to list the places you would be happy to get a call from tomorrow. If a job in a given town would not be worth the drive, leave it off. You can change the list later, so start with the core of your territory and adjust as you learn where the work really comes from.

04Verification: Proving the Business Is Real

Verification is where service-area businesses most often get stuck, because there is no storefront to photograph. Google's help page on video verification explains what it wants to see, and it is more flexible than many owners expect.

According to that page, the video must be captured live, unedited, at least 30 seconds long, and recorded and uploaded from a mobile device through the profile. It asks you to show where the business operates, such as street signs or landmarks nearby, and says home-based businesses can film landmarks and signs in the immediate neighborhood. It also asks for proof the business exists, such as tools, equipment, products, business cards or branded apparel.

The third piece is proof of management. Google says you can record yourself performing a service or accessing business-only assets, such as unlocking a branded van, or show a business permit, invoice or utility bill that matches the name on the profile. The page says review can take up to five business days, and that if the video is not accepted you can record a new one that includes everything from the first plus the missing items.

The page also says the video should not show sensitive or private information or other people's faces. So before you start filming, move the mail off the counter and keep the family out of frame. Google may also offer other verification methods depending on your business, so use whichever option the profile gives you.

05Keeping the Profile Useful After Verification

A verified profile that sits untouched tends to look abandoned. The upkeep is small: confirm your hours, add real photos of your work and vehicle, list your services in plain language, and reply to reviews as they come in. During snowbird season, seasonal residents often search from out of town, so correct hours and a working phone number matter more than clever wording.

Hurricane season is a good reminder to know how to update your hours quickly. If a storm closes your operation for a few days, a one-minute update saves customers a wasted call and tells them you are still in business.

A few mistakes come up again and again with businesses that have no storefront. Some owners keep a home address visible because the setup screen never asked them to hide it. Others rent a mailbox to look more established, which Google's guidelines say is not acceptable. And some give up after one failed video, when the help page says you can simply record a new one that adds whatever was missing.

It also helps to keep a short record of what you submitted: the exact business name, the categories you chose, the service areas and the date you verified. When something changes later, such as a new phone number or a new town on your route, you will know what the profile currently says and can update it in one sitting.

The profile should also agree with your website. The business name, phone number and service list should read the same in both places, and the site should say clearly which areas you serve. What it does is give Google accurate information about your business and give customers a reliable way to call.
