Restaurant Food Photography on a Phone

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01Why the Phone Is Enough

Most restaurants do not need a photographer for everyday social posts. The phone in your pocket has a better camera than the equipment pros used a decade ago, and what separates a flat photo from an appetizing one is mostly light, angle and what is on the plate. All three are things you control without buying anything.

This post is a practical routine for a kitchen or front-of-house team with about ten spare minutes. It leans on advice from working photographers and food-photo guides, and we name the source each time. If you are still working out what to post and when, our weekly social media routine for a restaurant covers the schedule, and this post covers the picture itself.

02Start With Light, Not the Camera

According to Rebecca Hale, a studio photographer at National Geographic, the easiest light to work with when shooting food on a phone is a large, soft source: an overcast day outdoors or a window indoors. She advises turning off overhead lights and the flash, and softening harsh sun with a white sheet or translucent reflector.

The photo guides from Snappr and Sauce say much the same. Snappr recommends a spot near a large window with indirect daylight, and says overhead fluorescent lights flatten a dish and add green tones. Sauce adds that a white poster board placed opposite the window can bounce light back into the shadows, and that a phone flash tends to flatten food.

In a Sarasota dining room, that has a very practical meaning. A table near a window at 3 in the afternoon, out of direct Gulf sun, is often better than the best spot under the dining room spotlights at 7. If your sunniest windows blast the tables in the afternoon, a sheer curtain or a sheet of white foam board from a craft store does the softening for you.

Evening service is the hard part. Both Snappr and Sauce suggest a small LED panel or ring light with a diffuser for windowless or nighttime shoots, and that is a reasonable small purchase if most of your photos have to happen after dark. The other approach is to shoot the day's special during the quiet hour before dinner, while there is still daylight.

03Pick the Right Angle for the Dish

There is no single best angle, and the guides do not fully agree. Hale says shooting directly overhead gives a more lifelike result on a phone than a 45-degree angle, which she describes as harder to replicate, and notes that shooting very close can distort bowls and glasses.

Snappr and Sauce are more specific about matching angle to dish. Snappr suggests overhead for pizzas, charcuterie, grain bowls and sushi, a 45-degree angle for burgers, stacked sandwiches and pasta, and eye level for cocktails and layered desserts such as a cake slice. Sauce makes a similar split and says to try several angles and see which looks best.

A simple working rule follows from that. Flat dishes, such as a pizza or a poke bowl, are shot from directly above with the phone parallel to the table. Tall dishes, such as a burger, a parfait or a stack of pancakes, are shot from the side so the height shows. When in doubt, take one of each and pick later; the extra shots cost nothing.

04Plate for the Camera, Then Keep It Honest

A camera shows things a diner does not notice in the moment. Both Snappr and Sauce say to wipe the rim of the plate before shooting, and Snappr says a single smear reads as careless. Sauce also points out that a large plate can make a small portion look sparse, so a smaller plate may photograph better.

Snappr suggests adding height and texture by stacking or layering components, and using a garnish such as an herb, a citrus wedge or a sauce drizzle for color. It also recommends grouping garnishes in odd numbers, since three tends to look more natural than two or four.

Snappr adds one more point that matters for trust: the photographed portion and components should match what the customer actually receives. A grouper sandwich photographed with a mound of fries that never arrives on the real plate leads to a disappointed guest and sometimes a bad review. Make the photo dish the same one the kitchen sends out on a normal night.

Keep the background plain. Sauce suggests natural surfaces such as wood, slate or neutral linens that do not compete with the food, and Hale says you do not need to include everything in the frame. A hint of a fork or the edge of a glass can be enough.

05Use Your Phone's Own Controls

You do not need extra apps to improve the picture. Hale says to tap the screen to choose the focus point, and on an iPhone to hold a finger on the right side of the screen and slide it up or down to adjust exposure. She notes that lowering exposure can give a moodier look.

Snappr gives a related tip for phones: tap to focus on the food, lower the exposure slightly because phones tend to overexpose food, and turn off automatic HDR if your phone offers that setting. Check how your own model behaves, since the menus differ between iPhone and Android.

When it comes to editing, the advice from all three sources is to keep it light. Hale says a filter will not rescue a weak photo, so get a good base shot first. Sauce recommends simple tools such as Snapseed, Lightroom Mobile or the phone's built-in editor, and says heavy filters and oversaturation make food look unrealistic. Snappr suggests applying the same small adjustments to every photo, which keeps your feed looking consistent.

Leave a little space around the dish when you frame it. Instagram crops images differently in the feed and on the profile grid, and a dish pushed to the very edge of the frame can lose part of the plate. Check the preview in the app before you post.

06Build a Ten-Minute Habit

The best food photo is the one that actually gets taken, and a restaurant at full speed rarely has time for a photo session. A short, fixed routine works better than occasional big efforts. Pick one weekly slot, perhaps the quiet stretch before the dinner rush, and shoot whatever is new that week: the special, a seasonal dessert, a cocktail, or the stone crab season menu when it starts.

A routine for the slot might look like this:

  • Clean the lens with a soft cloth, since a greasy fingerprint is the most common cause of a hazy photo.
  • Carry the dish to a window table and turn off any overhead lights nearby.
  • Wipe the plate rim, tap to focus on the food, and lower the exposure a touch.
  • Take an overhead shot, a 45-degree shot and a side shot, then move on.
  • Pick the best one in the evening and do one light edit.

Over a month this builds a small library of real photos of your own food. That library does not only serve Instagram and Facebook. The same pictures can go on your Google listing and on your website, where diners often check the menu before they decide.
