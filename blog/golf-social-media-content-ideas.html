Golf Social Media Content Ideas

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01A Course Makes Its Own Material Every Day

Most businesses have to invent things to post. A golf course does not. The grounds crew is out before sunrise, the pin positions change, the tee sheet fills, a league finishes, somebody makes a hole in one. The work is choosing which of those to share and getting them out on a schedule a small staff can keep.

The audience is larger than it has been in a long time. According to a November 10, 2026 report by Davis Campbell in The Golf Wire, citing National Golf Foundation research, an estimated 30.8 million Americans played golf on a course in 2026, passing the previous record of 30.6 million set in 2003. The same report says 39.6 million people took part in off-course golf, such as driving ranges, simulators and golf entertainment venues.

This post covers golf social media content ideas in three groups, course conditions, tournaments and people, and then a weekly rhythm. The website side of the same job is covered in our post on golf course and country club websites.

02Course Conditions: The Post Golfers Check Before They Drive Over

A golfer deciding whether to play tomorrow wants to know simple things. Is the course open, are carts on the path, were the greens just aerated, did last night’s rain close the back nine. A course that answers those questions every morning gives people a reason to look at its account daily, which few businesses can say.

Golfers have asked for this for a long time. In a Golf Monthly feature dated March 17, 2017, contributing editor Fergus Bisset wrote that club members value updates on course conditions and upcoming events, and that they appreciate pictures or videos from the greens staff. He was writing about clubs in the United Kingdom, and the article is several years old, but the habit of checking before you travel is the same here.

Condition posts that are easy to produce:

  • A morning status post or Story: open or closed, cart rules, any holes out of play, and the time it was posted.
  • A short clip from the superintendent explaining a project, such as why a bunker is being rebuilt or what aeration does for the greens.
  • Advance notice of maintenance weeks, posted two or three times before the date, so nobody books a round and is surprised.
  • A photo of one hole in early light, with the hole number and yardage. This is the easiest good photograph on the property.
  • After a storm, an honest update: what is closed, what is being cleaned up, and when you expect to post again.

Two cautions apply. First, a condition post goes stale fast, so put the date and time in the text and use Stories for anything that will be wrong by evening. Our post on Instagram Stories as a daily habit explains why that format suits short-lived news. Second, the same status should appear on the website. A PGA of Canada article by Brendan Stasiewich about The Pulpit Club in Ontario describes that club keeping practical details such as hours and cart rules on its website and saving its main feed for images. That is a sensible division: the feed shows the course, and Stories and the website carry the daily facts.

Be honest when conditions are poor. A photo of perfect greens during a week when the greens are sanded and slow will cost more goodwill than it earns. Golfers forgive maintenance. They do not forgive being misled about it.

03Tournaments: Before, During and After

A tournament is three kinds of content, and most courses post only one of them, the flyer. Lightspeed, a company that sells software to golf courses, wrote in a guide dated February 18, 2019 that promotional flyers generally do not do well on social media and that courses should avoid flooding their feeds with them. That is a vendor’s opinion, not a study, but it matches what most people do when they see a flyer in a feed, which is keep scrolling.

Spread the event across its whole life instead.

  • Before: the date, the format, the entry deadline and how to sign up, posted more than once. Add a photo from last year so people can picture it.
  • The day before: the course set up for the event, such as tee markers, the scoring table, or the hole chosen for closest to the pin.
  • During: a few short clips or Stories. The first group teeing off, the lunch line, a long putt on the last green.
  • After: the results, the winners with their permission, and a thank-you to sponsors and volunteers by name.

Weekly leagues and regular games are quieter than a big outing but more useful over a year. A standing Thursday post with the league results takes ten minutes, and the players named in it tend to show it to someone. Bisset’s Golf Monthly feature lists competition details, including entries and results, among the things members want from a club’s accounts.

04Members, Staff and the Question of Permission

People are the most engaging subject a club has and the one that needs the most thought. At a public course, a photo of a foursome on the first tee is usually welcome if you ask first. At a private club, some members chose the club partly because it is private, and the board may already have a policy on photographs. Read it before you post anyone.

A simple practice works at either kind of facility: ask each time, take no for an answer without discussion, and never post children without a parent’s clear consent. Rosters, guest names and anything from a private event stay off public accounts.

With that settled, there is plenty to share.

  • A hole in one, with the hole, the club used and the witness. Golfers love these and the player almost always agrees.
  • A staff introduction: the head professional, the superintendent, the person who has run the starter’s booth for years.
  • A thirty-second tip from the teaching professional, filmed on the range. One idea per clip is enough.
  • Junior clinics and beginner classes, showing the setting and the instructor more than the faces of the students.
  • The kitchen: one dish, the hours, and whether the dining room is open to the public.
  • The small things. The PGA of Canada article quotes Gabi Best, communications manager at The Pulpit Club, saying the club focuses on details people might not otherwise see, and it mentions a clip of turkeys crossing a fairway. On this coast it is more likely to be an osprey, a sandhill crane or an alligator sunning by a pond.

05Which Platform, and Who Is Watching

Published research on golfers and social media is thin and mostly old, so treat this section as a starting point. A National Golf Foundation article titled Golf’s Social Circle, which carries no date on the page and refers to events from 2018, reported that the share of golfers who prefer social media as their main point of contact with a course or golf business had risen from 9 percent to 21 percent over seven years. Among golfers aged 18 to 34 it put the figure at 45 percent. The same article said Facebook was the most popular social network among golfers of all age groups.

Those numbers are years out of date and the article does not describe the survey behind them, so do not plan a budget around them. The direction is still worth noting: younger golfers were already more likely to look at a course’s social accounts than to call. A month of asking at the pro shop counter how people heard about an event will tell you more about your own players.

For a course in this area, where many regular players are retired or seasonal, a reasonable split is Facebook for news, events and results, and Instagram for the photographs and short videos. You do not need every platform. Bisset’s feature makes the same point about Twitter, saying there is no use opening an account only because the club down the road has one.

06A Weekly Rhythm for the Season

Winter is when a Gulf Coast course is busiest and when the staff has the least time to think about posts. A fixed weekly pattern removes the thinking. This one is our suggestion, and you should adjust it to your own calendar.

  • Every morning: a conditions Story, posted by whoever opens the shop.
  • Monday: the week ahead. Events, maintenance and any times the course is closed for an outing.
  • Wednesday: a person. A staff member, a tip from the professional, or a hole in one from the past week.
  • Friday: the course itself. One good photograph or a short video of a hole.
  • After each event: results and thanks within a day, while the players still care.

Give the job to one person and a backup. Decide who posts when a storm closes the course, because that is the day people will look and the day the usual person may not be at work. In summer, when the calendar is lighter, the same rhythm can drop to two or three posts a week without anyone noticing.

Keep special offers to a small share of what you post. A twilight rate or a summer card is fair news, but an account that is mostly discounts teaches followers to wait for the next one. Bisset’s feature cautions clubs against posting offers too often for the same reason.

Finally, remember where the posts lead. A golfer who likes what they see will tap through to book, and the booking link, the rates and the day’s status need to be correct when they arrive.
