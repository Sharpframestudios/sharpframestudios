Med Spa Website Design: What to Show and What Not to Claim

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01What a Med Spa Website Has to Do

A med spa website is asked to do two jobs that pull in opposite directions. It has to feel calm and appealing enough that a nervous first-time visitor books a consultation, and it has to be careful enough that nothing on the page promises more than the practice can deliver. Most of the mistakes we see in this category come from pushing too hard on the first job.

The practical answer is to design for trust before persuasion. Show who runs the practice, what is offered, what a visit looks like and what it costs to start, and let restraint in the claims do some of the selling. In a market like Sarasota and Lakewood Ranch, where many clients are comparing several practices before they call, a plain and honest page often reads as more credible than a loud one.

One note before we go further. This post is general information from a web design studio, not legal advice. Rules for medical advertising depend on your license type, your services and your products, so have a Florida healthcare attorney or your compliance advisor review the final wording.

02Put the People and Credentials First

In most medical settings, the visitor wants to know who will actually treat them. Give each provider a short profile with a clear photo, their license type, and the specific services they perform. If a physician serves as medical director, say so plainly and describe the role accurately.

Florida's advertising rule for physicians, Rule 64B8-11.001 of the Florida Administrative Code, speaks directly to this. As summarized from the rule text, it treats an ad as misleading if it fails to conspicuously identify the physician by name, and it requires any licensee named in an ad to identify as an M.D., P.A. or A.A. as applicable. It also says that claiming a specialty requires recognition from a board the Florida Board of Medicine approves, and that citing a certification without naming the certifying board is deemed misleading.

That suggests a few habits for the provider pages:

  • Use full names with the correct license designation next to each one.
  • Name the certifying board for any certification you mention, and leave the claim off if you cannot document it.
  • Describe what each person does at the practice. Do not imply a physician performs treatments that someone else performs.
  • Keep the page current when staff change, because an outdated roster is its own kind of misleading.

Our guide to writing an About page for a local business covers the structure for these pages, and the same who, why and proof pattern works well for a team page.

03Before-and-After Photos: Consent and Context

Before-and-after photos are the most persuasive content a med spa has, and the most delicate. Two separate questions apply: whether you have the right to publish the image, and whether the image gives an accurate picture of what to expect.

On the first question, Michael Cohen of Cohen Healthcare Law Group wrote in 2024 that posting a patient photo likely violates HIPAA unless the practice has the patient's consent, since a patient can be identified from a photo even without a name. He points to the authorization rules in section 164.508 of the HIPAA Privacy Rule, which call for a description of what will be disclosed and why, the right to revoke, and an expiration date. He also notes that patients should understand a post can be widely shared, and that revoking later may not undo a photo that has already spread.

His practical suggestions are sensible for a website too. Do not collect consent only at the moment of treatment, when a patient is focused on the procedure, and let the patient see the exact images before they go live. Keep a signed record of the specific photos each patient approved, and remove them if the patient changes their mind.

On the second question, the Federal Trade Commission's Health Products Compliance Guidance says before-and-after images can convey an implied claim of dramatic improvement even without a written claim. The same guidance says testimonials showing results better than typical are likely deceptive, and that a Results not typical disclaimer does not fix this. The FTC's recommended approach is a clear and conspicuous statement of the results a typical person can expect.

04Choosing and Presenting the Photos

Taking those two ideas together, the safest approach is to show photos that are representative, not the best result of the year. If you can describe the typical outcome for a treatment, say so near the image instead of relying on small print.

Consistency matters as well. Use the same lighting, angle, distance and background for the before and after pair, and avoid heavy retouching or filters on either one. A photo pair that looks better because of the lighting, not the treatment, can mislead even if no word on the page is false.

On the site itself, a clean gallery works better than a scrolling wall of faces. Group images by treatment, add a short caption with the treatment type and number of sessions where you have that information, and add a line explaining that results vary by person. The caption is useful to visitors and also documents what the image shows, which helps if anyone later questions it.

If your practice is not yet comfortable collecting consent, it is better to leave galleries off than to publish them half-prepared. A strong treatment page with a plain description of what happens, who performs it and what recovery looks like can do a lot of the work on its own.

05Results Language to Avoid

The wording around the images deserves the same care. Under Rule 64B8-11.001, as summarized from the rule text, an advertisement is deemed misleading if it misrepresents facts, discloses only part of the relevant facts, or creates false or unjustified expectations of beneficial help. It also treats suggestions that a physician is superior to other physicians as misleading.

Applied to a website, that points away from phrases like guaranteed results, permanent, the best injector in Sarasota, or no downtime. Those lines are easy to write and hard to support. Plainer wording tends to be safer: describe what the treatment is, what it is commonly used for, what the usual process looks like, and that outcomes and recovery differ from person to person.

The FTC's guidance makes a related point about evidence. It says advertisers need a reasonable basis for their claims before running an ad, and that health benefit claims generally call for randomized, controlled human clinical testing. It also says consumer testimonials are never sufficient to substantiate claims about the effects of a health product. For a med spa, that means a glowing review is not proof that a treatment does what a headline says, so keep treatment claims modest and tied to what you can document.

Pricing and offers need care too. Florida Statute 456.062 covers advertising of free, discounted or reduced-fee services by licensed health care practitioners, and as summarized, it requires a capitalized notice that the patient and anyone responsible for payment has a right to refuse to pay, or be reimbursed, for other services performed because of the ad within 72 hours. If your homepage promotes a first-visit discount or a free consultation, ask your attorney exactly how that notice should appear.

06Page Structure, Reviews and Next Steps

With the claims question settled, the layout can stay simple. A homepage that says what the practice offers and where it is, a page for each treatment, a provider page, a gallery if consent allows, and a clear way to book or ask a question covers most of what visitors need. Our section-by-section homepage guide walks through the order that tends to work.

Reviews need the same honesty. The FTC says on its endorsement guidance page that you cannot condition an incentive, such as a discount, on a review being positive, and that endorsers with a connection to the business that viewers would not expect should disclose it clearly. Show real reviews as they are written, and do not write or buy any.

Practical details matter in Florida. Put the address, hours and phone number in plain text so they are easy to find, make the booking button easy to reach on a phone, and think about seasonal visitors. During snowbird season, many clients may be new to the area and comparing options from another state, so a page that explains how a first visit works helps them decide.
