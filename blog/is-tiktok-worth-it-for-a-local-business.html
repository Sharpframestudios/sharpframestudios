Is TikTok Worth It for a Local Business?

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01A Fair Question With a Conditional Answer

Owners tend to ask it bluntly: is TikTok for business good, or is it a place where teenagers dance? The honest answer is that it depends on three things you can check in an afternoon. Who your customers are, whether what you do is interesting to watch, and whether someone on your team can make a short video every week without resenting it.

If you are still deciding between platforms in general, start with our guide to the best social media platforms for a local small business. This post assumes TikTok is already on your shortlist and you want to know whether it deserves the hours.

02Check the Audience Before Anything Else

The best public numbers come from the Pew Research Center. In a summary published on March 2, 2026, Pew researchers Colleen McClain and Kirsten Eddy reported that 37% of U.S. adults say they use TikTok, up from 21% in 2021. The share drops steadily with age: 63% of adults under 30, 44% of those 30 to 49, 30% of those 50 to 64 and 12% of those 65 and older.

Read those figures against your own customer list. A smoothie bar near a college, a tattoo studio or a boutique that sells to women in their twenties is looking at a platform most of its customers use. A hearing clinic in Venice or a financial planner whose clients are mostly retired is looking at roughly one customer in eight, going by the Pew figure for adults 65 and older.

Two other findings in the same Pew summary are useful for a business. Citing a 2024 study, it says 95% of adult TikTok users name entertainment as a reason they use it, and 62% say looking at product reviews or recommendations is a reason. So people do use TikTok to decide what to buy, but they arrive expecting to be entertained, and a video that feels like an advertisement is working against the room.

The second finding is about who posts. According to Pew, about half of adult users, 52%, have ever posted a video, and a 2023 Pew study found that the most active quarter of users produced 98% of the publicly accessible videos. Most people on TikTok watch. That is good news if you are worried your customers are not the posting type, because they do not need to be.

Beyond age, ask whether your work is watchable. Some trades are naturally visual:

  • Food and drink being made, plated or poured.
  • Transformations with a clear before and after, such as pressure washing, detailing, landscaping, hair or a remodeled bathroom.
  • Places people want to visit, such as a charter boat, a beach shop or a venue.

03What Changed With TikTok in 2026

For several years the main argument against investing in TikTok was that it might be banned in the United States. That question is quieter now. According to a TechCrunch report by Lauren Forristal and Amanda Silberling dated January 23, 2026, TikTok announced on January 22 that a new entity, TikTok USDS Joint Venture LLC, had been established to run the U.S. business. TechCrunch reported that Oracle, Silver Lake and MGX each hold 15%, and that ByteDance, the Chinese parent company, keeps less than 20%.

For a business owner the practical points are smaller. The same report says users will not need to download a new app. It also says it was not yet clear how the videos people are shown would be affected. In plain terms, the legal risk is lower than it was, but the way TikTok chooses what to show can still change without notice, as it can on every platform.

The second change matters more to a local business. On February 11, 2026, MacRumors reported, in an article by Juli Clover, that TikTok in the U.S. was adding a Local Feed, available from a tab on the home screen. According to that report, the feed covers travel, events, restaurants and shopping, along with posts from nearby small businesses and local creators, and posts are chosen based on location, the topic of the content and when it was posted.

There are two limits to keep in mind. MacRumors reported that the feature is opt-in and that precise location is off by default, so only people who switch it on will see the feed. And Metricool, a social media software company, wrote in a guide dated March 31, 2026 that only public accounts are eligible to appear there. Neither source says how many people have turned the feature on, so treat the Local Feed as a reason to tag your location on every video, not as a promise of local viewers.

04The Price Is Paid in Hours

Posting on TikTok costs nothing in dollars. The cost is the time of whoever makes the videos, and for a small business that is usually the owner or the one employee who is comfortable on camera.

A single short video involves more steps than it appears to. Someone has to think of the idea, film it, usually more than once, trim it, add captions, write the description and then answer comments afterward. We have not found a reliable published figure for how long this takes a small business, so we are not going to quote one. Time your first three videos from idea to posted and you will have your own number, which is more useful than anyone else’s average.

A few choices keep the hours down:

  • Film in batches. One quiet hour on a Tuesday can produce a month of clips.
  • Reuse the work. A vertical video made for TikTok can also be posted as an Instagram Reel, and our post on Reels ideas for owners who are just starting lists formats that work on both.
  • Give one person the job. A shared responsibility for video usually means no video.

There is also a setup choice. TikTok offers business accounts and personal accounts, and they differ in ways that matter. According to a Sprout Social guide by Chloe West, first published on August 9, 2022, a business account can add a website link to its profile immediately, while a personal account has to reach 1,000 followers first. The same guide says business accounts are limited to a library of commercially licensed music, which it put at more than one million tracks, instead of the full catalog of popular songs. That guide is several years old, so confirm both points in the app before you choose. For most local businesses the website link is worth more than a trending song.

05Run an Eight-Week Test

You do not have to decide in theory. Eight weeks at one video a week is our suggestion, not a researched threshold.

Set it up this way:

  • Open a business account, add your website link, and write a one-line description that says what you do and where, such as “Custom cakes in Bradenton.”
  • Keep the account public and tag your location on every video, so the Local Feed has something to work with.
  • Post one video a week for eight weeks. Try at least three different formats, such as a process clip, a before and after, and an answer to a common question.
  • Say the name of your town out loud or put it in the caption. A viewer in Ohio is pleasant but will not book a table on Main Street.
  • Write down the minutes each video took.

Then decide what you are measuring, and be strict about it. Views are the easiest number to see and the least useful one for a local business, because a video can be watched widely by people who will never be near your door. Count these instead:

  • Visits to your profile and taps on your website link, both shown in the app’s analytics.
  • Comments and messages from people who are clearly local, such as someone asking about hours, prices or whether you serve their neighborhood.
  • Customers who mention TikTok when you ask how they found you. Add that question to your booking form and at the register for the length of the test.

Timing matters on this coast. A restaurant, a tour operator or a shop that depends on seasonal visitors will learn more from a test that runs from January through March, when visitors are here and looking for things to do, than from one that runs in August.

06How to Read the Result, and When to Skip It

At the end of eight weeks, set the hours beside the outcomes. If a handful of real inquiries came in and the videos took an hour or two a week, keep going and consider posting more often. If the videos were watched but nobody local got in touch, try one more month with different formats before you call it. If making them was a weekly struggle, stop.

It is also reasonable to skip the test entirely. TikTok is probably not worth your time right now if any of these apply:

  • Most of your customers are over 65 and find you through referrals, a search or a sign.
  • Your work is confidential or hard to film, as with legal, medical or financial services.
  • You are already behind on the basics: an accurate Google Business Profile, a website that loads quickly on a phone, and a Facebook or Instagram page that is kept current.
  • Nobody on the team wants to be on camera or behind it.

Whatever you decide, remember where the customer ends up. A good video creates curiosity, and curious people look you up. If the link in your profile leads to a slow page with no hours, no prices and no clear way to book, the video did its job and the visit was wasted.
