Law Firm Website Design and the Florida Bar Advertising Rules

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01Start With What the Rules Cover

A law firm website is the one piece of marketing a lawyer fully controls, and in Florida it is also treated as advertising. That means the design choices on the page, such as what the homepage headline says, whether reviews appear and how a case result is described, are not only matters of taste. They fall under the Rules Regulating The Florida Bar.

The Florida Bar publishes a quick reference checklist for lawyer websites, social media pages and video channels, which it says was revised August 21, 2023. According to that checklist, websites do not have to be filed with the Bar for review, but they must still follow Rules 4-7.12 through 4-7.17 and 4-7.21. The checklist also warns that answering no to every question on the list does not mean an advertisement complies.

This post is a plain-language guide for lawyers and the people who build their sites. We are a design studio, not a law firm, and nothing here is legal advice. Rules get amended, so check the current text and, where it matters, ask the Bar’s ethics staff or an ethics lawyer before publishing.

02The Basics Every Page Needs

Rule 4-7.12 sets out the required information. According to the Bar’s checklist, an advertisement must name at least one lawyer, law firm or qualifying provider responsible for it, and it must state the city, town or county of at least one bona fide office. If a matter will be referred to another lawyer or firm, the ad has to say so. Disclosures must be legible, audible and conspicuous.

For a website, this is a layout decision as much as a legal one. A firm name and an office location tucked into tiny gray text in the footer may meet the letter of the rule on some pages and still fail the “conspicuous” test. A simple, readable line in the header or footer of every page, with the firm name and the town of the office, is easy to design and easy to defend.

The checklist also covers firm names. Under Rule 4-7.21(c), a fictitious or trade name has to appear on all firm advertising, letterhead, business cards, office signs, pleadings and other firm documents. If your firm trades under a name that differs from the lawyers’ names, make sure the website uses it consistently.

03What You Cannot Say: Results, Rankings and Guarantees

Rule 4-7.13 prohibits advertising that is deceptive or inherently misleading. The text of the rule, as reproduced on floridajustice.com, says an ad is deceptive if it contains an inaccurate material statement, leaves out information needed to avoid misleading the reader, or implies a material fact that does not exist.

The same rule lists specific examples. According to that text and the Bar’s checklist, these include:

  • Statements that a prospective client could reasonably read as predicting or guaranteeing success or specific results.
  • References to past results that are not objectively verifiable, or that mislead in context, such as an atypical result or one that leaves out that a judgment was uncontested or by default.
  • Comparisons or descriptions of a lawyer’s skills, experience, reputation or record that cannot be objectively verified. The floridajustice.com text gives “the best” as an example of a claim that generally fails.
  • Advertising for practice areas the lawyer does not practice or does not intend to practice.

That last point matters for site structure. A page for every practice area looks thorough, but if the firm does not take those cases, the page is a risk and not an asset. Build pages only for the work the firm actually does.

Past results deserve extra care. The same text says advertising past results requires the affected client’s informed consent under Rule 4-1.6(a), even if the information is public record. A “Recent Results” section with dollar amounts is a common design pattern on law firm sites, and it is exactly where a firm should slow down, get the consent in writing and describe each matter fully.

There is also a plain point about search. Nothing on a website can guarantee a position on Google, and a law firm that promises one has the same problem the Bar’s rule describes with promised case outcomes.

04Titles, Specialties and Awards

Words like “specialist” and “expert” are tightly controlled. According to the Bar’s checklist, citing Rule 4-7.14, only a board-certified lawyer may claim certification, and only in the certified area. The ad must name the certifying organization and the area, and a law firm itself cannot claim certification. Claims of specialization or expertise must be objectively verifiable, and if not every lawyer at the firm qualifies, the ad needs a clear disclaimer saying so.

The same section of the checklist covers memberships and recognitions. They must come from bona fide organizations that use objective, uniformly applied selection criteria. A badge from a directory that sells placement is a risk, and a design that puts a row of badges above the fold only makes the risk more visible.

Former public officials are another trap. The checklist says former judges, executive officials and legislators cannot be named with their official titles in the way a sitting official would be. The rule text on floridajustice.com says a title must come after the name and be clearly marked as former or retired. A bio headline reading “Judge Smith” for a former judge is the kind of thing a designer might set in large type without knowing the problem.

Fee language has its own rule. The checklist says that when an ad mentions fees, including “no fee unless we win,” it must disclose whether the client is responsible for costs or expenses, and that disclosure must be prominent. If your hero section says “No fee unless you win” in large type, the cost statement cannot be buried in a footer.

05Testimonials, Photos and Tone

Client reviews are often the strongest content on a service business site, and for lawyers they carry conditions. According to the Rule 4-7.13 text on floridajustice.com, a testimonial is prohibited if it addresses matters the person is unqualified to evaluate, is not the person’s actual experience, does not represent what the firm’s clients generally experience, was written or drafted by the lawyer, was given in exchange for something of value, or lacks a disclaimer that the prospective client may not get the same or similar results.

In practice, that means a firm cannot write a testimonial for a client to sign, cannot offer a gift card for a five-star review, and should place the results disclaimer next to each testimonial. A design that shows a rotating carousel of quotes should have the disclaimer within view of every quote, not on a separate legal page.

Imagery and tone fall under Rule 4-7.15. According to the checklist, advertisements may not use images, sounds, videos or dramatizations that appeal to emotion instead of a rational evaluation of the lawyer’s suitability. Judges, law enforcement officers and actors portraying them cannot act as spokespersons, celebrity voices or images are not allowed, and ads cannot offer economic incentives such as giveaways to hire the lawyer or review the ad.

Stock photography sits in a gray zone. Under Rule 4-7.13, as summarized by the Bar, people who appear to be firm members or employees but are not need a clear disclaimer, and so do actors portraying professionals unless a reasonable viewer would know the portrayal is fictitious. A photo of a smiling “attorney” on the About page that is really a stock model is worth avoiding entirely. Real photos of the real team are safer.

The same rule says an ad cannot imply Bar approval. Stating that a lawyer is licensed in Florida is allowed. Adding a seal or wording that suggests the Bar has reviewed or endorsed the site is not.

06Filing, Review and Ongoing Care

The Bar’s filing page says lawyer and law firm websites are exempt from the filing requirement under Rule 4-7.20(g), including pop-ups on the firm’s own site. Internet banner and pop-up ads that appear elsewhere still require filing unless their content is limited to presumptively valid information. The page also says exemption from filing does not exempt a website from the other advertising rules.

The checklist notes that specific items can be submitted voluntarily under Rule 4-7.19(d) for a $250 fee, but the Bar will not accept an entire website for review. Check the Bar’s current fee and timing before relying on those numbers, since the filing page was updated in July 2026 and mentions new online submission through the member portal.

A website is never finished, and the rules are one reason. A short routine helps: review the whole site once or twice a year, check every claim, badge and result against the current rules, and remove anything the firm cannot document. 07Working With a Designer on a Law Firm Site

The best way to avoid problems is to separate who decides what. The attorney, or the firm’s ethics counsel, owns every claim on the site. The designer owns layout, clarity and speed. When a designer proposes a headline, an award row or a reviews slider, the lawyer should be able to say yes or no based on the rule, not on how it looks.

It helps to give the designer the facts up front: the exact firm name, the offices that are real, the practice areas the firm takes, the board certifications that exist, and the results the firm has client consent to share.
