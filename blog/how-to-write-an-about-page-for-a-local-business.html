How to Write an About Page for a Local Business

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01Why the About Page Gets Read

The about page is usually the one written last and in a hurry, which is a missed chance. A person who is about to call a plumber, book a dentist or hire a photographer wants to know who is on the other end before they commit.

Google's own guidance on helpful content points the same way. It asks whether it is obvious who is behind the content on a site and says an About page or author page can help readers judge whether a source is trustworthy. Google also treats invented creator profiles, such as made-up names or false credentials, as a sign of a low-quality page. So the honest version of your story is also the safer one.

None of this means an about page will move a site up in search results. No one can guarantee a position on Google, and an about page is written for the person reading it, not for a ranking. Think of it as the page that answers one question: should I trust these people with my money?

02The Four Questions: Who, Why, Where, Proof

A useful about page answers four questions in roughly this order. Who are you and what do you do for customers? Why do you do this work? Where are you and who do you serve? What proof is there that you do it well? Each answer can be a short paragraph, and together they make a page a visitor can read in about two minutes.

Vistaprint's guide to about pages suggests a similar shape: an introduction, your story, and next steps such as contact details or hours. It also advises leading with who you are, what you do and how you solve a problem near the top, and keeping the page short, since reading time matters more than word count. That fits the order above and is easy to follow without any writing experience.

The most common mistake is to start with the company's history and bury the part the visitor came for. A reader who lands on the page wants to see, in the first lines, what you do and for whom. The history can follow once they are interested.

03Opening Lines That Work

The first sentence sets the tone. Good ones are plain, specific and written in the first person. Here are a few invented examples to show the pattern, not text to copy.

  • We are a two-person air conditioning company in Bradenton, and we answer our own phones.
  • I have cut hair on Main Street in Sarasota for eleven years, and most of my clients still come from referrals.
  • We opened this bakery in Venice because we could not find a decent loaf of rye within thirty miles.
  • Our family has painted homes in Lakewood Ranch since the neighborhood had its first model home.

Notice that none of these tries to impress. They sound like a person talking, which is exactly what a nervous first-time customer hopes to find. A reader scanning on a phone in a parking lot should know within five seconds that a real business with a real address stands behind the page.

Each line names a place, says what the business does and hints at why a person might care. Weak openings, by contrast, tend to read like a slogan: a passion for excellence, a commitment to quality, a dedication to service. Those phrases could describe any company, which means they tell the reader nothing.

If you are not sure how to start, try this. Imagine a new customer asks you across the counter, so what is your business about? Write down what you would say out loud, then tidy it up. Vistaprint suggests a similar trick: record a conversation about your business and listen back for the points that come up most.

04The Story: Why You Do This Work

The middle of the page is where your story goes, and it does not need to be dramatic. A few honest sentences about how you got started, what you learned and what you care about are enough. A retired teacher who now runs a tutoring service, a former restaurant cook who opened a catering company, a family that has run a boat repair shop for three generations: each of those is a real reason for a customer to feel at ease.

Keep the story connected to the customer. It is fine to say you grew up fishing the Gulf, but the useful next sentence explains what that means for the charters you run today. Otherwise the story is about you and not about the person reading it.

Include one detail that only you could write. It might be the first job you ever did, the oddest request a customer made, or the reason you chose your service area. Specific details are hard to fake, and they are what readers remember afterward.

Where it applies, mention the local part. Many Sarasota-area businesses serve a mix of year-round residents and seasonal ones, and a line about how you handle that, such as extended hours in season or a plan for hurricane-season closures, tells a visitor you understand the place. Mention it only if it is true for your business.

05Proof, People and Next Steps

Proof is what turns a pleasant page into a persuasive one. Use things that are real and checkable: years in business, licenses and insurance, certifications, professional associations, awards you actually won, and customer comments you have permission to use. Contractors in particular benefit from showing license information openly, which our post on contractor website trust signals covers in more detail.

Show the people. A clear photo of the owner or the team, with names and a line about each person's role, is more convincing than any paragraph. If you are the only person in the business, say so plainly and with some pride. Customers of small businesses often choose them precisely because they will deal with the owner. Use a real photo, not a stock image, and never invent staff or credentials.

End with a next step. Add your phone number, address, hours and a way to ask for a quote, because a page that makes the reader hunt for contact details wastes the trust it just built. A short line such as call us or send a message is enough. The about page also works best as part of a complete homepage structure, which we cover in what should be on a small business homepage.

06Common Mistakes and a Simple Checklist

A few things come up again and again on small business about pages. The page talks only about the owner and never says what the customer gets. It is a wall of text with no photo and no headings. It claims to be the best, the leading or the most trusted without any evidence. It was written five years ago and still lists staff who have left. Or it simply does not exist, and the menu link goes to a contact form.

Before you publish, read the page once as a stranger and check these points:

  • The first two lines say what you do, for whom and where.
  • The story is short, true and tied to a benefit for the customer.
  • There is at least one piece of real proof, such as years in business, a license or a customer comment.
  • There is a real photo of the owner or team.
  • Phone, address, hours and a contact option are on the page.
