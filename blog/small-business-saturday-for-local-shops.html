Small Business Saturday for Local Shops

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01Eleven Days Out

Small Business Saturday falls on Saturday, November 28, 2026, according to the U.S. Small Business Administration’s page for the day. That is the Saturday after Thanksgiving, wedged between Black Friday and Cyber Monday, two days built around large retailers and large discounts.

The day has a clear origin. The SBA’s page says it was founded by American Express in 2010 and has been officially cosponsored by the SBA since 2011. So it is a marketing campaign that became a habit, and a fairly durable one.

For a shop on Main Street in Sarasota, a boutique on Venice Avenue or a bakery in Lakewood Ranch, the timing is kind. The day lands just as seasonal residents have settled back in and holiday visitors are in town with a free afternoon. This post is a short plan for the days in between: what to fix on the website, what to set on Google and what to post, without turning the week into a production.

02What the Day Is, and How Big It Really Is

There is nothing to sign up for and no fee. A shop takes part by being open, saying so, and giving people a reason to come by. The SBA’s page points owners to free Small Business Saturday marketing materials, including sample posters and social media resources, which are hosted on the American Express website.

One detail is worth knowing before you count on it. American Express also runs a Shop Small Map, and its Shop Small pages say that only qualifying merchants that accept American Express cards are featured there. The posters and social graphics are a separate thing, so read the terms on the download page before assuming which parts apply to you.

The numbers attached to the day deserve a careful reading. In a news release dated November 25, 2025, the SBA put estimated spending at local merchants on Small Business Saturday 2024 at around $22 billion. Its footnote credits the American Express 2024 Small Business Saturday Consumer Insights Survey, which means the figure is what shoppers said they spent in a survey, and it is a national total. It says nothing about what one store in Bradenton should expect.

A more useful pair of numbers comes from the CNBC and SurveyMonkey 2025 Small Business Saturday Survey, published November 27, 2025. It found that 23 percent of Americans planned to shop on the day. Among those who did not plan to, 41 percent cited a lack of awareness of local businesses.

That second figure is the whole case for the work below. A good share of the people who stay home are not against shopping locally. They simply do not know what is nearby, whether it is open, or what they would find there. Those are questions a website, a Google listing and a few posts can answer.

03The Website: Answer the Saturday Questions

Nobody needs a new website for one Saturday. What helps is making sure the site answers the handful of questions a person asks before getting in the car. Most of them can be handled with one short block near the top of the homepage, kept there from now until the end of the weekend.

  • Hours for the whole weekend. Write them out for Thanksgiving Day, Friday, Saturday and Sunday, even if some of those days say closed. A visitor should not have to work out whether your regular Saturday hours still apply.
  • What is happening, in one or two plain sentences. A discount, a gift with purchase, free wrapping, a local maker in the store from noon to three, coffee at the door. If nothing special is planned, say what is new on the shelves.
  • Where to park. Downtown Sarasota, St. Armands Circle and the island in Venice are all busy that weekend. One sentence about the nearest garage or lot removes a real reason people give up.
  • What you sell, shown in a few current photos. Someone who has never been inside is deciding whether your shop is worth a stop on the strength of four pictures.
  • A way to buy without coming in. This is the natural week to put a gift card on the site, because out-of-town family and returning customers both look for one. Our post on gift cards on your website covers the options.

Then check the page on a phone, on cellular data, standing outside. That is how most of these visits happen: someone is already out, a friend mentioned your shop, and they have about fifteen seconds of patience. If the hours and the address are not visible without hunting, move them up.

Set a reminder for Sunday night to take the block down. A homepage still announcing a Saturday event the following Wednesday tells visitors that nobody is minding the site.

04Google: Confirm the Hours, Then Post the Plan

For many people the first stop is not your website at all. It is the listing that appears in Google Search and Maps, and the first thing they check there is whether you are open.

Google’s help page on special hours describes them as a way to show a short-term change without altering your regular hours. It adds a piece of advice that owners often skip: “It’s a good idea to confirm your hours for official holidays, even if they’re the same as your regular hours.” The page says this makes it clear to customers that your hours are accurate.

According to the same page, the setting lives under Edit profile, then Hours, then Special hours, where you pick a date and either enter the hours or mark the day closed. Do it for Thanksgiving Day, for Friday and for Saturday. If you are opening an hour early or staying late on the 28th, this is where to say it. Google notes that special hours appear to customers only on the days you designate, so nothing needs to be undone afterward.

Next, add a post to the profile. Google’s help page on posts lists three types: updates, offers and events. An offer needs a title, dates and a time, and can also carry a description, a photo, a coupon code, a link and terms and conditions. An event needs a title with start and end dates and times. A trunk show or a tasting fits the event type; a one-day discount fits the offer type.

While you are in there, add two or three recent photos of the shop as it looks this month. The specifics are in our guide to Google Business Profile photos. A plain caution belongs here too: accurate hours and a fresh post make the listing more useful to the person reading it. They do not buy a higher position, and no one can guarantee where a business appears on Google.

05Social Media: Three Posts and One Morning

A small shop does not need a campaign. Three posts and a little attention on the day itself are enough, and all of it can be prepared in one sitting this week.

  • About a week before: the announcement. A photo of the storefront or the front table, the date, the hours and the one thing that makes the day worth a visit. Put the essentials in the first line of the caption, since many people read no further.
  • Wednesday or Friday: a reminder built around something specific. Three gift ideas under a certain price, a new arrival, the person who will be behind the counter. Specific items give people something to come in and ask for.
  • Saturday morning: a short video or photo of the doors opening, posted to Stories as well as the feed. Then two or three quick updates through the day as things happen. Our post on Instagram Stories as a daily habit explains why that format suits a day like this one.

Two habits make these posts work harder. The first is naming your neighbors. A post that says the coffee shop two doors down is opening early and the gallery across the street has a show gives people a reason to make an afternoon of it, and those businesses tend to return the favor. The second is using the same photo and the same wording everywhere: the homepage block, the Google post and the social post should match, so that nobody finds two different sets of hours.

If you use the free graphics from the American Express materials, mix them with your own photos. A feed made only of campaign artwork looks like every other shop’s feed that week, and it shows nothing of what is actually on your shelves.

06The Sunday After

The value of the day is mostly in who walks in for the first time. Some of those people live ten minutes away and have driven past for years. Others are here until April. Either way, one visit is a beginning, and the shop decides whether it leads anywhere.

Keep it simple. A sign-up sheet or a tablet at the register for an email list, with an honest line about how often you write. A small card in the bag with your web address and your Instagram name. A word at checkout that you are open on Sundays in season, if you are. Our post on getting ready for snowbird season goes further into keeping winter customers through the spring.

On Sunday, post a thank-you with a photo from the day, take the weekend block off the homepage, and write down what you would do differently. How many new faces came in, what they asked about and what sold out are better guides for next November than any national figure.

It is fair to keep expectations modest. One Saturday will not change a year, and a neglected website cannot be rescued in a week. If this weekend shows that yours is hard to update, or does not show what you sell, that is worth fixing in the quieter weeks of January.
