Web Designer vs Web Developer: Who Does a Small Business Actually Need?

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01Two Job Titles, One Overlapping Job

If you are comparing a web designer vs a web developer, the first thing to know is that the two titles overlap more than the names suggest. In practice, one person or one small studio often does both jobs, and a larger project may split them between two people. The words describe the kind of work, not always the kind of company.

According to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, whose Occupational Outlook Handbook page was last modified on August 27, 2026, web developers create and maintain websites, while digital designers develop, create and test a site's or interface's layout, functions and navigation for usability. That is a useful starting point. One group is concerned with how a site is put together to work, and the other with how a person finds their way around it.

For a small business owner the practical question is narrower than the job titles. You want to know who will make your site look trustworthy, who will make the booking form or the menu page actually work, and whether you need to pay two people to get both. The answer depends on the project, so this article walks through three typical local ones after covering the basics.

02What a Web Designer Usually Does

A web designer decides what the site looks like and how a visitor moves through it. That includes the layout of each page, the colors and type, the order of the sections, where the phone number sits and what a button says. Upwork describes web design as covering everything related to a website's visual aesthetics and usability.

Good design is not decoration. A visitor on a phone in a parking lot is trying to answer a few questions quickly: what does this business do, is it open, and how do I reach someone. A designer's job is to make those answers easy to find, and to make the page feel consistent with the rest of your brand.

Many designers also write or shape the text, choose or edit photos and set up the site on a platform where you can change things yourself. Upwork notes that designers work with tools such as Figma and Adobe software, that many use builders like WordPress or Wix, and that they often know some HTML, CSS and JavaScript, though most do not do heavy coding.

03What a Web Developer Usually Does

A web developer builds the working parts. Upwork describes web development as the engineering that supports a website and makes sure everything functions behind the scenes, and says developers code the designer's wireframes and mock-ups into working pages. The same article splits the field into front-end work, which covers what users interact with, and back-end work, which handles data and server-side functions.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics draws a similar line. It says back-end web developers are responsible for the overall technical construction of a website, front-end developers create the technical features for a site's look, and webmasters maintain and update websites. A developer who does both sides is often called full-stack.

At the most basic level, a web page is built from three technologies. According to MDN Web Docs, HTML is the markup language that tells browsers how to structure a page, and CSS is the language used to style it, for example by changing colors, fonts and spacing. JavaScript, the third piece, is the scripting language that makes parts of a page respond to what a visitor does. A developer works in these, and in the server-side tools behind them, in ways a typical designer does not.

04Three Local Projects and Who Each One Needs

These are hypothetical examples, not clients, but they match the kinds of requests a local studio hears. In each one, the useful question is where the difficulty actually lies.

Project one: a Venice landscaping company with a five-page site. The owner needs a clear list of services, photos of finished yards, a service area and a quote request form. This is mostly a design job. The form is a standard feature that a good website platform already provides, so a custom developer adds little. What matters is clear structure, honest photos and a contact method that works on a phone.

Project two: a Lakewood Ranch dental or medical office that wants online appointment requests tied to its scheduling software. Here the design still matters, because patients need to trust the page. But the harder part is the connection between the website and the software the office already runs. That is where developer skills earn their cost, or at least a designer who is comfortable setting up and testing the vendor's own embed or integration. Ask the software vendor what it supports before you hire anyone.

Project three: a Sarasota retailer that wants to sell products online, with inventory, shipping options and sales tax. This is a mixed job. The storefront needs careful design so that products are easy to browse, and the checkout and the connection to payment and shipping services need technical care. Most small shops use an established store platform for this rather than building from scratch, which keeps the developer's role small. A custom build is a different and larger project.

05When One Person Is Enough and When You Need Both

For most small business sites, a single person or a small studio who can design the pages and set them up on a maintained platform is enough. Brochure sites, restaurant sites, contractor sites and professional practices tend to fall into this group. The work is mostly about clear content, a sound structure and a site that loads and reads well on a phone.

You are more likely to need a dedicated developer when the site has to do something unusual. Examples include a members-only area, a custom calculator, a connection to software that has no ready-made plugin, or a large catalog. In those cases, ask whether the design and the build will be handled by the same team. Handoffs between a designer and a separate developer are a common place for misunderstandings, so someone should be responsible for the finished result.

Titles can also mislead. Some people who call themselves developers have little design training, and some designers who call themselves developers mostly assemble sites in a builder. Neither is a problem if the person is honest about what they do. Ask to see sites they have finished, ask what they built themselves and what they used off the shelf, and ask who will fix something if it breaks in six months.

06How to Decide and What to Ask

Start with a plain list of what your site must do. Write down the pages you need, the actions a visitor should take, and any other software it has to connect to. If the list is mostly pages and a contact form, you are shopping for a designer who builds. If it includes connections to other systems, you are shopping for a team that can show it has done that before.

When you talk to someone, a few questions help regardless of the title:

  • Who will design the pages, and who will build and test them?
  • Which parts of the site will be built from scratch, and which use an existing platform or plugin?
  • Will I be able to edit text and photos myself afterward?
  • Who owns the domain, the hosting account and the site files?
  • What happens if something stops working after launch, and who do I call?
