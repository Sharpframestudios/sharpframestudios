Facebook Business Page Setup, Done Right

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01Start With the Person Who Owns the Account

A Facebook business page is not a second personal profile. It is a separate public page for the business, and it is managed through a personal Facebook account. Optyo's guide to creating a Page notes that you need a personal account first, since Pages are managed through it.

That detail matters more than it sounds. If the page is created from a personal account that belongs to a former employee, a nephew who built the first version, or an agency that has since moved on, the owner can end up locked out of their own business. Before you click anything, decide which person at the business will be the long-term owner of the page, and create it from that person's own account.

Also decide what the page is for. For most local businesses it does three jobs: it confirms the business is real, it gives people a quick way to call or message, and it shows recent activity. It is not a replacement for a website, and our post on choosing social media platforms for a local business covers where Facebook fits next to the others.

02Name, Category and Description

Optyo's walkthrough describes the first steps as choosing a category that fits the business, then a name that is memorable and accurately represents it, then a short description of what you do and what sets you apart. Each of those is a place where small businesses make avoidable mistakes.

For the name, use the name customers already know, exactly as it appears on your sign, invoices and website. A page called something clever that nobody recognizes is harder to find than one that matches what people type. Adding the town can help if a similar name exists elsewhere, for example a bakery name followed by Venice, but only if it is how people already refer to you.

For the category, pick the one that describes what customers would call you, not what you wish to be. A person running a landscaping company should choose something close to landscaping, not a broad business label. The category appears under the name on the page, so it is one of the first things a visitor reads.

For the description, write one or two plain sentences: what you do, where, and for whom. A line such as family-run pool service covering Sarasota and Lakewood Ranch tells a stranger more than a paragraph about passion for quality. If you have already written an about page for your website, borrow its opening lines so the two stay consistent.

03Contact Details, Hours and the Web Address

Optyo's guide lists the contact details to add as your website, phone number, email and a physical address if you have one, followed by a custom username to make the page easier to find. Treat this as the most important part of the setup. A page with a lovely cover photo and no phone number loses the customer who was ready to call.

Use the same phone number, address and website everywhere. If the page says one thing, your website says another and an old directory listing says a third, customers lose confidence and you spend your time correcting people. Pick one set of details and copy it exactly.

Business hours deserve special care, because wrong hours are one of the quickest ways to annoy a customer who drove across town. Many Sarasota-area businesses change hours with the seasons, opening longer during the winter months when snowbirds are in town and cutting back in the summer. Update the hours when that happens, and check them again before any storm that could close you. A short post saying you are closed for a hurricane warning is better than a customer arriving at a locked door.

If your website lists the same hours, change both on the same day. Setting up the website to be the main source of truth, and the Facebook page to mirror it, keeps the two from drifting apart.

04The Action Button and the Cover Photo

Synup's how-to describes adding a button by choosing Add a Button below the cover photo, picking the action you want, and then choosing a destination such as a phone number, email or website. It describes buttons that let visitors contact you, shop, order food or book a service. The options you see can vary by business type and over time, so check what your own page offers.

The useful question is which single action you most want a new customer to take. For a plumber it is probably calling. For a restaurant it might be a reservation or the menu. For a photographer it could be a message. Choose that one and point it at a place that works. A button that sends people to a broken link or an unanswered inbox is worse than no button.

For photos, Optyo suggests a profile picture such as a logo, mascot or product image, and a cover photo that showcases the brand. In practice, a clear logo or a face works well for the small profile picture, because it is shown at a tiny size next to posts and comments. For the cover, use a real photo of your storefront, your team or your work instead of a stock image. Check how both look on a phone, since that is where most people will see them.

05Who Gets Access: Roles and Admin Control

This is the part most owners skip and later regret. Leadsie's guide to giving Facebook Page access explains three levels. Full control lets the holder manage content, messages, comments, ads, insights and settings, and it also covers managing other people's access and deleting the page. Partial control is the default and covers the same day-to-day work but not managing access and critical settings. Task access limits someone to chosen tasks such as content, messages, ads or insights.

Leadsie advises granting full control only to trusted owners, and using partial or task-based access for employees, freelancers and agencies, matched to their actual work. That is sound advice for a small business. The owner should hold full control, ideally with a second trusted person as a backup so one lost password does not lock everyone out. Whoever posts for you, whether a staff member, a social media manager or an agency, usually needs only enough access to do that job.

It also helps to review the list now and then. When an employee leaves or a contractor finishes a project, remove their access the same day. Keep a note, in a safe place and not in an email thread, of who holds full control and which account owns the page.

06A Short Checklist Before You Announce the Page

Before you invite anyone to follow the page, read it once as a stranger would. Does the name match your sign? Does the category describe what you sell? Can a person call, message or find the website in one tap? Are the hours right for this month? Is the owner account one you will still control in five years?

Then post a few things so the page does not look abandoned: a photo of your work, a note about what you offer, and a line about where you serve. Consistency matters more than volume, and our guide to how often a business should post can help you set a realistic rhythm. No one can promise how many people a page will reach, so aim to be accurate and easy to contact rather than chasing numbers.
