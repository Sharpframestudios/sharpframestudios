Why Isn't My New Website Showing Up on Google?

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01Start With What a Search Engine Actually Does

You launch a new website, type the business name into Google, and nothing comes up. After weeks of planning and a launch day that felt like an event, it is a deflating moment. It is also very common, and in most cases it does not mean anything is broken.

Google does not receive a notice when a site goes live. It has to discover the address, visit the pages, read them and decide whether to add them to its index. Each of those steps takes time, and none of them is under your control. In its documentation on asking Google to recrawl pages, Google says that crawling can take anywhere from a few days to a few weeks.

So the first thing to understand is the difference between being indexed and ranking. A page can be in Google's index and still sit far down the results for a search like "plumber near me." This article is about the first problem, getting seen at all. No one can guarantee a position on Google, and anyone who promises you one is selling something they cannot deliver.

02Set a Realistic Timeline

Google's own guidance gives a wide range, from a few days to a few weeks, and it says that requesting a crawl does not guarantee your pages will be included in search results instantly or even at all. For a brand new domain with no links pointing to it, the slower end of that range is normal. A page on a site that Google already knows well is usually picked up faster than the first page of a site it has never seen.

If your site went live yesterday, the most useful thing you can do is wait a few days before worrying. If it has been a month and nothing has appeared, that is worth investigating, and the checks below are where to start. Between those two points, the honest answer is that it depends on the site.

It also helps to separate your business name from everything else. A search for your exact business name is usually the easiest thing for a site to show up for, because there is little competition for it. Searches like "web designer Sarasota" or "roof repair Venice" involve many established businesses, and a new site may not appear for them for a long time even once it is indexed.

03Check Whether Google Can Actually See the Site

Sometimes the delay is not patience, it is a setting. The most common cause on a new site is a leftover instruction telling search engines to stay away. Many website builders and developers add one while a site is under construction, and it can be forgotten at launch.

There are two places to look. The first is a noindex tag in the page's code. According to Google's documentation, when Googlebot crawls a page and finds that tag, Google will drop the page entirely from search results. The second is the robots.txt file at the root of your site. Google's guidance is that a page blocked there cannot be crawled, and that for a noindex rule to work, the page must not be blocked by robots.txt. A site can carry both mistakes at once.

Here is a short checklist to run through, or to hand to whoever built your site:

  • Open your site's address followed by /robots.txt in a browser. If you see a line that says Disallow: / under User-agent: *, the whole site is closed to crawlers.
  • View the page source on your homepage and search for the word noindex. If it appears in a robots meta tag, remove it.
  • Make sure the site loads without a password, a coming-soon screen or a login in front of it.
  • Confirm the address you launched on is the one you are searching for, including whether it uses www or not, and whether the older temporary address still exists.

If you are not comfortable reading page source, ask your designer to check these four items. It should take a few minutes.

04Use Google Search Console to See What Google Sees

Google Search Console is a free tool from Google that shows how it sees your site. You verify that you own the site, and the tool then reports what it has found. It is the closest thing to a direct line to Google that a small business owner has, and it replaces guessing with information.

The URL Inspection tool lets you paste in a page address and see whether Google has it. You can also ask for indexing from there. Google's help page says that submitting a request does not guarantee the page will appear in the index, that there is a daily limit on requests, and that indexing typically takes a day or so but can take much longer in some cases. For many pages, Google recommends submitting a sitemap instead of requesting them one at a time. Its documentation also notes that requesting a recrawl several times for the same page will not get it crawled any faster.

A sitemap is a file listing the pages you want Google to know about. According to Google, it is an important way for Google to discover the URLs on your site. If your site was built with a sitemap, submitting it in Search Console is a sensible one-time step.

05Read the Status Messages Without Panicking

Search Console's page indexing report sorts your pages into groups, and some of the labels sound alarming. Google's help pages define them plainly. "Discovered, currently not indexed" means the page was found but not crawled yet. Google says it typically wanted to crawl the address but expected this to overload the site, so it rescheduled the crawl.

"Crawled, currently not indexed" means Google visited the page but chose not to add it. Google says it may or may not be indexed in the future and that there is no need to resubmit the address. In practice this often points to a page that is thin, nearly identical to another one, or too new for Google to be sure it is worth keeping. Adding real, specific information to the page is a better response than clicking the request button again.

Two other labels are worth knowing. "Excluded by noindex tag" and "Blocked by robots.txt" are the problems from the previous section, and they mean you have a setting to fix. "Duplicate, Google chose different canonical" means Google considers another address a better version of the same page, which can happen when a site is reachable at several addresses.

06Give Google Reasons to Take Your Site Seriously

Once the technical checks pass, the remaining work is ordinary and a little slow. A site that is clear about what the business does, where it operates and how to get in touch gives Google more to work with. A homepage that says only "welcome" gives it very little. Our guide to what should be on a small business homepage walks through what each section needs to say.

For a business that serves people in a particular area, a Google Business Profile is a separate thing from your website, and it is often what appears in map results. According to Google's Business Profile help, only verified businesses can show their business information on Maps and Search, and rankings for new businesses can take up to a month to appear. If you serve customers in Sarasota, Lakewood Ranch or Bradenton and have not claimed and verified your profile, do that alongside the site, and keep the name, address and phone number consistent with what is on your website.

Links from other places also help Google find you. A listing in a local chamber of commerce directory, a mention from a supplier or a link from your social profiles all give Google another route to your site. None of this is a trick, and none of it works overnight.
