How Much Does a Professional Website Cost in St. Petersburg?

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01Why the Same Brief Gets Quotes Ten Times Apart

St. Petersburg is a real agency market. Send the same one-page brief to six local vendors and you can genuinely receive quotes of $2,000, $9,000, $24,000 and $90,000 — and none of them need be dishonest.

That spread exists because St. Pete has an unusually deep bench of people who can build a website. The Warehouse Arts District alone houses more than two hundred artists and creative studios. The EDGE District, designated a Florida Main Street in 2014, and the Grand Central District, designated in 2001, are both dense with small creative firms. Meanwhile the Gateway area in the north of the city supports thousands of businesses and tens of thousands of employees around major employers including Raymond James Financial and Home Shopping Network, which has produced a supply of people with corporate marketing experience.

The result is a market where a solo designer working from a studio near Central Avenue and a twenty-person agency serving national accounts will both answer your enquiry. They are quoting for different work, using the same word.

Everything below describes typical market ranges in the Tampa Bay region rather than a quote. The useful skill is not finding the lowest number — it is working out what each number actually contains.

02The Four Kinds of Vendor You Are Comparing

Most quotes come from one of four sources, and knowing which one you are reading explains most of the price difference.

Do-it-yourself platforms

A subscription of a few hundred dollars a year plus a great deal of your own time. Genuinely adequate for a very small business with simple needs and someone willing to learn. The hidden cost is the owner's hours and the ceiling it hits when the business grows.

Independent freelancers, roughly $2,000 to $8,000

Often excellent designers, and St. Pete has many. You get direct access to the person doing the work, and lower overhead. The risks are capacity and continuity — one person gets ill, takes a full-time role, or simply stops answering, and there is no one behind them.

Small studios, roughly $8,000 to $30,000

A team of a few people covering strategy, design, build and content. This is where most established local businesses land. You get process, more than one person who understands your site, and usually a maintenance arrangement afterwards.

Full-service agencies, $30,000 and up

Appropriate for complex builds, multiple locations, regulated industries, or organizations that need procurement-friendly documentation and account management. You are also paying for that structure, which is worth it when you need it and wasteful when you do not.

There is also offshore development, which can be very inexpensive and occasionally very good. The recurring difficulty is that briefs are interpreted literally across time zones, so it suits well-specified work and suits vague creative work poorly.

03Typical Ranges by Project Type

Vendor type explains part of the price. What you are actually asking for explains the rest.

A straightforward presence site, roughly $3,000 to $12,000

Five to ten pages, custom design, your own content or light writing support, mobile-ready, basic search setup. Suits a law firm, a dental practice, a Grand Central boutique, a consultancy.

A lead-generation site, roughly $10,000 to $30,000

Everything above plus proper content work, service pages built around what customers search for, conversion tracking, analytics, and a structure designed to be added to. This is what most St. Pete service businesses actually need and frequently do not buy.

An online store, roughly $15,000 to $75,000

Driven by catalogue size, product variants, shipping rules, tax handling, and whether it must talk to inventory or point-of-sale systems. A gallery selling forty pieces and a retailer selling four thousand are not comparable projects.

A custom platform, $50,000 and up

Portals, booking systems, membership areas, multi-location management, or anything connected to research and institutional systems of the kind found around the Innovation District. Priced as software, because that is what it is.

These bands overlap deliberately. A quote landing between two of them usually means the scope is genuinely between two of them, which is fine as long as everyone has said so out loud.

04The Line Items That Move the Number Most

Six things account for most of the variation between two proposals for the same business.

  • Content. Whether the vendor writes the pages or you do. This is the largest single swing in most projects and the one most often assumed rather than agreed.
  • Design origin. A customized template costs a fraction of a design created from your brand. Both are legitimate; they are not the same purchase.
  • Photography and video. Real images of your premises, team and work, versus stock. In a city this visually competitive, this is rarely a trivial choice.
  • Integrations. Booking, payments, CRM, email, inventory, practice management. Each connection is real engineering work, and each one is where cheap quotes quietly assume you will handle it.
  • Search and measurement setup. Structure, titles, internal linking, analytics, tracking of calls and form submissions.
  • Accessibility and performance. Meeting contrast, keyboard and screen-reader standards properly takes deliberate time, and matters more for organizations serving the public or holding public contracts.

Ask every vendor to price content and photography as separate lines. Two proposals that look thousands apart are often identical once you discover one of them included the writing and the other did not.

05Reading the Gap Between Two Proposals

When you have several quotes in front of you, the totals are the least informative part. Four questions extract more.

What is explicitly excluded

A good proposal states what is not included. A proposal that lists only what you get is usually relying on the exclusions to stay invisible until later.

What can I change without calling you

Ask for a demonstration, not an assurance. Watch someone add a page, change a price and swap a photograph. If that requires a developer, you have a recurring cost that is not on the quote.

Who owns the site if we part company

You should own the domain, the content, the design assets and the ability to move the site elsewhere. Some low prices are subsidised by arrangements that make leaving expensive. This is a fair business model when disclosed and a problem when it is not.

What happens in month seven

Sites break. Plugins update, integrations change, forms stop delivering silently. Ask what support costs, what response time you get, and who to contact. Regional care retainers commonly run from under a hundred dollars a month for basic upkeep to several hundred for active improvement.

One thing no proposal can honestly offer is a guaranteed search ranking. Nobody controls Google's results, and a promise of a specific position is a reason to discount everything else in the document. What good work does is improve coverage, relevance and the fundamentals that raise your probability over time.

06What St. Pete Businesses Tend to Get Wrong

Two mistakes recur in this market, and they pull in opposite directions.

Overbuying design, underbuying content

In a city with this much creative talent and this much visual competition, it is easy to spend the entire budget on how the site looks and leave nothing for what it says. The result is a beautiful site that cannot answer a customer's third question.

For most businesses the ratio should be less lopsided than it usually is. A striking site with thin content loses to a plain site that explains things properly, particularly for services people research carefully.

Underbuying structure, then paying twice

The other failure is buying a site sized for the business as it is today. A practice that adds a second location, a firm that adds three service lines, a retailer that adds a store — all discover that the site cannot hold it, and pay for a rebuild eighteen months after launch.

Buying a structure with room in it costs modestly more at the start and considerably less over five years.

A reasonable way to decide

Work out what one new customer is worth to you and how many the site needs to produce to justify its cost. For a Downtown law firm that number might be one client. For a Deuces Live café it might be several hundred visits. That calculation settles most budget arguments faster than comparing quotes does.
