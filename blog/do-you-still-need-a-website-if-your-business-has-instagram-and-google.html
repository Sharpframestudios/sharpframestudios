Do You Still Need a Website If Your Business Has Instagram and Google?

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01The Short Answer Is Yes, With a Reason

Plenty of local businesses run on an Instagram account, a Facebook Page and a Google Business Profile, and for a while that works. Customers find the profile, send a message, and book. The question is not whether those tools are useful. It is whether a business should depend on them alone.

A Clutch survey of small businesses, reported in an August 2025 press release, found that 17 percent had no website. The report says 83 percent had one, up from 64 percent in 2018. The release attributes much of the gap to misconceptions. It says over a third of the businesses without a site believed a website was not relevant and relied on social media or online marketplaces instead.

So the belief is common. This post looks at what the belief leaves out, using plain examples, and at what a website does that a profile cannot. Nobody can promise that a website will bring more customers or a better position on Google, and this post does not. It makes a narrower case about control.

02What You Are Renting on Social Platforms

An Instagram or Facebook account is space inside someone else’s product. The platform decides how posts are shown, what the profile layout looks like, which features exist next year and which do not. A business can follow every rule and still see those decisions change underneath it.

Reach is the clearest example. According to a 2026 article from Smash Balloon, a social feed plugin company, citing its own research, Facebook organic reach has fallen from 16 percent in 2012 to under 2 percent for most business pages. That is a vendor’s figure, not an independent study, so treat it as a rough direction and not a precise number. Still, most owners who post regularly will recognize the pattern: the same effort reaches fewer of the people who already follow the page.

Then there is access. A profile can be hacked, locked or removed for a reason that the owner may not understand. Appeals exist, but they run on the platform’s timeline. A website on a domain you own does not depend on one company’s review queue to stay online.

Picture a Lakewood Ranch landscaping company whose only presence is a Facebook Page with six years of project photos. If the page is restricted the week before a busy season, every photo, review and customer message goes with it. The business still exists, but its public record does not.

03Google Is Also a Rented Space

A Google Business Profile feels more official than a social page, and for local searches it often matters more. It is also governed by guidelines and can be suspended. According to Google’s Business Profile help pages, a profile that breaks its guidelines can be suspended or disabled, and when a profile is removed the public cannot view it and its owners and managers lose the ability to manage it.

The same help page describes an appeals tool. It says each appeal can be filed once, that supporting documents such as a business registration, license, tax certificate or utility bill can strengthen it if the name and address match the profile, and that owners should not create a new profile for the same business. In other words, there is a process, and it takes time and paperwork.

Google’s own help material also says verifying a business tells Google you are authorized to represent it, so it is more likely to show up in search results, and that there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking. That is worth sitting with. Even the best-kept profile cannot buy position, and a website does not change that. What a website adds is a home page that Google, and everyone else, can point to.

For a business without a storefront, the profile is often the only public listing. Our post on setting up a Google Business Profile with no storefront covers how to do that well, and it works best when the profile has a website to link to.

04What a Website Does That a Profile Cannot

A website is the one place where the business sets all of the rules. That sounds abstract, so here are the practical differences:

  • You choose the structure. A profile gives you a bio and a few buttons. A site can have a page for each service, a clear price range or process, an FAQ and a booking form, all in an order that makes sense for your customers.
  • You own the address. A domain name that is registered to you can move between hosts and designers. A social handle cannot move anywhere.
  • You can explain things at length. A caption is a few lines. A service page can answer the five questions every customer asks before they call.
  • You can be found without an account. People who do not use Instagram, or who do not want to sign in to see a business, can still read a web page.
  • You can link everything together. The website can point to the Facebook Page, the Instagram account and the Google profile, so each one supports the others.

That last point is the one most often missed. A website is not a replacement for social media. It is the hub the other pages point back to. Our guide on why your website is still your most important digital asset goes into the ownership side in more depth.

05Who Can Get By Without One, and Who Probably Cannot

Some businesses really do operate well on a profile alone. A food truck that posts its location each morning, a hairstylist with a full book from referrals, or a seasonal vendor at a weekend market may not gain much from a site. For them, the cost and upkeep of a website may be more than the benefit.

The picture changes when customers need to compare, trust or plan. A few signs a profile is no longer enough:

  • People ask the same questions in direct messages again and again, such as service areas, hours, pricing or what to prepare.
  • Customers want to book, request a quote or pay without a back-and-forth conversation.
  • Your work is high-trust or high-cost, such as roofing, legal help, remodeling or healthcare, where buyers look for proof before they call.
  • You serve seasonal residents who research from another state before they arrive on the Gulf Coast, and who may not follow local social accounts.
  • You depend on one platform for almost all of your leads.

Snowbird season is a good test. A homeowner in another state looking for a pool service or a house watcher in Sarasota may search by name, open a few results and decide quickly. A clear page with services, service area and a contact form answers faster than scrolling a feed. Our post on getting your website and social ready before snowbird season looks at that pattern.

06A Practical Way to Start Without Overbuilding

A website does not have to be large. For many small businesses, a home page, one page per main service, an about page and a contact page is a solid first version. Each page should say what you do, where you do it, who it is for and how to reach you. If you want a checklist, we wrote a section-by-section guide to a small business homepage.

Before building anything, do three small things this week. Make sure you control the login and recovery email for every account the business uses. Write down the exact business name, address, phone number and hours so they match everywhere. And register the domain name in the business owner’s own account, not a freelancer’s or a friend’s.

Once you have a site, link to it from every profile and add the profiles to the site. If you are weighing what that would take, you can read about how pricing works or start a conversation with us. Scope drives cost, so a short list of the pages you need will make any estimate more useful.
