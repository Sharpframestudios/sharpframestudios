Instagram for Business: Setup and the First 30 Days

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01Start With the Right Kind of Account

Instagram offers three kinds of account: personal, business and creator. Business and creator accounts are both called professional accounts. For a shop, a restaurant, a contractor or a salon, the business type is usually the better fit, because it is built around customers contacting you rather than followers watching a personality.

According to Shopify’s guide to Instagram business accounts, updated in September 2026, a business account includes Instagram Insights, access to Meta’s advertising tools, shopping features, contact buttons for email, phone or directions, and a category label. Creator accounts lean toward public figures and influencers. Shopify also notes that you can switch between the two at any time without losing your data, so the choice is not permanent.

To switch an existing personal account, Shopify describes the path as Edit profile, then Switch to professional account, then choosing a category and confirming. Setup takes a few minutes and the account is free. If you already run a Facebook page, it is worth reading our post on setting up a Facebook business page first, since the two can be connected and it helps to have your business details settled in one place.

02Name, Username and Bio

Your username is what people type or tap to find you, so it should look like your business name with as few extras as possible. A Venice landscaping company is easier to find as its actual name than as a string of numbers and underscores. If the exact name is taken, add a short word such as the town rather than a long suffix.

According to a 2026 guide from Replug, the bio is limited to 150 characters, the name field to 30 characters and the username to 30 characters. The same guide says the bio limit has not changed in 2026. That is very little room, so every line has to do a job.

A bio that works for a local business usually answers three questions in order:

  • What do you do? Use plain words, such as “Family-owned bakery” or “Interior painting”, not a slogan.
  • Where do you do it? Name the area you serve, such as Sarasota, Lakewood Ranch or Bradenton, so someone nearby knows right away.
  • What should they do next? Call, book, order or visit the website.

Shopify notes that a business account can also show a category, location and address without using up bio characters, so put the address there rather than spelling it out in the text. A clear profile photo matters as much as the words. Most businesses should use a logo or a simple, recognizable photo of the storefront or team, at a size that still reads when it is shrunk to a small circle.

03Links and Contact Buttons

The link on your profile is the one place on Instagram where people can click straight through to you. Replug reports that a profile can hold up to five links, added under Edit profile, and that they do not use any of the 150 bio characters. Instagram can change features like this, so check the current screen on your own phone.

Send the first link to the page you most want visitors to reach: your website, a booking page or a menu. If you have a website, it should be the destination most of the time, because that is where you control the wording, the layout and the contact form. Our post on whether you still need a website if you have Instagram explains why a profile alone is not enough.

Turn on the contact buttons too. A visitor who has already decided to call should not have to hunt for the number. For a business with a physical location, such as a cafe in downtown Sarasota or a boutique on St. Armands Circle, the directions button saves a step for tourists and new residents.

04The First Posts Before You Tell Anyone

An empty profile gives a new visitor no reason to follow. Before you invite customers, add six to nine posts so the grid looks lived in. They do not have to be polished. They do have to be real, which means your actual work, your actual space and your actual people.

A good mix for a first batch covers what you do, who does it and why someone should trust you. For example, a Bradenton pool service might post a before-and-after of a green pool, a photo of the crew at the start of a route, a short caption explaining how often a pool needs testing, and a note about the service area. A retiree-heavy customer base tends to respond to clear, readable captions and plain information such as hours and phone numbers, not trend-driven edits.

Photos from your phone are fine, as long as they are in focus and in good light. Stand near a window, wipe the lens, and shoot the work or the product from a few angles. A steady, honest picture of a finished kitchen or a fresh pastry tray will do more for a new account than a stock image ever could, and customers can tell the difference.

Write captions as if you were talking to a customer standing at the counter. Say what the photo shows, add one useful detail and, where it fits, a simple next step. Avoid stuffing a caption with tags. Replug reports that Instagram reduced the hashtag limit to five per post or reel on December 18, 2025, so a handful of relevant tags is the most you can use anyway.

05Highlights and Stories as a Storefront Window

Stories disappear from view after a short time, but Highlights let you keep chosen Stories on your profile, in a row of circles under the bio. That row works like the signs in a shop window. A new visitor can tap through and learn the basics without scrolling the whole grid.

Pick four to six topics that answer the questions customers already ask you. A restaurant might use Menu, Hours and Location, Events and Reviews. A home services company might use Services, Recent Jobs, Service Area and Questions. A seasonal business near the Gulf might add a Season Hours highlight that you update when the snowbirds arrive in the fall and leave in the spring.

Stories are also the easiest place to practice. They ask for less polish than a feed post, so a quick clip of the morning prep, a new arrival on the shelf or a job site at the end of the day is enough. Whatever proves useful to a new customer can later be saved into a Highlight, which turns a few seconds of effort into something that keeps working on your profile.

Give each one a short name and a consistent cover so the row looks tidy. Keep it current: an outdated highlight with last year’s hours is worse than none. After a storm, a closure or a holiday, update the relevant highlight the same day, since many people check Instagram before they check a website.

06A Simple 30-Day Plan

The first month is about building habits, not chasing numbers. No one can promise how many followers a new account will gain, and a small, local, engaged audience is worth more to most businesses than a large, distant one. A plan you can actually keep looks something like this:

  • Week one: switch to a business account, finish the profile, add the contact buttons and publish your first batch of posts.
  • Week two: build the first Highlights, tell existing customers by email, in person and on your website, and reply to every comment and message.
  • Week three: settle on a steady rhythm. Our post on how often a business should post compares what is realistic by platform.
  • Week four: open Insights and look at which posts people viewed, saved or tapped, then do more of what worked.

On Insights, the Meta developer documentation says some metrics are not available on Instagram accounts with fewer than 100 followers, so do not be alarmed if a new account shows limited data at first. The numbers become more useful as the account grows.
