What a Restaurant Website Needs in 2027, and What It Can Skip

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01What Diners Check Before They Decide

A person deciding where to eat rarely reads a restaurant website the way they read a magazine. They are looking for a handful of answers, fast: is it open, where is it, what is on the menu, and can I get a table or an order in. If the site answers those in a few seconds, they move on to the decision. If it does not, they go back to the search results and pick the next name.

There is some data behind this, though it is older than we would like. In a survey published in August 2019, the marketing agency MGH asked 1,101 U.S. adults who dine out or order in at least monthly about restaurant websites. According to that survey, 77% visit a restaurant's website before dining in or ordering, and 68% said a website had discouraged them from visiting a restaurant. Habits have moved on since 2019, so treat the numbers as a snapshot of direction rather than a current measurement.

What the survey reported as reasons is more useful than the totals. Among those who had been put off from dining in, 33% pointed to difficult navigation, 30% to menus that were hard to read, and 30% to an outdated look. Those are fixable problems, and none of them require a large budget or a fashionable design. They require a site that is organized around what a hungry visitor came to find.

02The Essentials, in Order of Importance

If you can only get a few things right, get these right first. They are also the things a diner checks before anything else, roughly in this order.

Hours that are correct today

Outdated hours are one of the most common restaurant website problems, according to the restaurant-marketing site Restaurant Den, and most owners know why: hours change with the season, with holidays, and with staffing. On the Gulf Coast they also change with weather. If you close ahead of a storm, the website is the place people look, so make sure someone on your team can update it in minutes, not days.

The menu as real text on the page

A menu that opens as a PDF is hard to read on a phone and slow to load on a weak connection. Restaurant Den describes this as one of the biggest frustrations for diners, and the survey above found that 30% of people who were discouraged from dining in cited menus that were hard to read. A menu built into the page can be read by anyone, updated by you, and understood by search engines and screen readers alike. Keep prices visible, note the dishes that are seasonal, and add a short line about allergens or a way to ask about them.

Address, phone number and a tap-to-call button

Put the full address and phone number where they can be found without scrolling, and make the phone number a tap-to-call link on mobile. Add parking or valet details if they are not obvious, a map link, and anything that helps a first-time visitor find the door. Around Sarasota and Lakewood Ranch, where a shared plaza or a downtown garage can be confusing, one sentence about where to park saves a phone call.

One clear way to reserve or order

Pick the action you want most, whether that is a reservation, an online order or a phone call, and give it a single prominent button. If you use a third-party booking or ordering service, link to it directly instead of describing it. A visitor who has to work out how to give you money is a visitor you may lose.

03Photos, and How Much Design You Actually Need

The same MGH survey reported that 45% of respondents look for food photography on a restaurant's website, and 36% had been discouraged by photos that were not appealing. The lesson is not that every restaurant needs a professional shoot, although one is often worth it. It is that a few honest, well-lit photos of the food and the room do more than a large gallery of dark phone snapshots.

Show the dishes you want to be known for, the dining room or patio as it actually looks, and perhaps the people who work there. Skip stock images of generic plated food, since regulars and locals can tell. Compress every photo so the page loads quickly, because a slow page on a phone connection is another reason for a visitor to leave.

Design matters mostly through clarity. A calm layout, readable type, and buttons that are easy to hit with a thumb will serve you better than an elaborate animation. 04What a Restaurant Website Can Skip

Knowing what to leave out is half the work, particularly for a small team that has to maintain whatever gets built. These are the items we would question first.

  • An intro animation or splash screen that stands between the visitor and the menu.
  • A video that plays automatically with sound, or a hero slideshow that takes several seconds to load.
  • A menu locked inside a downloadable file or an image with no text.
  • A long history page that pushes the hours and the address further down.
  • A feed of every social post on the homepage, especially if it slows the page or shows stale content.
  • A blog that nobody has time to keep current. An abandoned blog looks worse than none.

None of these is forbidden, and a strong story page or a good video can suit the right restaurant. The test is simple: does it help someone find hours, the menu, the location or a way to book? If not, it should not be in front of those things.

It also helps to ask what happens in the off-season. A restaurant that fills up with snowbirds from November to April and gets quiet in summer may want a small banner for seasonal hours or a summer special, and a page structure that makes such a change a five-minute edit.

05Keep Your Google Listing and Your Website in Agreement

For many diners the first look at your restaurant is not your website but your Google Business Profile, and the two need to say the same thing. Google's help pages say the menu editor lets you add menu items with descriptions and prices and group them into sections such as appetizers and desserts. It also says you can upload a photo or PDF of a menu to have a detailed menu generated, and that changes can take 24 to 48 hours to show on Google Maps and Search.

Google's guidance for restaurants also covers setting business hours, adding separate hours for services like delivery and takeout, uploading photos of the interior and exterior, and connecting order or booking options. Whenever your hours, menu or booking link change, update both places on the same day. Mismatched information is a small thing that quietly costs goodwill, and it is easy to prevent with a short checklist taped up near the office computer.

A quick word on search. A clear, current website and a well-kept Google profile give people accurate information and give search engines something to work with, but no one can guarantee a particular position on Google, and anyone who offers to is promising something they cannot control.

06A Practical Way to Start

Open your website on your phone right now, stand somewhere with an average signal, and try to do what a hungry stranger would do. Find tonight's hours, read the menu, and reach the reservation or ordering link. Time yourself, and note every place you hesitated. That list is your starting brief, whether you fix things yourself or hire help.
