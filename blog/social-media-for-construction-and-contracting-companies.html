Social Media for Construction and Contracting Companies

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01What Social Media Can and Cannot Do for a Contractor

Most building and remodeling work is still won through people. In a nationwide homeowner survey published by Roofing Contractor magazine on April 1, 2025, 79 percent of homeowners said they would look for a contractor through word-of-mouth referrals or recommendations, 62 percent through an internet search, and 54 percent by going back to a firm they had used before. Nearly a quarter said they would use traditional social media channels. The survey was fielded in the fall of 2024 and was sponsored by Roofle Technologies and Owens Corning, so treat it as an industry study about roofing, not a census of every trade.

The same survey shows a wide gap by age. According to the article, 40 percent of millennial homeowners said they would use social media to find a roofer, compared with 27 percent of Generation X and 15 percent of baby boomers. On a coast where many of the people hiring a remodeler in Sarasota, Venice or Lakewood Ranch are retirees, that lower figure deserves attention.

So set the expectation honestly. For most contractors, social media marketing for construction companies is less about strangers finding you and more about what a referred homeowner sees when they look you up. A neighbor gives them your name, and that evening they search for it. A page with recent jobs, clean sites and real crews confirms the referral. A page last updated two summers ago raises a question.

That makes the job smaller than most marketing advice suggests. You do not need daily posts or dance trends. You need a steady record of real work, photographed carefully, that a cautious buyer can scroll through in two minutes.

02Check Every Photo for Safety Before It Goes Up

A job site is the only kind of business location where a marketing photo can also be evidence. Trent Cotney, a partner at the law firm Adams & Reese and general counsel of the Florida Roofing and Sheet Metal Contractors Association, wrote in a May 2026 article for the association that publicly available content, including company websites, social media posts, marketing materials and drone footage, has become a routine starting point for OSHA investigations. He adds that material posted by the employer is generally admissible as evidence. His article is general education and not legal advice, and neither is this post.

Other trade writers put the risk more mildly. An October 2022 article on RoofersCoffeeShop, a roofing trade site, says OSHA is not actively searching company social media pages for violations, but calls posted safety violations one of the most significant mistakes its author sees in company promotions. The two views agree on the practical point: a photo of a worker on a roof without fall protection is a problem whether a regulator, an insurer or a prospective customer notices it first.

An article in Compact Equipment magazine on job-site photography recommends having the safety director screen every image for unsafe conditions before it is used, and setting a policy on who is authorized to take pictures. A small company has no safety director, so give the job to one person, usually the owner or the lead, and have them look at each photo for the following before it is posted.

  • Hard hats, eye protection, gloves and fall protection on everyone in the frame, worn correctly.
  • Ladders, scaffolds and open edges. If you would not want an inspector to see it, do not post it.
  • Trenches, electrical panels and tools in use, where a shortcut is easy to spot.
  • Housekeeping. Loose debris and trailing cords make a careful crew look careless.

If a photo fails, do not crop around the issue and post it anyway. Fix the condition on site, then take a new picture. Homeowners read a tidy, well-protected site as a sign of how you will treat their property.

03It Is Someone Else’s Property

A restaurant photographs its own dining room. A contractor photographs a client’s home, a developer’s building or a business that is still open to the public. The RoofersCoffeeShop article recommends getting prior written consent before posting on a company website or social media, with the consent spelling out the purposes and how the material will be used. It also advises checking your project contracts for clauses that expressly prohibit posting photos or videos of the project. Commercial and government jobs are where such clauses tend to appear.

The simplest way to handle this on residential work is one sentence in your own contract that asks permission to photograph the work and share it, with a box the owner can tick to decline. Asking at signing is easier than asking in the middle of a kitchen demolition. Ask your crew the same question once, in writing, since their faces will be in the pictures.

Even with permission, leave out what identifies the house. That means the house number, the street sign, a license plate in the driveway, a gate or alarm keypad, and family photos on a wall. On the Gulf Coast there is an added reason for care. Many owners are away for months at a time, and a post that says a named street has an empty house under renovation tells more people than the owner would like. Name the town or the neighborhood at most, such as a bathroom remodel in Bradenton, and wait until a crew is no longer leaving tools there overnight.

04Turn One Job Into a Progress Series

The hardest part of social media for a busy contractor is deciding what to post. A progress series removes that decision. You pick one job, take the same few photos at each stage, and post them in order over the life of the project. One kitchen can supply six to eight posts without anyone inventing anything.

A workable sequence for a remodel looks like this.

  • Before. The room as you found it, shot from one fixed corner you will return to each time.
  • Protection and demolition. Floor covering, dust barriers and the stripped room. Homeowners worry about mess more than almost anything.
  • What is behind the wall. New plumbing, wiring or framing, with one sentence on why it was done that way.
  • The inspection stage. A plain note that the rough work passed, which reminds readers that you pull permits.
  • Finishes going in. Cabinets, tile, a close view of one detail.
  • After. The same corner as the first photo, in daylight, with the site cleaned.

Write each caption like you would explain it to the owner. Say what the stage is, why it matters, and what comes next. A sentence such as moving the sink meant opening the slab, so this is the trench before the concrete goes back teaches something, and teaching is what makes a stranger trust a tradesperson.

The series suits Florida work in particular. A roof replacement before hurricane season, impact windows, a pool cage rebuilt after a storm or a seawall repair all have stages that owners are curious about and rarely get to see. Short video works too: a slow ten-second walk across a finished room often shows more than a photo. 05Put Your Florida License Number on the Profile

Florida treats advertising by licensed contractors as regulated speech. Section 489.119(5)(b) of the Florida Statutes says the registration or certification number of each contractor shall appear in each offer of services, business proposal, bid, contract or advertisement, regardless of medium, as defined by board rule. We read that text on the Florida Senate’s page for the 2024 statutes. The same section says the board issues a notice of noncompliance for a first offense and may fine or cite a contractor who does not correct it within 30 days or who repeats it.

The Jacksonville law firm Jimerson Birr addressed social media directly in a March 2013 blog post. It noted that the board’s rule defines advertising to include any electronic media including internet sites, and told contractors to include the license number on websites, social media pages, Facebook, Twitter and other advertising outlets. That post is more than a decade old and the rule may have been amended since, so confirm the current wording with the Construction Industry Licensing Board or your attorney.

The cautious practice costs nothing. Put the license number in the bio or About section of every profile, and on any image that works as an ad, such as a graphic offering free estimates. 06A Routine That Survives the Busy Season

Plans fail in February, when the winter residents are in town and every crew is booked. Build the routine so it takes minutes. Each lead takes the same three photos at the end of the day, from the same spots, and drops them in a shared album. One person reviews them on Friday against the safety and privacy checks above and schedules one or two posts for the following week.

Reuse the best photos. The finished shots belong on your website’s project pages and on your Google Business Profile, where people who search your name will see them. Our post on Google Business Profile photos covers the sizes and types Google asks for. No one can guarantee a position on Google, and posting more will not change that, but current photos give a searcher something real to judge.

Remember what the homeowner trusts most. In the Roofing Contractor survey, 88 percent of homeowners named referrals as a measure of trust and 74 percent named online reviews, while 31 percent named a company’s online presence. Social media supports the first two. When a job ends well, ask for a review, thank the client in a post if they agree to it, and let the series speak for the work.
