How to Run Social Media for a Restaurant: A Weekly Routine

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01Start With the Hours You Actually Have

A restaurant owner or manager usually has the least spare time of any small business owner. The kitchen runs on a clock, the dining room runs on people, and social media competes with everything else on the list. A routine that assumes two free hours a day will be dropped by the second week of the busy season.

So the useful question is not how much a restaurant could post, but how much it can post every week, month after month. This article lays out a weekly routine sized for roughly an hour or two of planning plus a few minutes a day. It is built around a small number of posts that you can keep up through a Sarasota season, when the dining room is full and nobody has time, and through the slower summer, when it matters just as much.

None of this guarantees a result. Social platforms change how they show posts, and no one can promise reach or sales from a posting schedule. What a routine does give you is a profile that looks alive and current when a new customer checks it before choosing where to eat.

02Decide How Often You Will Post

Published advice varies, and most of it comes from vendors that sell restaurant software, so treat it as a range and not a rule. ChowNow, an online ordering company, suggests two to four feed posts per week on Instagram, plus two or three stories, and two to four posts per week on Facebook. It suggests three to five short videos per week on TikTok. OpenTable's restaurant guide recommends two to three posts per week on Facebook.

For a busy kitchen with no dedicated social media person, the low end of those ranges is the sensible place to start. Two or three feed posts and a couple of stories a week on one or two platforms is plenty. Doing that every week is worth more than posting daily for a month and then going quiet, because a profile whose last post was in March tells a customer the place may not be open.

Pick the platforms by where your guests already are. A beachside spot in Venice with a visitor crowd may live on Instagram. A family restaurant in Bradenton with a regular, older clientele may get more from Facebook. If you cannot do both well, do one well and keep the other accurate, with correct hours, a current menu link and a working phone number.

03Give Each Day a Job

The hardest part of a weekly routine is not the writing, it is deciding what to post at 4 p.m. on a Tuesday. Giving each day a standing theme removes that decision. ChowNow's calendar guide suggests this approach, with examples such as a menu spotlight on Monday, a customer review or guest photo on Wednesday, a behind-the-scenes or staff spotlight on Friday, and a loyalty or rewards reminder on Sunday.

You do not need to copy that schedule. What matters is that the themes fit what your kitchen can produce without extra work. A sample week for a typical independent restaurant might look like this:

  • Monday: one dish or special, photographed in the kitchen light before service. A short caption with the name, the key ingredients and when it is available.
  • Wednesday: a guest photo or a kind review, shared with permission. Tag the guest if they tagged you, and say thank you.
  • Friday: a quick look behind the line, such as prep, a new hire or the dessert being plated, ideally a ten-second clip.
  • Sunday or Thursday: a practical post, such as this weekend's hours, a reservation reminder, live music or a seasonal change to the menu.

If you only manage two of these, keep the dish and the practical post. They answer the two things a new guest wants to know, which are what the food looks like and when they can come in.

04Keep Most Posts About the Place, Not the Sales Pitch

A common rule of thumb is to mix promotion with everything else. ChowNow recommends what it calls an 80/20 approach, in which about 80 percent of posts focus on community, storytelling and entertainment and about 20 percent directly drive sales. It is a guideline from one vendor, not a measured law, but it matches how people behave. A feed made only of discounts reads like an advertisement, and guests scroll past it.

For a restaurant, community content is easy to find because it is already happening in the building. A farmer who drops off tomatoes, a line cook who has been there for ten years, a regular who has ordered the same thing every Thursday since the place opened. These are the posts that make a restaurant feel like a particular place and not a listing.

Video deserves a mention, but only at the scale you can sustain. OpenTable's guide describes short vertical video, from a few seconds to about a minute, as a core format, and it recommends behind-the-scenes clips and reposting guest photos and videos. A phone, a tripod and a steady hand are enough. A rough, honest ten-second clip of the pass during a busy Saturday often serves better than a polished one you will never have time to make.

05Spend One Hour a Month Planning

The routine only holds up if the planning is separated from the posting. ChowNow suggests planning a social calendar a month in advance, or four to six weeks ahead. For a restaurant that means one sitting, once a month, where you look at what is coming and decide what each themed day will cover.

Walk through the calendar with the kitchen in mind. Note the menu changes, holidays, the start and end of the snowbird season, local events, private-event bookings and anything planned for hurricane season, such as a policy for closing and reopening. A closure notice needs to go up quickly, so decide in advance who posts it and where, instead of working that out in the middle of a storm warning.

Then batch the photos. Spend ten minutes at the start of a shift taking pictures of the next several dishes, the way you would plate them for a guest, and save them in one folder named by month. Most platforms let you schedule posts ahead, so the weekly work becomes writing captions and queuing them, not remembering to post.

06Keep Your Google Listing in Step With Your Posts

For many diners, the first look at a restaurant happens in search results or on a map, not on a social profile. Google's Business Profile lets you publish posts that appear in Search and Maps. According to Google's help pages, there are three kinds, which are updates, offers and events. Google also says posts older than six months are archived unless a date range is set, and that posts can be scheduled in advance or set to repeat.

That makes the Business Profile a good place to put the same practical post you use on Sunday, such as holiday hours or a special event, with the same photo. Google reviews posts against its content policy, and it does not allow content about regulated goods such as alcohol, so a post about a cocktail menu may not be approved. Keep the language plain, check the spelling and make sure the hours shown are right.

Social posts and your website should also agree. If the profile says you close at 9 and the website says 10, guests notice. Our article on what a restaurant website needs in 2027 covers how to keep those details consistent.

07Answer People and Know When to Hand It Off

Social media is two-way, and the quickest way to waste a good routine is to ignore the replies. Set a small daily habit of two or three minutes, ideally at the same moment each day, to read comments and direct messages. Answer reservation questions with a phone number or booking link, thank people for kind comments and respond to complaints calmly and in public once, then move the detail to a private message.

If the weekly routine still feels like too much, that is useful information and not a failure. The first trade-off to try is posting less often on fewer platforms. The second is handing the work to someone who does it as part of their job.
