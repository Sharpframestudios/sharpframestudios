Choosing Brand Colors for a Local Business

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01Start With Where the Colors Have to Work

Most owners choose colors by taste. They like a certain green, or a relative has strong feelings about orange, and that becomes the brand. Taste is a fair starting point, but business brand colors have a job to do, and the job is the better test.

Write down every place the colors will appear in the next two years. For a typical local business that list includes a website, a phone screen in daylight, a sign, a vehicle, a shirt, a business card and a small round profile picture.

Three practical questions follow from that list. Is this what customers expect from a business like mine? Can people read text set in these colors or on top of them? And does anything important depend on telling two colors apart?

This post is about choosing the colors. Recording them with exact codes is covered in our guide to a one-page brand style guide.

02What Customers Expect From Your Kind of Business

Every trade has a look. Drive through Sarasota or Bradenton and you will notice how many pool companies, boat services and vacation rental offices use some version of blue or teal, and how many lawn and landscape trucks are green. Color is one of the quickest clues a stranger has about what a business does.

Researchers have measured the pattern among large brands. In a 2013 paper in the journal Marketing Letters, Lauren Labrecque and George Milne examined the colors of 281 top brands across 15 product categories and 40 subcategories. According to the published abstract, the results highlight the existence of visual norms within product categories. The abstract also says that color differentiation is helpful for some categories and can be harmful for others, and that following the norm may be beneficial in categories with a dominant market leader.

The study looked at top national brands, not at a plumber in Venice, and the abstract does not say which categories fell on which side. It supports a modest point: being different is not automatically good.

The same two authors published a set of four studies in the Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science, in the volume dated 2012, under the title “Exciting red and competent blue.” The abstract says the first study relates hues to dimensions of brand personality, and the second looks at how saturation and value, meaning how vivid and how light or dark a color is, amplify those traits. The abstract does not give a chart of which color means what, so be wary of any website that hands you one with great confidence.

So look before you choose. Collect the logos, trucks and websites of eight or ten businesses in your trade and your area, lay them side by side, and decide deliberately.

  • If customers need to recognize what you do at a glance, such as on a van in traffic, stay inside the family of colors people expect and stand apart through shade, a second color or the logo itself.
  • If everyone in your trade uses the same color and customers cannot tell the companies apart, a clear departure may be worth it, as long as the name or the wording still says what you do.
  • If your service is expensive or involves trust, such as legal, medical or financial work, be cautious about a look that reads as playful.

03Test Contrast Before You Fall in Love

The most common color problem on small business websites is not an ugly palette. It is a handsome brand color that cannot carry text.

The standard measure comes from the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, published by the W3C. Its explanation of success criterion 1.4.3, updated on October 4, 2026, says text needs a contrast ratio of at least 4.5 to 1 against its background, and large text needs at least 3 to 1. WebAIM, short for Web Accessibility In Mind, explains in its article on contrast and color that the ratio runs from 1 to 1, which is white on white, up to 21 to 1, which is black on white.

The W3C page also explains where 4.5 comes from, and the reason matters on this coast. It says the figure was chosen to account for the loss of contrast that comes with moderately low vision, and it notes that 20/40 is commonly reported as the typical visual acuity of people at roughly age 80. If a good share of your customers are retirees, that standard was written with them in mind.

WebAIM’s article gives examples that surprise most people. On a white background, pure red has a ratio of 4 to 1, which is below the level required for normal text. Pure green on white is 1.4 to 1, which is close to unreadable. Pure blue on white is 8.6 to 1. The article adds that the ratio stays the same if you swap the text and background colors, so white lettering on a pure red button has the same weakness.

You can still use a bright color. You have to decide in advance what it is allowed to do.

  • Paste each candidate color and white into a free contrast checker, then each color and your dark text color. Write down the ratios.
  • Make sure at least one brand color passes 4.5 to 1 with white, so it can be used for buttons and links.
  • If your favorite color fails, try a slightly darker version of it. A small step is often enough.
  • Keep the light, bright colors for backgrounds, shapes and accents, with dark text on top.

One exception is worth knowing. The W3C page says text that is part of a logo or brand name has no contrast requirement. So a pale logo does not break the guideline, but the menu, the phone number and the button next to it still have to be readable.

04Some of Your Customers See Color Differently

Color vision differences are common enough to plan for. NIH News in Health, a newsletter from the National Institutes of Health, reported in February 2010 that about 1 in every 76 Americans has a color vision defect, citing the National Center for Health Statistics, and that men are affected more often than women. A Live Science article by Marilyn Perkins dated July 16, 2025 cites Cleveland Clinic for a higher figure of about 1 in 12 males and 1 in 200 females. A 2012 review in the Journal of the Optical Society of America A put inherited red-green deficiency among men of European ancestry at about 8 percent, and among women at about 0.4 percent.

The sources count different groups in different ways, so do not lean on any single number. The NIH article says most people with a color vision defect have trouble telling colors apart in the red and green range.

This does not mean red and green are off limits. WebAIM states plainly that the guidelines do not prohibit any specific color or color combination. The actual rule, criterion 1.4.1, is that color must not be the only visual means of conveying information. For a brand, that turns into a few habits.

  • Do not rely on red against green alone to carry meaning, such as a green dot for open and a red dot for closed. Add the word.
  • Pick brand colors that differ in lightness as well as hue. The W3C page notes that the contrast calculation is built so that hue is not the key factor, which is why a dark color beside a light one works for nearly everyone.
  • Print your logo in black and white. If it turns into a gray block, the two colors are too close in lightness.

05Build a Small Palette With Assigned Jobs

Once you have a main color that fits your trade and passes the contrast test, the rest of the palette is mostly supporting work. A small business does well with three roles filled.

  • A main color that carries the identity: the sign, the truck, the headings, the buttons.
  • An accent used sparingly for one purpose, such as the main call to action or a sale tag. This is where a bright color belongs.
  • Neutrals for nearly everything else: an off-white or light sand for backgrounds and a very dark gray or navy for body text.

Most of a well-made website is neutral. The brand color shows up in a few deliberate places, and that restraint is what makes it noticeable.

Vividness and lightness are your adjustment tools. If your teal looks like every other teal on the Gulf Coast, a deeper or grayer version of it may set you apart without leaving the family customers expect.

06Try the Colors in Real Conditions

Before anything is ordered, test the palette where it will live. Bright Florida sun washes out a phone screen, so combinations that looked fine indoors can become unreadable in a parking lot. Ask your sign maker and vehicle wrap installer how your chosen colors hold up outside before you approve a proof.

A short field test covers most surprises.

  • Open a mock-up of your homepage on a phone and read it outdoors at midday.
  • Shrink the logo to the size of a profile picture and see whether the colors still separate.
  • Print the palette on an ordinary office printer. Printed color rarely matches a screen exactly.
  • Show it to two or three customers, ideally including someone over 70, and ask what kind of business they think it is.

Then stop adjusting. Recognition comes from repetition over years. Pick colors that meet the three tests, record the codes, and use them the same way everywhere.
