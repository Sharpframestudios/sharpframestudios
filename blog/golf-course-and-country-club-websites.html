Golf Course and Country Club Websites

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01Two Audiences, One Website

A golf course website has a harder job than most local business sites, because the visitors want different things. A member wants to book a tee time, check the pairings or see this month’s dinner menu. A visitor from Ohio planning a January week wants to know what a round costs, whether the course is open to the public and how to get a time on the sheet.

Private clubs, semi-private clubs, public courses and resort courses all sit somewhere on that spectrum, and the website should say plainly where yours sits. The mistake we see most often in this category is a site that talks to everyone at once and so helps nobody: a homepage that mixes a member login, a wedding venue pitch and a daily-fee rate card without any order.

This post looks at how to organize a site around those audiences, how to handle tee times and events, and what to leave out. It is written for owners, general managers and board members who are deciding what a club site should do, not for software buyers comparing systems.

02Decide Who the Front Door Is For

Start with one question: who does the homepage serve first? For a public or daily-fee course, the answer is usually the visiting golfer, and the member area is a small link in the header. For a private club, the front door often serves prospective members and event clients, while current members go straight to a login. A semi-private club has to serve both, and it helps to give each a clearly labeled path.

A simple way to do that is a short row of choices near the top of the page:

  • Book a tee time, for anyone who can play.
  • Membership, for people considering joining.
  • Events and weddings, for groups and planners.
  • Member login, for people who already belong.

Each choice leads to a page written for that person alone. A member does not need to read about membership tiers, and a prospective member does not need the dues-payment screen. Keeping those separate also keeps the public pages free of anything private.

Be careful about what a private club puts on public pages. Rosters, member photos and event attendee lists belong behind a login, and the club’s own policies should decide where that line falls. A public page can describe the experience without exposing anyone.

03Tee Times Are the Main Job

For most courses the most valuable action on the site is booking a round. That means the booking button should be easy to spot on every page, on a phone as well as a desktop. Teesnap, a golf software vendor, advises in a March 2026 post that courses use a large, high-contrast booking button high on the page and test the site on several devices. That is a vendor’s view, but it matches common sense: if a golfer has to hunt for the button, some will give up.

The harder question is what happens after the click. Club Caddie, another golf software vendor, makes a point in a July 2026 post that every Book Now button should point to the same online booking page, and that web bookings should land directly on the tee sheet and not in an email inbox for staff to retype. Its advice is to test the process from a phone every so often by booking a time and then canceling it. Whatever system your club uses, the principle holds: one booking path, tested regularly.

If your course uses booking software, the website designer and the software vendor need to work together. A design can be handsome and still fail if the booking link is broken, points to an old page or opens in a way that is hard to use on a small screen. Ask who is responsible for checking the link after every change, and put that on a calendar.

Also be clear on the page about rates and rules. Rates change by season, day and time, so say what you can say and give the date it was last updated. A wrong rate on a website is worse than no rate.

04Members and the Public Together

Semi-private courses face a particular puzzle: members expect priority, and the public expects to be able to play. In a January 2026 post on semi-private clubs, Club Caddie describes common approaches, including giving members first access to the tee sheet for a set number of days before opening it to the public, protecting recurring member-only times, and using different booking windows or cancellation rules for different kinds of players. Those are policy choices for each club to make, and a software vendor’s post is only a menu of options.

What the website can do is explain the rules in plain language so staff do not have to explain them by phone all day. A short page called “How booking works” can cover:

  • When the tee sheet opens for members and when it opens for guests.
  • What the cancellation rule is, and any fee.
  • Whether a guest needs to be accompanied by a member.
  • Whether payment is taken at booking or at the course.

Write each rule once, in the same words used at the pro shop counter. If the website says one thing and the staff says another, the website loses the argument and the staff pays for it in awkward conversations.

A shared booking page for members and the public is common, but it is not the only design. Some clubs prefer a separate member portal because the member experience, with handicap, guest passes and account balances, is different. Either can work as long as a visitor knows which one to use.

05Events, Tournaments and Weddings

Many courses earn a large share of income from events, and the website is the first place a planner looks. A tournament organizer needs capacity, formats, food and beverage options, and a clear way to ask about dates. A wedding couple needs photographs, a sense of the space and an idea of what is included.

Give events their own section with a page for each type, and give each event a single registration link rather than a sign-up email address. Club Caddie makes the same point in its July 2026 post: one online registration link per event, used everywhere, keeps the information from splitting across pages and inboxes. For recurring events, such as a weekly league or a monthly scramble, one page that is updated each season is easier to maintain than a new page every time.

Photography matters here more than in many industries. Pictures of the course, the clubhouse and the dining room carry much of the message, and they should be recent and well exposed. A stock photo of a golf course in Scotland on a Lakewood Ranch club’s homepage does not help anyone. If you have budget for one thing, spend it on a half day of good photographs in the early morning light.

Test the inquiry form the same way you test the tee time button. Our post on contact page best practices covers the form basics, and the same ideas apply to an event inquiry: ask for the few details you need to reply, and promise a response time you can keep.

06Seasons, Snowbirds and Storms

On the Gulf Coast, a golf club’s year has a shape. Winter brings visitors and seasonal residents, summer brings heat, afternoon storms and a slower calendar, and hurricane season can close a course on short notice. The website should reflect all of that.

Put a status banner at the top of the homepage that can be updated in a minute: course open, cart path only, closed for storm cleanup, aeration this week. Golfers check the website before they drive over, and a clear banner saves phone calls and a wasted trip. Make sure whoever can update it is reachable on weekends and during storm warnings.

Prepare for the busy season ahead of time. Seasonal rates, snowbird memberships, early-season events and visitor information are better set up in the fall than the week visitors arrive. Our post on getting your website and social ready before snowbird season goes through that kind of preparation.

Keep the club’s details identical across the website, the booking software and the Google Business Profile: name, phone number, address, hours and booking link. Club Caddie notes that mismatches between these can cause trouble, including with Reserve with Google, which the company says requires the details to line up. Nobody can guarantee where a course appears in search or in Maps, so treat consistency as housekeeping.

07What to Leave Out, and Where to Start

Clubs tend to collect pages over the years. A news feed that stops in 2023, a staff list with people who have left, a rates page with last year’s prices and a photo gallery of a tournament nobody remembers all signal neglect. Remove what you cannot keep current. A smaller site that is accurate beats a larger one that is not.

A reasonable starting list for most courses looks like this:

  • A homepage with a clear booking button and a status banner.
  • A rates and booking rules page with a visible update date.
  • A course page with hole descriptions and good photographs.
  • Membership information, if you take new members.
  • Events and weddings, with one inquiry path.
  • A member login or portal link, kept simple.
  • Contact details, hours, directions and a map link.
