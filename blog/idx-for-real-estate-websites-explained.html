IDX for Real Estate Websites Explained

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01What IDX Actually Is

IDX stands for Internet Data Exchange. According to IDX Broker, a company that sells IDX services, it allows agents and brokers to access and display MLS listing information on their own websites. The same company describes it as the software, rules and regulations that let MLS data appear on your site, and says it is also known as broker reciprocity.

The idea is simple. Each broker’s listings go into a shared pool, and each participating broker can show the pool’s active listings on a personal site. A buyer searching on an agent’s website can see homes listed by other offices, and the agent gets a way to meet that buyer.

IDX is not a website. As one vendor puts it, it is software that shares MLS data on a third-party site. You still need a website that is well designed and that explains who you are, and IDX is one feature on it.

02Who Is Allowed to Display It

There is no single national answer, and this is the part that surprises new agents. IDX Broker says who may display listings depends on the rules set by your local board or Multiple Listing Service. Rules differ from one MLS to the next.

The same source explains two terms. A participant is the principal broker member of an MLS, and a subscriber is a non-principal broker member. Normally all participants may take part, while a subscriber, such as an agent working under a broker, may or may not, depending on the rules. Sometimes an agent can show listings only with the broker’s permission.

Opt-in and opt-out also come into it. IDX Broker says most boards and MLSs already include all member brokers and agents by default, and that opting out is done through the board’s form. A separate MLS-education blog says a listing owner can ask to have a listing excluded from display. Because these are vendor and blog descriptions of how things usually work, treat them as background and confirm the specifics with your own MLS.

For an agent in the Sarasota and Bradenton area, the practical step is to ask your broker and your MLS before you pay for anything. Ask whether you may display IDX on a personal site, what the approval process is, and whether there are fees on the MLS side. Membership in an MLS is a prerequisite for approval with IDX Broker, according to that company’s fee page.

03The Rules Your Site Has to Follow

Because MLSs set their own conditions, we cannot give you one definitive checklist, and you should read your MLS’s IDX rules yourself. The National Association of Realtors publishes the policy that sits behind most of them, but its handbook page requires a login, so we could not read it for this post. IDX Broker describes IDX as the policy NAR instituted to govern how participants can display active listing information.

What the vendor sources do say is useful for planning. Most MLSs require that you identify the listing firm, and IDX Broker notes that usually means the listing agent or office name and phone number, in a combination that differs by board. The data fields available for public display are approved by the board and do not contain confidential information.

On freshness, IDX Broker says MLSs generally require only a refresh every 12 hours, and that it aims to update more often, hourly in many cases. A vendor’s claim is not a legal requirement, so confirm the refresh and attribution rules with your own MLS.

One more point on sold data. IDX Broker says availability depends on your MLS and that the search and display of sold data is limited to its higher plans. If sold prices matter to your content plan, check this before you choose a plan.

04Your Options and What They Cost

Most agents end up choosing between a few paths. The first is a hosted real estate website that comes with IDX built in, which can be quick but often looks like a template. The second is an IDX service added to a custom or existing site, through a plugin or an embedded search. IDX Broker says its service works with platforms such as WordPress, GoDaddy, Squarespace and Wix. The third is to rely on the brokerage’s site and skip a personal one.

On cost, here is a sourced example, and it is just one vendor. IDX Broker’s plan page lists three monthly plans: Core at $60, Engage at $99 and Elite at $149 per month, with a month-to-month subscription and no annual contract. It says every plan carries a one-time setup fee, and it does not list the amount. The same company says MLS membership and board fees are separate from its service fees.

So the full cost of IDX has at least three layers: the vendor’s monthly fee, any one-time setup, and whatever your MLS or board charges. Add to that the cost of the website itself, which is separate and depends on its scope. If you want an idea of how a site is priced, see how pricing works on our site.

Behind the scenes, MLS data reaches vendors through a technical feed. IDX Broker says most MLSs today use the RESO Web API standard. You do not need to manage that yourself, but it explains why a new MLS connection can take a few weeks. The vendor quotes different setup times in different places, so ask for the current estimate.

05What Buyers Care About on the Site

IDX gets a visitor to a list of homes. What keeps them is how the search feels. A few things make a difference to someone browsing from a phone:

  • Search that is quick and simple, with filters for price, bedrooms, property type and area that make sense locally. In this region, filters for waterfront, a 55-plus community or a flood zone often matter more than they would elsewhere.
  • Listing pages that load fast and read well on a small screen, with clear photos and a button to ask a question or request a showing.
  • A way to save a search or get alerts, which is how a casual browser becomes a lead.
  • Your own voice around the data. The listings are the same on every agent’s site, so your neighborhood guides, market notes and past-client reviews you have permission to share are what set you apart.

Think about the buyers this region attracts. Many are relocating from another state or buying a second home, and they often start searching long before they visit. A site that lets them save a search and read honest notes on neighborhoods such as Lakewood Ranch, downtown Sarasota or Venice does more for them than a long list of properties with no context. Hurricane season and flood-zone questions also come up often here, so a plain explainer on how you help buyers research a property is worth writing.

Be careful about promises. IDX does not make a site rank on Google. The listing pages are on many other agents’ sites too, and no one can guarantee a position in search results. Your own local content is a more reliable way to give visitors a reason to choose you.

06How to Decide Without Overspending

Start with the simplest version that works. If your brokerage gives you a site with IDX included, decide whether a personal site adds enough to justify the cost. Our post on whether you need your own website if your brokerage gives you one walks through that question.

If you do want your own, get answers to four questions before you sign anything: what the MLS permits, what the total monthly cost is across all layers, whether you can leave without losing your site, and who owns the content and the domain. Month-to-month terms, which IDX Broker says it offers, make it easier to change course.

Also keep IDX in proportion. Many agents spend heavily on search tools and little on the basics that turn a visit into a call, such as a clear About page, an easy contact form and recent local content.
