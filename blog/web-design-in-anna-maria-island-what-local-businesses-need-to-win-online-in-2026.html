Web Design in Anna Maria Island: What Local Businesses Need to Win Online in 2026

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01One Island, Three City Governments

Anna Maria Island is about seven miles long and contains three separate municipalities: Anna Maria at the north end, Holmes Beach in the middle, and Bradenton Beach to the south. Visitors experience it as one place. Legally and operationally it is not.

That distinction matters more to a business website here than almost anywhere else on the Gulf Coast. Rules on short-term rentals, signage, parking and licensing are set city by city, and a policy that applies to a property on Pine Avenue may not apply to one near Bridge Street.

The practical consequence is that vague location language costs you. A rental listing that says “Anna Maria Island” and nothing more forces a guest to work out which city they are booking in, how far they will be from the shops they saw in photographs, and which set of rules governs their stay.

Name the city and the street

Every property page, every location page and every contact block should state the city, not just the island. “Holmes Beach, two blocks from Manatee Public Beach” answers a question the word “island” leaves open.

For rental operators, the same applies to registration and compliance information. Whichever city you operate in, the license details and house rules should live in a part of the site you can edit yourself, because these rules have changed before and will change again. Tourism has been a stated economic priority on the island since the early 2000s, and the conversion of older homes to short-term rentals has been contested locally for much of that time.

Build the site so a rule change is a five-minute edit, not a call to a developer.

02Designing for a Visitor Who Is Already in the Car

Most business websites are designed for someone sitting at a desk deciding whether to buy. On Anna Maria Island, a large share of your traffic is someone who has already decided, is currently driving over a bridge, and needs one specific fact immediately.

There are only two ways onto the island. State Road 64 crosses the Anna Maria Island Bridge into Holmes Beach from the Palma Sola area of Bradenton, and State Road 684 crosses the Cortez Bridge into Bradenton Beach from the fishing village of Cortez. Both are drawbridges. Both back up in season.

So your visitor is on a phone, on a weak signal, possibly with a passenger reading aloud, and impatient. Design for that person and the desk visitor is also served. Design for the desk visitor only and you lose the one who was ten minutes away.

What that changes in practice

Put the things a moving visitor needs above everything else: whether you are open right now, where exactly you are, whether they can park, and a phone number they can tap once. Not in a footer. Not behind a menu.

Keep the pages light. A design that depends on large background videos and heavy image galleries will fail precisely when it is most needed, which is on a congested island network in March. Compress images properly, avoid decorative scripts, and test the site on a phone with the connection throttled rather than on office wifi.

Make the address work as a destination. The address should be linked so it opens directly in a maps application, and it should match exactly what you have published on your Google Business Profile, because a mismatch between the two sends people to the wrong end of a seven-mile island.

03The Logistics Questions Nobody Answers on Their Site

Island tourism has brought congestion, noise and parking complaints alongside the revenue, and those frustrations shape what visitors search for before they arrive. Most island business websites answer none of it.

The questions are predictable, and answering them is close to free:

  • Where do I park, is it free, and what do I do when it is full.
  • Can I get to you on the free island trolley, and which stop.
  • Do you take reservations, or is it a wait, and how long in season.
  • Are you open today, and are your hours different in summer.
  • Are you dog friendly, family friendly, wheelchair accessible.
  • Is there a minimum stay, and what is the changeover day.

The trolley is worth calling out specifically. A free trolley runs north and south along Gulf Drive for the length of the island, connecting with Manatee County and Sarasota County transit at Manatee Public Beach and Coquina Beach. For a visitor who has given up on parking, “we are a two-minute walk from the trolley stop at…” is the sentence that wins the booking.

A short, honest page covering getting here, parking and the trolley will quietly become one of the most visited pages on your site. It also tends to earn links and shares from rental managers and local guides, which is a genuine visibility benefit rather than a trick.

04Photography That Does Not Look Like Everyone Else's

Everyone on Anna Maria Island has a photograph of a sunset over the Gulf. It is a beautiful image and it sells nothing, because it is indistinguishable from every competitor and from the stock library.

The beach is the reason people come to the island. It is not the reason they choose you over the business next door. Your photography should be doing the second job.

Photograph what a guest is actually uncertain about

For a rental, that is the real bedroom, the actual kitchen, the true view from the balcony including whatever is in the way, the parking space, and the walk to the sand. Honest photographs of an imperfect property outperform flattering ones, because the review that follows a disappointment costs far more than the booking was worth.

For a restaurant, photograph the room at the time of day people will arrive, the front door from the street, and the food as it is actually plated. For a charter, bike or golf cart rental, photograph the equipment and the person handing it over.

This is also where video earns its cost. A thirty-second walk from the parking space to the front door of a rental resolves more guest anxiety than a page of description, and it is content you can reuse across social channels for years.

One practical note: shoot in shoulder season if you can. High-season photography on the island is full of other people's cars, umbrellas and crowds, which is accurate but rarely what you want to lead with.

05Storms, Bridges and Keeping the Site Honest

Any business on a barrier island operates with the understanding that access can be interrupted. Bridges close, power goes out, evacuations are ordered, and seasons end abruptly.

The website is the first place people check, and it is usually the last place anyone updates. That is backwards, and fixing it is mostly a matter of specifying it before the site is built.

Ask for three capabilities:

  • A notice area at the top of every page that you can switch on, write and switch off yourself in a couple of minutes from a phone.
  • Hours and closures stored as editable data rather than written into page text, so changing them does not mean editing six pages.
  • A single page you can point people to for current status, which you can link from social posts and from your Google Business Profile.

Businesses that communicate clearly during a disruption are remembered well afterwards. Businesses whose websites still advertise normal hours during a closure spend the following season repairing the damage.

The same honesty applies to the quiet months. If you close for six weeks in September, say so on the site rather than letting people find a locked door. Guests forgive a closure they were told about.

06Making the Season Last Longer

The island's commercial year is uneven, and the businesses that do best are the ones using the website to flatten it rather than simply riding it.

Winter and spring largely take care of themselves. The work worth doing is aimed at the months either side, and it is content work more than design work.

Three things that tend to pay off:

  • Content aimed at the drive-in Florida market — families from Tampa, Orlando and inland who come for long weekends in summer rather than flying in for a week in March.
  • Direct booking incentives on your own site, so repeat guests have a reason to come back to you instead of to a booking channel that charges commission on a guest you already earned.
  • An email list built from people who have already visited, which for a small island business is usually a more reliable asset than any social platform.

Capture the guest you already have

Someone standing in your rental or eating in your restaurant is the cheapest future customer you will ever get. A simple, visible way to join a list, follow a page or book directly next time turns one visit into several. Most island sites make this hard to find.
