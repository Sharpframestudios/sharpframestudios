What Should Be on a Small Business Homepage? A Section-by-Section Guide

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01A Homepage Has One Job: Answer the Visitor's Questions in Order

Most small business homepages are built from the inside out. The owner decides what is important about the business, stacks it on the page, and hopes the right visitor reads far enough to find what they came for. A visitor does the opposite. They arrive with a handful of questions, ask them roughly in the same order every time, and leave when one goes unanswered.

The Nielsen Norman Group, a research firm that has tested website usability for decades, says in its homepage guidance that effective homepages communicate the purpose of the organization and the site, show engaging content and prompt people to take action. That is a short list, and it is a useful test for every section you add. If a block of the page does not help a visitor understand, trust or act, it is probably taking up room that something else needs.

What follows is a top-to-bottom order for a typical service business, with the question each section has to answer. Your business may reorder a few of these, but the questions stay the same.

02The Top of the Page: What Is This and Is It for Me?

The first screen has to say what you do, who you do it for and where. Nielsen Norman Group recommends that a tagline be brief, simple and to the point, and warns that vague taglines can confuse people or even make them mistrust the site. "Quality solutions for your needs" says nothing. "Roof repair and replacement for homes in Sarasota and Manatee counties" says everything a first-time visitor needs.

Next to that sentence, put the one action you most want a visitor to take. For a plumber it may be a phone number, for a salon a booking link, for a landscaper a quote request. Choose one primary action and make it obvious. Two or three equal buttons ask the visitor to make a decision before they know anything about you.

Also show your business name or logo in a reasonable size and a noticeable place, which is another of the guidelines from that same research group. It sounds too basic to mention, but plenty of sites bury the name in a corner. Add your service area in plain words as well. A Lakewood Ranch visitor wants to know you work in Lakewood Ranch, and a snowbird who is trying to line up work before they arrive wants to know you can be reached while they are still up north.

03Services and Proof: What Do You Do, and Why Believe It?

Directly below the opening, list your main services in short blocks, each with a one-sentence description and a link to a page with more detail. Aim for the three to six things people most often hire you for. A homepage is a menu, and a menu that lists forty items is hard to order from.

Then give a visitor a reason to believe you. The honest forms of proof are the ones you can back up:

  • Real reviews from real customers, quoted with permission and, where the platform allows, linked back to the source.
  • Photographs of your actual work, your actual team and your actual shop or dining room, in place of generic stock images.
  • Licenses, insurance and certifications that apply to your trade, with the numbers or issuing bodies a customer could check.
  • How long you have been in business and, if it is true, how long you have served the area.

Leave out anything you cannot document. An invented testimonial or a badge for an award you did not win does more harm than a short, accurate page. If you are new and have few reviews, show the process instead: how a job runs from first call to finished work, with the steps written plainly.

04Your Process, Your Prices and the Questions People Ask Before Calling

Once a visitor knows what you do and has a reason to trust you, the next question is what working with you is like. A short three or four step description of the process takes the fear out of the first call. Something like: you contact us, we visit or talk through the project, we send a written quote, and we schedule the work. This also filters out poor fits before they take up your time.

Price is the question many owners avoid. You do not have to list every number, but a visitor should be able to tell whether you are a fit. A starting price, a typical range or a plain statement of what drives the cost is better than silence. If you cannot publish a figure, say what the price depends on, such as the size of the job, the materials or the timeline.

A small frequently asked questions block belongs here too. Use it for the questions you hear on the phone every week: whether you are licensed and insured, how quickly you can start, whether you serve a certain neighborhood, how you handle hurricane season scheduling. Write the answers as you would say them to a customer. For a longer example of how this works for one kind of business, our article on what a restaurant website needs shows how the same thinking applies when the visitor is a hungry person with a phone in hand.

05The Bottom of the Page: Where Are You and How Do I Reach You?

Contact details do not belong only on a contact page. Put your phone number, email address, service area and hours at the bottom of the homepage as well, and make the phone number a tap-to-call link on mobile. Nielsen Norman Group also advises including a clear contact link on the homepage that leads to a page with every way of reaching the company.

If customers visit you in person, add a street address, a map link, parking notes and the days you are open. For seasonal businesses, say so directly. A shop in Venice that cuts its hours in summer, or a boat service that books up in the winter months, should say it where visitors will see it, rather than letting them find out by calling a closed number.

End with a second call to action that repeats the first. A visitor who has read the whole page is your most interested visitor, and they should not have to scroll back up to find the button. The footer can carry the less urgent items, such as your social media links and legal pages.

06What to Check Before You Publish

A few technical points decide whether a good layout works in practice. Most of your visitors will be on a phone, and Google says it uses the mobile version of a site's content for indexing and ranking, a practice it calls mobile-first indexing. Google's documentation also says the mobile version of your site should contain the same content as the desktop version, including the same headings and image descriptions. So if your homepage hides a section on mobile, treat that as a content decision and not only a design one. No one can guarantee a position on Google, but a homepage that is clear to a person and consistent across devices gives search engines less to guess about.

Nielsen Norman Group also names four common homepage mistakes: false floors, weak cues that more content sits below the first screen, ignoring familiar web conventions and a muddy visual hierarchy. In plain terms, make sure the page visibly continues past the first screen, keep the menu and logo where people expect them, and let the most important thing be the biggest and clearest thing.

Finally, read the page the way a stranger would. Ask a friend who has never used your service to look at it for ten seconds on their phone, then tell you what the business does and what they would do next. If they cannot, the page needs another pass.
