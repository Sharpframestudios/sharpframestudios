Black Friday for Service Businesses: Does It Make Sense?

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01A Retail Day on a Service Calendar

Black Friday is the day after Thanksgiving, which is November 27 in 2026, according to a NerdWallet article updated on November 17, 2026. If you run a plumbing company, a salon, a bookkeeping practice or a dog grooming van, the day arrives with a small pressure attached. Every store in the country is running a sale, and it can feel like you should be running one too.

The crowd is real. The National Retail Federation said in a release dated November 19, 2026 that an estimated 182.7 million consumers plan to shop during the Thanksgiving weekend, and that 126 million of them plan to shop on Black Friday. Those figures come from a survey of 7,956 adults carried out by Prosper Insights & Analytics, so they describe plans, not completed purchases.

The federation’s forecast for the whole season, released on October 15, 2026, puts November and December retail sales at $1.013 trillion, which would be 4.2 percent above last year. Read the footnote, though. The federation says its definition of retail excludes auto dealers, gas stations and restaurants, and the survey is about holiday shopping for gifts and goods.

So the big numbers measure people buying things. They say very little about whether anyone is in the mood to book a pressure washing visit or a tax consultation that weekend. The useful question for a Black Friday service business offer is narrower: does your particular service fit the way people behave on that weekend, and can you take part without teaching customers that your normal price is negotiable.

02Why a Discount Works Differently on a Service

A store that marks down a television is selling something it already paid for and needs to move. A service business sells hours, and the hours are limited. If your crew can do four jobs a day, a half-price job does not add a fifth. It takes the place of a full-price one.

There is also the question of what the price tells people. A customer cannot inspect a haircut, a roof repair or a set of books before buying, so the price is one of the few clues they have about quality.

Researchers have tested this. A 2010 study in the International Journal of Contemporary Hospitality Management by Khaldoon Nusair, Hae Jin Yoon, Sandra Naipaul and H. G. Parsa ran an experiment on discounts for restaurants, hotels, mailing services and retail services. According to its published abstract, the way a discount was framed and how deep it went both affected what consumers thought of the quality of the service, as well as their view of the deal and their intention to buy. The authors advise service firms to consider the quality image they intend to signal before choosing a discount level.

That is one study of lower-priced services, and the abstract does not say how large the effect was, so do not read it as a rule. It does support what many owners suspect. George Anagnostopoulos, writing on the blog of Alba Graduate Business School in November 2021, argued in an opinion piece that a service bought on promotion is usually not the same as its non-discounted version, because providers often design a thinner product for the deal.

Kris Faraldo of IttyBiz, a training site for solo business owners, made the practical version of the point in a 2020 article. Her example was a consultant who normally charges $250 an hour: cutting that to $150 for a weekend feels like it will cheapen the brand, and the regular clients who paid full rate in October will notice.

03Three Questions That Decide It

Before writing an offer, answer three questions honestly. They matter more than the wording of the offer.

Do you have open capacity? On the Gulf Coast this question often answers itself. Late November is when winter residents are back in Sarasota, Venice and Lakewood Ranch, and many local service businesses are heading into their busiest months. A pool service or a house cleaner with a full December schedule has no reason to sell those slots for less. A business with a slow stretch coming, such as an exterior painter facing a quiet January, has a better case for filling it in advance.

Can someone give your service as a gift? Black Friday weekend is gift shopping. A massage, a round of golf lessons, a family photo session or a mobile car detail can go under a tree. A drain cleaning cannot. If your service is giftable, the weekend suits you well, and the right product is usually a gift card, not a markdown.

Do people decide on it quickly? Nobody replaces a roof because of a weekend sale. For large, considered purchases a 48-hour deadline mostly annoys the careful buyers you want. For small, repeatable services, such as a wash, a trim or a tune-up, a deadline can prompt someone who was already planning to book.

If the answers are no, no and no, skip the day. If one or two are yes, shape the offer around that answer and leave the rest alone.

04Offers That Leave Your Rate Alone

The published advice for service businesses is fairly consistent on one point: add something to the purchase and leave the price where it is. Here are the forms that come up most often, each with a source you can read.

  • Add a small service to a larger one. CallRail, a call tracking software company, gave the example in a November 2022 article of a free winter maintenance visit included with an annual HVAC maintenance contract. Faraldo’s advice is to choose a bonus that is easy for you to deliver.
  • Sell more of the same. Faraldo describes a coaching promotion of 12 hours with 3 more free. By our arithmetic the client pays 20 percent less per hour, but the hourly rate on your price list never changes, and you are paid for a larger block upfront.
  • Sell a package that runs into the new year. Acuity Scheduling, which sells booking software, suggests a three-session package bought now and used later. That turns one weekend into bookings for January and February.
  • Sell prepaid credit. In an Entrepreneur article dated November 10, 2026, Georgi Todorov describes a cake business that mailed handwritten letters to existing customers offering credits redeemable over 12 months. The business reported selling credits for 271 cakes, nearly €16,000 in Black Friday sales. Cakes are a product, but prepaid credit works the same way for an hourly service.
  • Limit it to first-time clients, or cap the quantity. Faraldo names both as ways to keep a promotion from spreading across your whole client list.

Gift cards and prepaid credit come with rules about expiration dates and fees. We went through the Florida rule and the main tools in our post on selling gift cards on your website, so read that before you print anything with an expiration date on it.

Whatever you choose, cap it. Acuity’s article recommends limiting the number of promotional slots and setting a booking deadline. Ten packages is an offer you can honor comfortably. An unlimited one can fill February with work at a rate you did not plan for.

05If You Do Cut the Price, Keep It Honest

Sometimes a plain discount is the right tool. A new business with an empty calendar may reasonably decide that a lower price for first visits is worth it. If you go that way, two things keep it clean.

First, the regular price has to be real. The Federal Trade Commission’s Guides Against Deceptive Pricing, at 16 CFR Part 233, say that a comparison with your own former price is legitimate when the former price is the actual, bona fide price at which the item was offered to the public on a regular basis for a reasonably substantial period of time. The guide calls an inflated former price fictitious.

The guide is written in terms of articles and products, and this post is not legal advice. The standard is still a sensible one for a service. If your website says a detail package is normally $300, it should have been $300 for the customers who booked last month.

Second, write the terms down in plain words and put them where the offer is. Say what is included, who qualifies, how many are available, the last day to buy, the last day to use it, and whether it can be combined with anything else. Most disputes over a promotion start with a term that existed only in the owner’s head.

06Where the Offer Lives, and When to Skip It

A service offer needs one clear home. Put it on a single page of your website with the terms, the deadline and a way to buy or book, and link to that page from everything else. A post on Instagram disappears down the feed in a day, and a customer who half remembers it on Sunday needs somewhere to find the details.

Then tell the people who already know you. Past customers are the most likely buyers of a package or a gift card, and an email to your list costs almost nothing. Add the offer to your Google Business Profile as well, and confirm your holiday hours while you are there. Our Small Business Saturday guide covers those profile steps, and the day after Black Friday may suit a local service business better anyway, since its whole point is choosing a neighbor over a national chain.

Finally, not taking part is a legitimate choice. If your calendar is full, your service is not a gift and your customers take weeks to decide, the best use of the weekend may be a short thank-you note to this year’s clients and nothing else. If you do run an offer, take its page down when the deadline passes. Nobody loses respect for a business because it kept its prices steady in November.
