How Much Does a Professional Website Cost in Siesta Key?

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01Why the Real Question on Siesta Key Is Not “How Many Pages”

Most website quotes are built around page count. On Siesta Key that is close to the least useful way to estimate a project, because the businesses here are not really publishing pages. They are taking bookings.

The Key is a small commercial market with two main clusters. Siesta Key Village, around Ocean Boulevard and Canal Road, holds beach retail, restaurants, salons and a grocery. South Village, at Stickney Point Road and Midnight Pass Road, adds a marina, boutique retail, beach equipment rentals and fishing charters. The free Siesta Key Breeze trolley runs between them.

Almost every business in those two clusters sells something with a calendar attached: a week in a condo, a table at seven, a two-hour charter, a stand-up paddleboard for the afternoon, a cleaning turnover between guests. The website's job is to take that reservation without a phone call.

That single fact is what separates a $4,000 Siesta Key website from a $30,000 one. Not the number of pages. The complexity of what happens after someone clicks “book”.

Everything below is a typical market range for the Sarasota area rather than a quote. Scope sets the price, and scope varies enormously between two businesses that look similar from the street.

02Typical Ranges You Will Actually Be Quoted

Four rough tiers cover most of what gets built on the Key. The bands overlap, and a serious quote will land inside one of them only after someone has asked what your booking process looks like.

A simple presence site: roughly $2,500 to $6,000

Four to eight pages, a template or lightly customized design, your own copy, photography you supply. This is appropriate for a business whose bookings genuinely happen elsewhere — a boutique with no e-commerce, a salon that lives inside a scheduling app, a charter captain who is happy to be called.

It is also the tier where most of the disappointment happens, because businesses buy it expecting the next tier up.

A custom small-business site: roughly $6,000 to $15,000

Eight to fifteen pages, a design made for your brand rather than adapted to it, help with the writing, a content system you can update, analytics and search fundamentals set up properly. Restaurants, spas, real estate agents and most Village retail sit here comfortably.

A rental or booking site: roughly $15,000 to $40,000

Individual property or trip pages, live availability, seasonal rate tables, deposits or full payment, waivers, automated confirmations, and synchronization with the channels you already list on. This is where most Siesta Key vacation rental businesses end up, and the range is wide because the software underneath varies so much.

A larger multi-property or multi-brand build: $40,000 and up

Dozens or hundreds of units, owner portals, housekeeping and maintenance workflows, revenue rules, and integrations with a property management system. Fewer businesses need this than think they do.

03The Booking Layer, and Where the Money Actually Goes

If you run rentals, the largest single line item in the quote is usually not design. It is the connection between the website and whatever system holds your calendar.

Most rental operators on the Key already pay for property management software and already list on the major booking channels. The website has to agree with all of it, permanently. Double bookings are the expensive failure, so this work is done carefully or it is not worth doing.

Three things drive that cost:

  • Whether your management system offers a proper, documented way for a website to read and write bookings, or only a basic calendar feed. A calendar feed is cheaper to build against and much weaker in practice.
  • How many units you have, and whether their rates, minimum stays and fees follow consistent rules or are handled case by case.
  • Whether you want to take payment on your own site or hand the guest off to another platform to pay.

Handing off is far cheaper to build. Taking the booking yourself costs more and avoids the channel commission, which is why many operators eventually pay for it.

For restaurants and charters the same logic applies at a smaller scale. Sending people to an existing reservation or ticketing tool is inexpensive. Building time-slot booking, deposits and waivers into your own site is not.

One honest warning about the cheap version: a site that shows availability but does not truly sync will eventually take a booking for an occupied week. The cost of fixing that with a guest is higher than the cost of building it correctly.

04Photography, Seasons and the Cost of Looking Current

Siesta Key sells on images more than on words. The beach does a lot of the persuading, but the beach is not your product — the unit, the table, the boat and the view from the balcony are.

Supplied photography is free and usually costs you bookings. Professional interior and property photography is a real line item, typically several hundred to a few thousand dollars depending on how many units and how much video is involved. For a rental that earns thousands per week in season, it is one of the easier decisions in the whole project.

Plan the site around a year, not a launch

Siesta Key runs on a calendar. Winter season fills, summer brings families and Florida drive-in traffic, and hurricane season sits across the middle of the year whether anyone mentions it or not. A site that has to be rewritten each time the season turns is a site with a hidden annual cost.

Ask for seasonal rates, minimum stays, hours and closures to be handled as data you can edit, not as sentences buried in a page. This costs a little more up front and removes a recurring bill.

The same applies to storm messaging. A site that lets you put an accurate notice at the top within five minutes — without calling anyone — is worth specifying while the project is being scoped.

It is also worth knowing that Siesta Key is unincorporated Sarasota County, and there has been a long-running local debate about incorporating as a town, which would change who writes short-term rental rules. Build the site so registration numbers, rules and policy text are easy to edit. Regulatory language should never require a developer.

05What Continues After Launch, and What Cheap Sites Leave Out

A website is not a one-time purchase, and quotes that present it as one are usually leaving something out.

Expect ongoing costs in three buckets:

  • Hosting, domain and certificates — modest, often $200 to $600 a year for a small business site.
  • Third-party software — booking, channel management, reservations, email. This is frequently the largest ongoing number and it is paid to the software vendor, not the designer.
  • Care and improvement — updates, monitoring, content changes, fixing what breaks. Typical retainers in this region run from under a hundred dollars a month for light upkeep to several hundred for active work.

What the cheapest proposal is usually missing

Low quotes are not dishonest so much as narrow. The things most often absent are the writing, the photography, real booking integration, analytics and event tracking, search fundamentals, accessibility, and any plan for the site after launch. You can buy those later, generally at a higher total cost than including them from the start.

What makes an expensive site worth the difference

An expensive site earns its price when it removes work or captures revenue. Direct bookings that avoid commission, a form that stops half the phone calls, a structure that lets you add four units without hiring anyone, photography that raises your nightly rate. If a proposal cannot explain which of those it is buying, the extra cost is decoration.

How to get a quote that means something

Before asking for pricing, write down: how many units or services you sell, what software holds your calendar today, whether you want to take payment yourself, how many photographs you have that you would be happy to show a guest, and what you want a visitor to do. Those five answers move a quote from a guess to an estimate.
