Contact Page Best Practices

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01What a Contact Page Is Really For

Most visitors who reach a contact page have already decided they want to talk to you. They are not browsing anymore. They want to know how to reach you, when you will answer, and what happens next, and a page that makes any of those three hard to find loses people at the last step.

A contact page does not need to be clever. It needs to be complete, accurate and quick to use on a phone, because that is where many local searches happen. This post walks through the pieces in the order a customer meets them: the ways to reach you, the form, the practical details, and a few small things that are easy to forget.

One note before we start. Nothing here is a promise about results, and the studies quoted below come from third parties, mostly measuring large collections of forms. Treat them as directions to test, not guarantees.

02Offer More Than One Way to Reach You

People contact a business in different ways. A homeowner with a leaking pipe wants to call. A busy office manager would rather send an email at 9 p.m. A retiree might prefer to phone, while a younger customer may not want to talk at all. Offering two or three options covers most of them.

List the options in a short block at the top of the page, before the form, in a plain order:

  • A phone number that is a tap-to-call link on mobile, so no one has to copy and paste digits.
  • An email address written out as text and also linked, so it works whether or not the visitor has a mail app set up.
  • A contact form for people who want to describe their situation without picking up the phone.
  • A street address, if customers actually visit you, with a link to directions.

Leave out any channel you will not watch. A chat widget nobody answers, or a social media inbox you check once a month, is worse than not offering one, because it sets an expectation you then break. If you do list a social account, say which one you actually read.

A single line of text next to each option helps too. “Call for anything urgent” and “Email for quotes and general questions” tell visitors which door fits their need, and they take some load off your phone.

03Keep the Form Short

The form is where most contact pages succeed or fail. According to Zuko data from 2025, as reported by Crazy Egg in a July 2026 roundup of form statistics, contact forms are among the lowest-converting form types, with about 38 percent of people who interact with one going on to submit it. That figure covers all kinds of businesses and says nothing about yours, but it is a reminder that a form is a small hurdle people can walk away from.

The same roundup cites a 2025 Typeform report finding that lead-capture forms with ten questions averaged 28 percent lower completion than forms with three. Again, this is a third-party average and not a rule. The sensible reading is that every extra field has a cost, so each one should earn its place.

For most local service businesses, a good starting set is short:

  • Name, so you know what to call the person.
  • Email or phone, whichever you will use to reply. Ask for one, and mark the other optional if you want it.
  • A message box, with a hint about what to include.
  • Optionally, one dropdown that routes the request, such as the type of service.

Resist the urge to collect everything up front. You can ask for a street address, a budget or a preferred date during the first reply, once the person has decided you are worth answering. If your team really needs more detail to give a quote, put the extra questions on a separate page that your reply links to.

04Make the Form Easy to Fill In

A short form can still be annoying. A few habits remove most of the friction, and none of them needs special software.

Put each label above its field, in plain words, and keep it visible after someone starts typing. Crazy Egg, citing a 2026 WebAIM report, notes that 33.1 percent of form inputs on the pages WebAIM tested were not properly labeled, which makes forms harder to use with screen readers. Proper labels also help everyone else on a small screen.

Mark which fields are required. Baymard Institute, which researches online checkouts, found that people hit errors when only some fields were marked. Baymard also says its findings apply mostly to long checkout and account forms, and that short contact forms are affected far less. For a three-field form, a simple “required” or “optional” note costs you nothing and removes doubt.

Let the browser help. Crazy Egg cites a 2024 Chrome figure that people using autofill abandoned forms at least 75 percent less often. You support that by using standard field types, so the phone keyboard shows numbers for a phone field and the browser knows an email field is an email.

Finally, write the error messages like a person. “Please add a phone number so we can call you back” is better than “Invalid input.” And when the form is sent, show a clear confirmation on the page itself, not just a quiet change of button color.

05Promise a Response Time You Can Keep

After someone presses send, they wonder whether anything happened. A single sentence under the button answers that: “We reply to every message within one business day.” Choose a promise that matches how you actually work. A plumber who answers within the hour can say so. A solo designer who checks email twice a day should say one business day and mean it.

Send an automatic confirmation email as well, repeating what the person submitted and when to expect a reply. It acts as a receipt, and it catches the case where a visitor mistyped an email address, since the confirmation bounces.

Seasonal rhythm matters on the Gulf Coast. A business that serves snowbirds may get a wave of messages in October and November from people who are not in town yet, and one that closes for a few days around a hurricane warning should say so near the top of the page. If you will be slow to answer, tell people. Honest delay is far better than silence.

06Hours, Address and the Details That Build Trust

Below the form, give the facts a stranger checks before trusting a local business: the full street address, the hours, the service area and, if you have a storefront, a note on parking or where to find the entrance. A map embed can help, but it is optional. A plain link to directions loads faster and works on every phone.

Keep these details identical everywhere they appear. If the footer says you close at 5 p.m. and the contact page says 6, one of them is wrong, and visitors will notice. The same goes for your Google Business Profile, where a mismatch can send someone to a locked door. For a closer look at that listing, see our post on Google Business Profile description examples.

If you want search engines to read the same details, Google documents LocalBusiness structured data, which can describe your address, phone number and opening hours in a machine-readable way. According to Google’s documentation, last updated in September 2026, address is a required property and telephone is recommended, and Google does not guarantee that any feature built on structured data will appear in search results. Nobody can promise a position on Google, so treat this as tidy housekeeping and not as a ranking tactic.

Last, a contact page is a good place for a short human touch. A line about who answers the phone, or a staff photo, makes the page feel less like a form factory. Our post on how to write an About page for a local business covers that kind of trust-building in more depth.

07A Quick Checklist and Where the Page Fits

Before you publish or revise your page, run through it on your own phone:

  • Can you call or email with one tap?
  • Does the form have only the fields you truly need?
  • Are required and optional fields clear, with visible labels?
  • Does the page say when you will reply?
  • Do the address and hours match your other listings?
  • Is there a visible confirmation after sending, and did you test that the message actually reaches your inbox?

That last item deserves a real test. Submit the form yourself every few months. Forms break quietly after a plugin update or an email change, and some businesses lose weeks of inquiries before they notice.
