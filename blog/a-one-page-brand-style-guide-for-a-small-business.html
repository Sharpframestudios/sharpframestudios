A One-Page Brand Style Guide for a Small Business

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01Why One Page Is Enough

Most small businesses hear “brand guidelines” and picture a forty-page PDF from a large company. That is not what a bakery in Venice or a landscaper in Lakewood Ranch needs. What you need is a single sheet that answers the questions people ask every time something new gets made: which logo file, which colors, which fonts, how should it sound, and what should the photos look like.

Without that sheet, decisions get made again each time. The sign shop picks a blue that is close to your blue. A part-time helper writes a Facebook post in a different voice. A printer substitutes a font because yours was not installed. None of it is a disaster, but together it makes a business look less settled than it is.

Vistaprint’s guide to creating a brand style guide, dated November 2025, lists the usual elements as logo usage, a color palette, typography, tone of voice, imagery style, and print and digital specifications. It suggests a clear guide can run two to five pages, which is the guide’s own recommendation. For a small business, one page that covers those basics in plain words is a fine place to start, and you can add pages later if you need them.

If you are still sorting out what a logo is and what a full brand identity includes, our post on logo versus brand identity is a good companion to this one.

02Logo Rules: Which Files, How Much Space, What Not to Do

Start the page with the logo, because it is the piece most likely to be misused. List the versions you actually have: the full logo, a stacked or square version, a one-color version, and a white version for dark backgrounds. Name the files plainly so anyone can find the right one, and note which format to use where, such as a transparent PNG for a social post and a vector file for a printer.

Then write three short rules. First, clear space: leave a margin around the logo that no text or image may enter. A simple measure works, such as the height of the first letter. Second, minimum size: say how small the logo can be on a business card, a pen or a website header before it stops being readable. Third, a short list of things not to do, such as stretching it, recoloring it, putting it over a busy photo or adding a shadow.

Vistaprint’s guide describes the same set of basics: approved versions, clear space, minimum size and examples of correct and incorrect use. Showing two or three “wrong” examples, even rough ones, saves a great deal of explaining later.

A local touch is worth including if your logo ends up in unusual places. A boat lettering job in Bradenton, a truck wrap or a window decal in downtown Sarasota may crop or stretch your logo in ways a business card never would, so note which version works on a long, narrow space and which works in a small circle.

03Colors: A Short Palette With Real Codes

A small business rarely needs more than four or five colors. Pick one or two main colors, one or two supporting colors, and a neutral for backgrounds and text. For each, write the exact values: a hex code for screens such as #1F4E79, and, if you print, the values your printer uses. Do not describe a color as “the teal we like,” because two people will pick two teals.

Next to each color, write what it is for. For example: the main blue for headings and buttons, the warm sand for backgrounds, the deep gray for body text, and the coral only for small highlights. That one line prevents the most common mistake, which is using every color everywhere.

Check that your text colors can be read. The W3C’s explanation of the WCAG 2.2 contrast requirement says normal text needs a contrast ratio of at least 4.5 to 1 against its background, while large text needs at least 3 to 1. In the same document, large text is defined as at least 18 point, or 14 point bold. Free contrast checkers let you paste two color codes and see the ratio.

This matters more than it first appears in Florida. Bright sun on a phone screen washes out pale gray text on white, and many of your customers may be older and need clearer type. A palette that passes the contrast test is easier to read on a bright patio at noon as well as in a quiet office.

04Fonts: Pick Two and Say Where Each Goes

Two typefaces are enough for nearly any small business: one for headings and one for body text. Write the names, the weights you use, such as regular and bold, and a simple rule for each, for example headings in the display face and everything else in the plain one. If you have a third script or decorative font in your logo, say that it is for the logo only.

Add one fallback. Not every device has your chosen font installed, so list a common substitute, such as Arial or Georgia, for documents and emails that cannot use custom fonts. That way a quote sent from a phone still looks tidy.

If you have a website, say where the fonts come from, because the site may load them from a font service while your Word documents use something else. Many free fonts are licensed for both screens and print, but some are not, so read the license before you build your documents around one. Note the license in the guide so the next person does not have to guess.

Keep sizes simple as well. A heading size, a body size and a small-text size are enough. People who read your flyers, menus and web pages will not notice your type choices directly, but they will feel the calm that comes from consistency.

05Voice and Photo Style: The Two Parts People Skip

Voice is how you sound when you write, and it is the part most small businesses leave unwritten. Describe it in three or four words, then give a short example. A family-run HVAC company might be direct, friendly and plain, with a sample such as “We will be there between 9 and 11, and we will call when we are ten minutes away.” A boutique might be warm and a little playful.

Then list a few words and phrases you like and a few you avoid. Vistaprint’s guide mentions preferred and avoided words and sample writing as part of tone of voice. If you hate exclamation points, say so. If you always say “neighbors” and never “consumers,” write that down. This is the page you hand to a part-time helper before they write their first post.

Photo style is the same idea for images. Say whether you favor bright natural light or a darker, moodier look, whether people are in the shot, and what the background usually is. For a Gulf Coast business, a sentence like “real customers and staff, outdoor light, no stock photos of handshakes” is already a useful rule.

Add two or three example photos to the page if you can, along with a line on what to avoid, such as heavy filters or crowded counters. If you take your own pictures, our guide to taking photos on a phone has practical tips that apply well beyond restaurants.

06Put It Together and Keep It Where People Can Find It

Now lay the sections out on one page: logo at the top, colors with codes, fonts, a few lines of voice, and two or three photo examples. Use your own colors and fonts, so the page itself shows how they work together. A simple document or slide, saved as a PDF, is enough. Vistaprint’s guide also lists print specifications such as image resolution, and it gives 300 DPI as the usual recommendation for print.

Save the finished page in a shared folder, and put the logo files beside it. Send the link to anyone who makes things for you: your sign shop, your printer, the person who posts for you and whoever updates the website. Review it once a year, or when you change something major, such as a new location or a new service.

A one-page guide will not guarantee that people choose you, and it will not move you up any list on Google, and nobody can promise it will. What it does is make the work you already pay for look like it comes from one business. It also makes the next project cheaper and faster, because fewer questions have to be asked.
