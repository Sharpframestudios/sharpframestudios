Contractor Websites: The Trust Signals Florida Homeowners Look For

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01Why Trust Comes Before Design for a Contractor

A homeowner hiring a roofer, a remodeler or an electrician is about to hand over a large amount of money and let strangers work on the most expensive thing they own. Most people feel that risk, even if they do not say it out loud. A contractor website has one job before any other: to make that risk feel smaller.

That is different from what a restaurant or a boutique site needs to do. Nobody worries that a bad dinner will cost them thousands of dollars and a ruined ceiling. So the usual advice about clean layouts and nice photography only gets you part of the way. The rest is proof, and proof has to be specific and checkable.

In Florida the proof starts with licensing, because the state regulates the trade and publishes the records. The sections below cover the signals homeowners and their insurers actually look for, in roughly the order a careful buyer would check them. Nothing here guarantees you will rank on Google or win a bid, and no one can promise a search position. What a site can do is remove reasons to hesitate.

02Put Your License Number Where People Can Find It

In Florida, showing the license number is not only good practice, it is a legal requirement for licensed contractors. According to Florida Statute 489.119, the registration or certification number of each contractor must appear in each offer of services, business proposal, bid, contract or advertisement, regardless of medium. The statute states that the first violation brings a notice of noncompliance, with fines or citations for failing to correct it within 30 days or for repeat violations. A website is a medium, so the number belongs on it.

The simplest approach is to put it in the footer of every page, so it appears everywhere without taking attention from the content. Many contractors also repeat it near the contact form and on the About page, in a short line such as the license type, the number and the name of the licensed business. If you hold more than one license, list each one with its own label so a homeowner can look up the one that matches the job.

Make sure the name on the site is the name on the license. Revolution Florida's guide to checking contractors lists a business name on the contract that does not match the licensed entity as a red flag. If you trade under a brand name, say plainly which licensed company stands behind it. A homeowner who searches the number and finds a different company name will assume the worst.

There is also a practical benefit. The Florida Department of Business and Professional Regulation, known as DBPR, advises homeowners to always verify a professional's license with its License Verification Tool on MyFloridaLicense.com. When your number is easy to copy, that check takes a minute, and a contractor who passes it quickly has already started the relationship well. If you want to help, link to the state's lookup page from your site so people do not have to hunt for it.

03Explain What Your Insurance Covers, and Show Proof Without Oversharing

Homeowners are told to ask about insurance, and many do not know what to ask. A contractor site that answers the question before it is asked stands out. DBPR warns that unlicensed contractors may lack proper training, experience and insurance, which exposes homeowners to poor workmanship and financial loss. A licensed, insured contractor who says so clearly is addressing exactly that worry.

Keep this short and factual. Say that the company carries general liability insurance and workers' compensation coverage, if it does, and that a certificate of insurance is available on request. Revolution Florida's guide suggests that homeowners ask for a certificate naming them as the certificate holder and ask whether crew members are employees or subcontractors, so be ready to answer both.

Do not post the full certificate, policy numbers or anything else you would not hand to a stranger on the street. A sentence offering the certificate to any customer who asks is enough. Never state coverage you do not actually carry, because a claim that cannot be backed up is worse than silence.

04Talk About Permits Like Someone Who Pulls Them

Permits are one of the clearest dividing lines between careful contractors and the other kind. DBPR's list of warning signs for homeowners includes a contractor who refuses to obtain required permits, along with demands for a large upfront cash payment, the absence of a written agreement, and unusually low pricing. If your business does the opposite of each of those, your site can say so.

A short section on how your process handles permits works well. Explain that you pull the permits the job requires, that inspections are scheduled through the local building department, and that the permit information is available to the homeowner. You do not need to cite code sections. A plain statement that you handle the paperwork for the customer is more useful than a long explanation.

The same section is a good place to describe the written agreement and payment schedule in general terms. You do not have to list prices. Saying that every job starts with a written scope, a clear payment schedule and a signed contract signals that you run a proper business. After a hurricane or a hailstorm, when out-of-town crews arrive and homeowners are rushed, that signal carries extra weight on the Gulf Coast.

05Show Real Projects, Not Stock Photos

Project photos do more work on a contractor website than almost anything else, and stock photography does the opposite of its job. A homeowner can usually tell when a kitchen or a roof in a photo is not yours. A gallery of your own finished jobs, and ideally a few before and after pairs, shows the quality of the work and proves you have done it.

Each project needs a little context to be useful. A short caption can name the type of work, the neighborhood or town, and what made the job interesting. For example: a tile roof replacement in Lakewood Ranch, a bathroom renovation in a Venice condo, or a screened lanai repaired in Bradenton. Get the customer's permission before you publish photos of their home, and leave out house numbers and anything that could identify an occupant's routine.

A few practical points keep a gallery credible:

  • Use photos taken on the job, not renderings.
  • Show the unglamorous work too, such as framing, flashing or wiring, because it tells buyers you care about what ends up behind the wall.
  • Group projects by service so a homeowner looking for a re-roof does not scroll past bathrooms.
  • Do not claim a project as yours if another company did the work.

06Make Reviews Easy to Verify and Easy to Act On

Reviews are the other half of proof. The BrightLocal Local Consumer Review Survey 2026, which polled 1,002 US adults and was published in February 2026, reports that 97 percent of consumers read reviews when choosing a local business. It also found that 74 percent look for reviews written in the last three months, and that 47 percent will not use a business with fewer than 20 reviews. The survey is not specific to contractors, but the pattern is a fair guide to what a careful homeowner does before calling anyone.

The same survey reports that 54 percent of consumers visit a business's website after reading positive reviews. In other words, the site is often the second stop. If a homeowner arrives from a good Google review and then finds no license number, no photos and a phone number buried at the bottom, the review has been wasted.

On the site itself, link to your Google Business Profile reviews rather than retyping them, so visitors can see that they are real and recent. If you quote customers, use first names and the town only with their permission, and use quotes they actually wrote. Never invent testimonials or edit them to say something different. Replying to reviews, including critical ones, helps too: BrightLocal's survey reports that 80 percent of consumers are more likely to use a business that responds to all of its reviews.

Finally, make it simple to contact you. A phone number that can be tapped on a phone, a short quote request form and a plain statement of the areas you serve do more than a clever slogan. Homeowners with water coming through a ceiling are not browsing, and they will not search for a hidden button.

If most of this is missing from your current site, the fix is mostly writing and photography rather than a rebuild.
