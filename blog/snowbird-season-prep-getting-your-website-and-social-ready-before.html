Snowbird Season Prep: Getting Your Website and Social Ready Before November

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01Why the Work Has to Happen Before the Season Starts

Seasonal residents do not wait for November to start planning. Someone in Ontario or Ohio looks up restaurants, doctors, boat services and landscapers weeks before the drive south, and often decides while still at home. If your website still shows last February's hours and a summer menu, that person gets the wrong first impression from a business that has not even met them yet.

The timing is earlier than many owners assume. According to The Suncoast Post, in an article published on September 28, 2026, snowbird season typically begins in September or October, and local businesses are already reviewing staffing, ordering seasonal inventory and filling event calendars. Florida Smart describes the season as running typically from October or November through April or May. So the useful deadline for your online presence is the end of October, not the day the first license plates from out of state appear.

The scale is worth keeping in mind. The same Suncoast Post article, citing Visit Sarasota County, reports that Sarasota County welcomed 2.71 million visitors in fiscal 2025, who spent $2.3 billion and supported 21,200 jobs. Those figures cover visitors in general rather than seasonal residents alone, but they show how much of the local economy depends on people who are new to your business or returning after months away. Many of them will form an opinion from your website and social pages before they ever walk in.

Nothing in this article is a promise about search rankings, and no one can guarantee a position on Google. What a careful update can do is make sure that when someone does find you, what they see is accurate and current.

02Audit the Facts That Go Stale Over the Summer

Start with a plain read-through of your own site, as if you were a stranger. Summer in a retiree-heavy market is often slower, and many owners trim hours, pause services or run a smaller menu. Those changes tend to get made in a hurry and never reversed on the website.

Check these items in order, because each one costs a customer a trip or a phone call when it is wrong:

  • Opening and closing times, including any day you now close and any evening you now stay open later.
  • Phone number, email address and street address, plus the map link.
  • Menu, price list or service list, and any items that are discontinued or new.
  • Holiday dates, since Thanksgiving and the December holidays arrive quickly.
  • Booking, reservation or quote forms, tested by sending a real message to yourself.
  • Staff names and photos, in case someone has left or joined.
  • Any banner or announcement that still mentions summer.

Write the changes down on one page before you edit anything, then make them all in one sitting. A single owner or manager should be responsible for the final read, since two people editing in parts is how a stale phone number survives. If you hired someone to build the site, send them the list in one message rather than a series of small requests.

For a restaurant, the menu deserves its own pass. Our guide on what a restaurant website needs covers what diners check in the first seconds, and an out-of-date menu or hours line is the quickest way to lose that moment.

03Fix Your Google Business Profile Hours Too

For many seasonal residents, your Google listing is the first thing they see, ahead of your website. It is worth matching it to your site on the same day. According to BrightLocal's guide to Google Business Profile hours, special hours can be added once regular hours are set, and they appear in the Local Pack, Local Finder, Google Maps and the Business Profile itself. The guide says special hours populate your opening hours according to the dates you set.

The same guide describes a Temporarily Closed status for a business that closes for seven or more consecutive days, and notes that extra hour types such as seasonal or delivery hours do not change the main opening time shown in search results. That last point matters for a business whose regular schedule changes by season. If your hours really differ between summer and winter, update the regular hours themselves rather than assuming a secondary label will do the job.

Do the same for any other place people look you up. Facebook, Instagram, Apple Maps and local directories often carry old hours for years. A visitor who finds two different closing times will call to ask, or simply go somewhere else.

One caution applies to every platform. Settings and menu names change, so check the current help pages rather than relying on a screenshot from an old tutorial. If a step does not look the way a guide describes it, trust what is on your screen.

04Refresh Your Photos, Events and Offers

Photos are the next thing to age. A parking lot shown in summer light with no one in it, a patio under construction, a staff photo with people who have since left: all of these are small reminders that the page has not been touched. A short session with a phone, in good light, on a normal busy day will give you a handful of current images. You do not need a studio. You need the place as it looks now.

Events and seasonal offers are where the season actually shows up. Think about what your regular winter customers ask for every year, such as early-bird times, group bookings, holiday gatherings, a reservation policy for a full house, or a standing weekly event. Put the first month of these on the site as plain text with dates, and add the rest as they are confirmed. A calendar with only two dates is more useful than a blank page.

A local angle helps when it is real. A Lakewood Ranch landscaper might note which neighborhoods and associations the crew already serves, a Venice boat service might say when it books haul-outs, and a Sarasota gallery can list its opening nights. A fake local mention, like a list of towns stuffed into a footer, does nothing for a reader and can look careless. Say what is true about your business and leave the rest out.

Because storms are a fact of life on the Gulf Coast, it is also sensible to keep a short note ready on your site and social pages explaining how customers will hear about closures. You do not need to post it now. Having the wording prepared means the first message goes out calmly, not in the middle of a storm.

05Plan Your Social Posts and Reviews for the First Six Weeks

Social media is where returning customers are reminded that you are open. Instead of posting whenever there is time, plan the first six weeks in advance and put them in a simple calendar. A good mix is a welcome-back message, one post about what is new, one about the people who work there, one that answers a common question, and one about an event or special.

Frequency is a question of what your team can keep up. Our article on how often a business should post on social media walks through realistic cadences, and the short version is that a steady, modest schedule beats a burst in October followed by silence. Pick a rhythm you can still maintain in February, when the season is at its busiest and you have the least spare time.

Reviews deserve a thought as well. Seasonal customers often look at recent reviews, and a listing whose latest comment is from last spring can look dormant. A polite request at the point of service, such as a card at the register or a line on the receipt or confirmation email, tends to work better than a mass message. Reply to the reviews you already have, including the critical ones, in a calm and specific way. Never write or buy reviews, and do not offer a reward in return for one.

Finally, do a last check from the customer's side. Search for your business on a phone, tap through to your website, read the hours, find the phone number, and look at the most recent posts. If anything is wrong, fix it today.
