How Often Should a Business Post on Social Media? A Platform-by-Platform Answer

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01Why Every Answer You Find Disagrees

Search for how often a business should post on social media and you will find advice ranging from a few times a week to several times a day. The numbers differ because the sources measure different things. Some report what large brands with dedicated teams actually do, some analyze which accounts got more reach, and some repeat what a platform's own staff have said in public.

For a small business with one or two people handling everything, most of that advice is written for someone else. A restaurant in Lakewood Ranch, a boat detailer on Longboat Key or a two-chair salon in Venice does not have a content team. The useful question is not what the ideal number is, but what number you can keep up for a year without the quality falling apart.

That is also what the better sources say once you read past the headline figures. This article walks through what three well-known social media software companies report for each major platform, where they disagree, and how to turn the ranges into a plan a small team can actually follow.

02What the Published Numbers Say, Platform by Platform

The figures below come from guides published by Buffer, Hootsuite and Sprout Social. Treat them as ranges, not rules, and note that each company sells tools for scheduling and analytics, so their guidance leans toward posting regularly.

Instagram

According to Buffer, accounts posting three to five times a week saw about 12% more reach per post than accounts posting once or twice a week, and its data showed further gains at higher volumes. Hootsuite also recommends three to five feed posts a week, plus Stories about twice a day for teams that can manage it. Sprout Social's guide suggests one to two posts a day, and notes that large enterprise brands post considerably more than mid-size companies. For a small team, the lower end of the Buffer and Hootsuite range is a sensible start.

Facebook

Hootsuite recommends one to two posts a day, and Buffer gives the same range, citing a study by HubSpot. Sprout Social's guide points to a much higher volume, up to five times a day, which is clearly a figure for larger brands with a lot of content to repurpose. A local business is often better served by a few strong posts a week than by daily filler. Hours changes, events and photos of real work tend to do more than generic quotes.

LinkedIn

Buffer says the real turning point comes when an account moves from one post a week to two to five. Sprout Social calls two to five posts a week an optimal balance, while Hootsuite's range is higher, at one to two a day. If your customers are other businesses, such as a contractor or accountant, two or three posts a week is a reasonable range. If your customers are homeowners and retirees, LinkedIn may deserve less of your time.

TikTok

Buffer recommends two to five posts a week and reports that moving from one weekly post into that range brought up to 17% more views per post. Hootsuite suggests three to five a week. Sprout Social cites official TikTok guidance of one to four videos a day, though it also notes that most brands post closer to two a week. Video takes real production time, so this is often where small teams overcommit.

Google Business Profile

Hootsuite lists Google Business Profile posts at least once a week. These are short updates that appear on your Google listing, and they are a low-effort place to share an offer, an event or a change in hours.

03Why Consistency Beats Volume

Buffer's guide puts it plainly: the right number is the number you can stick to. Hootsuite makes a similar point, quoting one of its own social media managers who says that if your team cannot post once a day on each network, aim for every other day, and that three quality posts are worth more than five low-quality ones. Sprout Social's analysis of nearly three billion messages from about one million public profiles found that the average across platforms had fallen to 9.5 posts a day from 11 in 2022, and it reads that as a shift toward quality over quantity.

Those three sources agree on the main point, even where their numbers do not. An account that posts three good things every week for a year usually tells a customer more about your business than one that posts daily for a month and goes silent. Long gaps also leave a page looking abandoned, which is a real problem when a potential customer checks your Facebook page before calling.

It is worth being honest about what the data cannot tell you. The reach and view figures above describe averages across many accounts, and posting more often does not cause growth on its own. Nobody can promise that a given schedule will produce followers, customers or a position in anyone's feed.

04A Realistic Cadence for a Small Team

Start smaller than you think you should, then add only when the routine feels easy. Here is a sample for a business with one person handling social media a few hours a week.

  • Instagram: three posts a week, plus a few Stories when something is happening in the shop, kitchen or job site.
  • Facebook: three posts a week, reusing the Instagram photos with slightly longer captions, and one post for anything time-sensitive.
  • Google Business Profile: one short update a week.
  • LinkedIn: one or two posts a week if you serve other businesses, otherwise share your best posts occasionally.
  • TikTok: skip it until you have a repeatable way to shoot video, or start with one short video a week.

Choose two platforms and do them well before adding a third. Pick the ones where your customers already are. A landscaper may find Facebook and Instagram are enough, while a business that sells to other companies may want LinkedIn ahead of Instagram.

Repurposing keeps the workload honest. One afternoon of photos from a project, a kitchen or a storefront can become a feed post, a few Stories, a Facebook update and a Google Business Profile note. Rewrite the caption for each place rather than pasting the same text everywhere, and keep a running list of simple ideas: a before and after, a question customers ask often, a team member, a seasonal reminder.

After eight weeks or so, look at what you have. Note which posts drew comments, messages or phone calls, not only likes, and which took the most effort for the least result. Then adjust the cadence up or down. A schedule that fits your week and your customers will beat a perfect one that you abandon in March.

Batching helps more than any posting schedule. Spend an hour every week or two taking photos, writing captions and scheduling posts, instead of squeezing content into busy days. It is also worth building the calendar around your local rhythm: the snowbird season from late fall to spring, holiday weekends, and the weeks before and after hurricane season, when customers need to know your hours and whether you are open.
