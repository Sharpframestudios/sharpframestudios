Website or Landing Page: What Does a Brand-New Business Need First?

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01Two Different Jobs

When people ask whether they need a website or just a landing page, they are usually asking about size and cost. The more useful question is what the page has to do. A landing page is a single page built around one action, such as calling, booking or leaving an email address. A website is a set of pages that answers the many questions a customer might have before choosing you.

According to an April 2026 article from Best Version Media by a web designer, “a landing page is usually one focused page with one goal: get the visitor to take one action.” That is a fair definition, and it also shows why the two are not rivals. One is a doorway, the other is a building.

For a brand-new business, the doorway is often the right place to begin. You may not have a finished logo, a full list of services, photos of completed work or a single review yet. A full website built before those things exist tends to be thin, and thin pages do not help anyone.

02What One Strong Page Can Do

A single page can carry more than people expect. It can say what you do, who it is for, where you work, how to reach you and what happens next. For a new house cleaner in Lakewood Ranch or a mobile dog groomer in Venice, that is often all a first-time customer needs to make a call.

The same article makes the point plainly: if your goal is leads this week, it suggests starting with a landing page and driving traffic to it from sources such as ads, referrals, local listings and social media. Notice the condition. A landing page works when people are already being sent to it, because by design it is not a page that explores the rest of your business.

A good first page usually includes the following:

  • A headline that states the service and the area you cover in plain words.
  • Two or three short paragraphs on what you offer and how a job works.
  • A phone number and a simple contact form or booking link near the top.
  • A few real photos, even if they are from your own first jobs.
  • Your hours, your service area and a short note about who runs the business.

Keep it honest. If you have no reviews yet, leave the reviews section out instead of filling it with invented praise. A customer can tell the difference, and so can the platforms that publish the reviews.

03Where a Single Page Starts to Strain

A landing page begins to struggle when you have more than one story to tell. If you offer four different services, each with its own customers and questions, they will not fit on one screen without becoming a wall of text. If people ask the same questions again and again, such as how much a visit costs or whether you serve a certain neighborhood, that is a sign those answers deserve their own pages.

The Best Version Media article suggests that if your goal is ranking on Google and growing steadily, you should start with a full website, even a smaller version. It also describes a hybrid plan: launch a landing page first to start generating calls, then build out the website in phases, with service pages first. That fits how many new businesses actually grow, because the site follows the work instead of guessing at it. Keep in mind the author runs a web design firm, so read it as one practitioner’s view and not a rule.

There is also the matter of trust. A one-page site can look like a placeholder to a careful buyer, especially someone choosing a contractor or a financial professional. Retirees and snowbirds who are new to the area often check several sources before they call, and a few clear pages with an address and a real name can reassure them. Our post on whether you still need a website if you have Instagram and Google goes into what a profile alone cannot show.

04What Google Does and Does Not Need

Many new owners assume that a bigger site is the only way to be found. It is more modest than that. Google’s SEO Starter Guide, last updated in December 2025, says Google uses automated programs called crawlers, and that you usually do not need to do anything except publish your site on the web. It also says Google primarily finds pages through links from other pages it has already crawled.

The same guide is careful about promises. It states that there is no guarantee any particular site will be added to Google’s index, and that changes can take from a few hours to several months to show up. In other words, nobody can promise you a position on Google, and a single page and a thirty-page site are in the same position on that point. If your new page is slow to appear, our article on why a new website may not show up on Google covers the usual causes.

Your Google Business Profile is a separate matter, and it is worth setting up early. According to Google’s Business Profile guidelines, a business with a physical location customers can visit, or one that travels to customers, can create a profile. In the website and phone section, the guidelines ask for a phone number that connects to your business or a website that represents it, so a website is one of two options and not a requirement. Even so, a page you control gives that profile somewhere to send people.

05A Simple Path From One Page to Many

A staged approach lets you spend less at the start and learn from real customers before you commit to a bigger build. Here is one way to sequence it:

Stage one is the focused page, live as soon as you can describe your service clearly and take a call. Stage two, a month or two in, is adding the questions people actually asked you, perhaps as a short FAQ. Stage three is a page for each main service, with its own photos and details. Stage four is the full site with an about page, a gallery of finished work and reviews you have earned.

Along the way, choose a domain name you plan to keep, and register it in your own name from the start. Our guide on where to buy and keep your business domain explains why. A page that moves to a new address later should be set up to forward visitors, so your early listings and printed cards keep working.

Seasonal timing matters on the Gulf Coast. If your customers arrive with the snowbirds in November, a clear page live in October is worth more than a perfect site in March. Hurricane season is another reason to keep a simple, fast page that you can update quickly with closures and hours.

06How to Decide

Ask yourself three questions. Can you describe your service in a few sentences? Do you already have a way to send people to the page, such as referrals, a listing or social posts? And do you have enough different services and answers to fill more than one page? If the answers are yes, yes and no, start with the single page. If the last answer is yes, plan for a small website from the beginning.

If you are unsure which fits, look at what belongs on the homepage of a full site in our section-by-section homepage guide. If you can fill most of those sections honestly today, you are probably ready for more than one page.
