Jewelry Business Websites

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01Start With What You Actually Sell

People who search for the best website builder for a small jewelry business usually expect a single name in reply. The better answer depends on what leaves your bench. A studio that sells forty finished pairs of earrings a month, a goldsmith who makes six engagement rings a year, and a shop on Main Street that mostly does repairs and resizing need three different websites.

Sort your sales into three groups before you compare anything. Finished pieces that can be bought as shown. Custom work that begins with a conversation. Services such as repair, resizing, restringing and appraisals.

The builder matters most for the first group, because that is where a cart, stock counts and shipping come in. For the other two, the website is closer to a portfolio with a good inquiry form. Whichever group is largest, three things decide whether a visitor trusts the site enough to act: the photographs, how accurately the pieces are described, and how clearly custom work is explained. The rest of this post takes those in turn, after a short look at the builders themselves.

02The Builders, Briefly, With Current Prices

Two names come up most often. Shopify is built around the store. According to Shopify’s own pricing page, read on November 25, 2026, the Basic plan is $39 a month, or $29 a month when paid yearly, with online card rates from 2.9% plus 30 cents.

Squarespace is built around the pages and adds a store to them. Its pricing page, read the same day, shows the Basic plan at $25 a month, with a lower figure of $19 shown for annual billing, and the Core plan at $39 and $29. The detail that matters to a jeweler is in the fee table. Squarespace lists a 2% online store transaction fee on Basic and 0% on Core, on top of a standard card rate of 2.9% plus 30 cents on both.

On jewelry a 2% fee is not small. Take a $1,200 ring. By our arithmetic on those published rates, the card fee is $35.10, and the extra 2% on the Squarespace Basic plan adds another $24 to that single sale. Prices and fees change often, so read both pricing pages yourself before you choose.

A maker with dozens of finished pieces in several sizes or metals, who ships regularly, will be glad of Shopify’s inventory tools. A jeweler whose site is mainly a gallery and a custom inquiry form, with a handful of pieces for sale, can do well with something lighter. We compared the store-first and simpler routes in more detail in our post on boutique clothing websites.

A custom-designed site is the third route, and it suits a jeweler whose work is mostly commissions. Scope drives the price of that kind of project, so there is no single figure to quote. Our pricing section shows how we structure it.

03Photography: The Customer Cannot Pick It Up

In a shop, a customer turns a ring under the light, tries it on, and feels the weight. Online, the photographs have to do all of that.

You do not need a studio. You need the same simple setup every time, so the whole catalog looks like one collection.

  • Use soft daylight from a window, with a sheet of white paper or card opposite to fill the shadows. Turn the overhead lights off, since mixed light changes the color of gold.
  • Put the phone on a small stand. A steady camera is most of what makes a small object sharp.
  • Wipe every piece before you shoot. Fingerprints and dust that nobody notices in the hand are obvious in a close photograph.
  • Take the same set for each piece: front, side, back or clasp, one on a hand, ear or neck for scale, and one close detail of the setting or texture.

The photograph that shows scale is the one owners leave out most often, and it prevents the most disappointment. A picture on a person, plus measurements in the description, settles the question before the box is opened.

Resist heavy editing. Brightening a stone or warming the gold may make a prettier picture, but a customer who receives a paler sapphire than the one on the screen will send it back and may say so in a review. For one-of-a-kind work, say in the listing that the piece photographed is the piece that will be shipped.

04Trust: Describe the Piece the Way the Rules Expect

A stranger is being asked to spend real money on something small that they have not seen. Accurate wording is the first thing that earns that trust, and it is also a matter of federal guidance. The Federal Trade Commission publishes a plain-language summary of its Jewelry Guides called In the Loupe: Advertising Diamond, Gemstones and Pearls. The page is dated July 2019, and it describes itself as a summary that does not replace the Guides.

Several points in it apply directly to a product page. This is general information, not legal advice.

  • Laboratory-grown stones. The FTC says to describe them with a term such as laboratory-grown or laboratory-created placed immediately before the word diamond or the name of the stone, and just as conspicuously.
  • Imitation stones. The summary says to call them imitation or simulated, again immediately before the stone name, and not to use the word faux for laboratory-created or imitation stones.
  • Treatments. According to the FTC, a seller should disclose a gemstone treatment if it is not permanent, if it creates special care requirements, or if it significantly affects the value of the stone. The summary says this applies to online advertisements where a person can buy without seeing the item.
  • Pearls. The FTC says the word pearl should not be used alone unless the product contains only natural pearls. Cultured pearls should be called cultured, and a type name by itself is not enough: the summary gives Akoya cultured pearls as the correct form.

The FTC summary deals with stones and pearls. It notes that the full Guides also cover gold, silver and platinum, so if you describe metal content, read that part of the Guides or ask your trade association before you write your product template.

The practical step is to build these fields into every listing so nothing depends on memory: metal, stone type with the right qualifier, any treatment, weight, dimensions, and care.

The other trust signals are simpler. Put the return and resizing policy where it can be found from every product page. Explain how a parcel is insured and whether a signature is required. Show a real address or at least the town, a phone number, and the person who makes the work. Our post on how to write an about page covers that last part.

05Custom Orders: Explain the Process Before Anyone Asks

Custom work is where most jewelry websites say the least. A line that reads custom orders welcome, with an email address, leaves the visitor to guess at everything that matters: what it might cost, how long it takes, and what happens to Grandmother’s ring once it is handed over.

A custom page should walk through the process in order.

  • How it starts: a form, a call, or an appointment at the studio, and what to bring or send, such as photographs, a ring size or a budget.
  • What happens next: sketches or a rendering, how many rounds of changes are included, and when the design is considered final.
  • Money: the deposit, when the balance is due, and what is refundable if the customer changes course.
  • Time: a realistic range from approval to delivery, and the order deadlines for the dates people care about.
  • Heirloom stones and old gold: how they are recorded when received, and what you can and cannot reuse.

Be exact about time, because a federal rule is involved. According to the FTC’s business guide to its Mail, Internet, or Telephone Order Merchandise Rule, a seller must have a reasonable basis for any shipping time it states, and if it states none, a reasonable basis for believing it can ship within 30 days. The guide says the clock begins when the seller receives a properly completed order, which includes full or partial payment. If the date cannot be met, the guide says the seller must seek the customer’s consent to the delay or refund the money. The portion of the guide we read does not list an exception for made-to-order goods, so state your real timeline in writing, and ask an attorney if your terms are unusual.

On this coast the calendar adds one more wrinkle. Winter residents may want a piece finished before they go north in spring, and many commissions are tied to a holiday or an anniversary. Keep the current lead time on the custom page and update it each month.

06A Simple Way to Decide

Go back to the three groups from the start and let the largest one choose the tool.

  • Mostly finished pieces, shipped often: a store-first platform, with the product template described above.
  • Mostly custom work: a gallery of past commissions, a clear process page and a good inquiry form. A cart is optional.
  • Mostly repairs and services: a simple site with services, typical turnaround, hours and directions, and a few pieces for sale if you like.

Whatever you choose, test it the way a customer would. Open the site on a phone, find one piece, and try to learn what it is made of, how big it is, what it costs, and how to return it. If any of those is hard to find, fix that before changing platforms.
