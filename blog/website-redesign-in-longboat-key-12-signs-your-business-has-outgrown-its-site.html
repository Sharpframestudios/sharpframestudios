Website Redesign in Longboat Key: 12 Signs Your Business Has Outgrown Its Site

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01Who Is Reading a Longboat Key Website, and When

Longboat Key does not behave like the barrier islands around it. There is no day-tripper economy to speak of, no strip of shops to drift through, and no volume of walk-ins to absorb a weak first impression. The town's own description is blunt about it: Longboat Key has no industrial development and limited commercial uses, and it reached near build-out around the year 2000. What happens here now is redevelopment, maintenance and service to people who already live on the island or own a home on it.

That shapes who reads your website. The Census Bureau puts the town at 7,359 residents, 68.4 percent of them 65 or over, with a median household income of $158,030, a median owner-occupied home value of $1,022,100, and 95.5 percent of households on broadband. The town reports a median resident age of 71.5 and a taxable value base that reached $8.3 billion in fiscal 2024. These are not casual buyers, and they are not price shopping on a phone in a parking lot.

They are also frequently not here. The permanent population sits near 7,500 and climbs toward roughly 20,000 in the winter months. For a large share of the year, the person evaluating your business is reading from Michigan, New Jersey or Ontario, in August, planning work they want finished before they come back in November. Your website is not supporting a conversation. It is the entire conversation.

The island is also changing at the top end. The St. Regis Longboat Key opened in October 2024 with 168 rooms and 26 suites, described as the largest construction project on the island in fifty years. Expectations of finish, service and presentation have moved, and clients who spend time in that environment carry those expectations into how they judge a contractor, designer, practice or management company.

Twelve signs your site has fallen behind that audience, grouped by the kind of failure rather than scattered as a list.

02Signs One to Three: It No Longer Matches the Work You Take

The most common problem on Longboat Key is not an ugly website. It is an accurate website that describes the business from five years ago, before the work got larger and the clients got more particular.

Sign one: the site sells a smaller version of you

If you now handle full condominium renovations but the site still reads like a handyman service, or you run a design practice but the pages describe furniture sourcing, every inquiry arrives pre-framed at the wrong scale. You then spend the first call resetting expectations, and you lose the clients who never called because they assumed you were too small.

Sign two: there is no way to signal what a project costs

On an island where the median home is valued above a million dollars, the mismatch cuts both ways. A site with no indication of range attracts inquiries from people who want a weekend job and repels people who assume anyone without a price is either expensive in a bad way or not serious. You do not need a price list. You need a clear statement of the kind of project you do well and roughly what that involves.

Sign three: the portfolio is thin, generic or old

Stock photography reads as evasion to this audience, and eight-year-old project shots read as a business that has stopped. If your best recent work is on a phone and never made it onto the site, the site is now actively understating you. Real, current, well-photographed work is the single highest-return content a Longboat Key service business can publish.

03Signs Four to Six: It Does Not Survive the Credibility Check

Buyers here verify. They have time, they have advisors, and in many cases they have a condominium board or a neighbor with an opinion. A website that cannot withstand fifteen minutes of scrutiny quietly loses without anyone telling you.

Sign four: licensing, insurance and credentials are not stated

License numbers, insurance coverage, bonding, professional memberships and years in business are not decoration. For work inside a building with an association, they are the gate. Leaving them off does not look modest. It looks like something to look up, and looking things up is where you lose the thread.

Sign five: there is no evidence you have worked in this kind of property

Much of the island is mid-rise and high-rise condominium, gated single-family and club communities, each with its own approval process, access rules, elevator scheduling, noise windows and construction calendar. A contractor, cleaner, installer or management firm that can show it has navigated that is worth more than one that is simply competent. Say plainly which kinds of properties and associations you work in and what your process looks like inside them.

Sign six: there are no people on the site

A client who is handing over a key to a home they will not see for four months is making a trust decision, not a purchase. Named people, real faces, tenure, who will actually show up and who answers the phone. Businesses that hide behind a logo are asking an older, careful, remote buyer to take a risk they have no reason to take.

04Signs Seven to Nine: It Assumes the Client Is Already Here

A great many Longboat Key websites are written as though the reader is on the island, free to call during business hours and able to stop by. For much of the year that is wrong, and it produces friction that never shows up as a complaint.

Sign seven: contact means a phone number and a short form

Someone in a different time zone, planning ahead, needs more than a call button. Give them the ability to send detail, attach photographs of the room or the issue, and choose a time to talk. Say when you respond and who responds. An inquiry from an absentee owner is usually well considered and high value, and it is exactly the kind that a thin contact page discourages.

Sign eight: it is hard to read for the audience it serves

With more than two thirds of residents past 65 and a median age above seventy, light gray text at small sizes, low-contrast overlays on photographs, labels hidden inside form fields and carousels that move on their own are not stylistic choices. They are a way of losing the customer. Set type larger than a design award would suggest, keep contrast genuine, and never put a price, a phone number or an address inside an image.

Sign nine: nothing explains how you work at a distance

Can you do a walkthrough by video? Do you send photo updates? How do you handle access, keys, deliveries and payment when the owner is two thousand miles away? Home watch, property management, renovation and maintenance businesses on this island live or die on those answers, and most sites do not attempt them. Writing them down is often the difference between an inquiry and a booked job.

05Signs Ten to Twelve: It Cannot Hold the Island's Calendar

Longboat Key runs on an inverted rhythm. The busy social season is roughly November through April, but for the trades the real working window is summer, when owners are away and a project can run without anyone living through it. A site that treats the year as flat misses both halves.

Sign ten: it cannot say what you are booking and when

The message that converts in July is not the message that converts in January. Summer is when you sell work completed before the owner's return. Season is when you sell availability, response time and someone who can come today. If changing that message means asking a developer, the site will carry the wrong one for most of the year.

Sign eleven: routine updates are a project

Adding a service, a team member, a new community you cover or a set of project photographs should take minutes. When it does not, content stops being added, and a site that has not changed in two years reads as a business winding down. That impression is very hard to overcome with an audience that is explicitly checking whether you are still there.

Sign twelve: you cannot see which inquiries the site produced

Volume is low here and value per client is high. A handful of inquiries a month can be an excellent result, which is exactly why guessing is expensive. Track form submissions, calls, and which pages preceded them. Without that, a quiet month looks like a marketing failure when it may be a seasonal pattern, and you end up rebuilding the wrong thing.

06Whether to Repair or Rebuild

Several of these signs can be fixed without a redesign. Adding credentials, publishing recent work, rewriting a contact page for a remote client and enlarging the type are a week of focused effort, and on Longboat Key they often move more work than a new visual direction would.

A rebuild earns its cost when the underlying structure is the constraint. That usually means you cannot publish work easily, cannot add a service or a property type without distorting the menu, cannot connect the site to scheduling or client communication, or are working around a platform decision made for a much simpler business. Those limits compound, and the cheapest moment to resolve them is before another season passes.

Either way, start with the same question the client is asking. Can this business be trusted with my home, from a distance, while I am not watching? Everything useful on a Longboat Key website is an answer to that, and most of what is missing is missing because nobody framed the site that way.
