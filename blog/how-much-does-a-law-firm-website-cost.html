How Much Does a Law Firm Website Cost?

By Sharp Frame Studios · Back to the journal ↗

01Why the Published Numbers Disagree

Search for law firm website cost and you will find confident answers that do not match one another. They come mostly from agencies that sell these sites, so they are estimates from sellers and not survey data. Read side by side, they still show a pattern.

Civille, a legal marketing company, says in a guide by Calvin Krusick, last modified in July 2026 according to the page’s metadata, that most solo and small firms pay between $1,000 and $10,000 upfront for a templated or semi-custom site, and it puts premium builds at $15,000 to $50,000 or more. Timmermann Group, in a guide by Rob Timmermann dated November 14, 2025, lists $2,000 to $8,000 for a freelancer, $5,000 to $15,000 for a basic agency project and $10,000 to $35,000 or more for a full-service one. For a 20-page legal website, the same guide says to expect $8,000 to $25,000.

The ranges are wide because that the two firms at either end are not buying the same thing. A solo estate planning attorney in Venice with five pages and a phone number is at one end. A twelve-lawyer firm in downtown Sarasota with four practice groups, a Bradenton office and an intake system tied to its case software is at the other.

This post is about the build, and about the four things that push a law firm’s price up or down inside those ranges. The bills that arrive every year afterward are a separate subject, covered in our post on what a website costs each year after launch.

02Practice Area Pages: Mostly a Writing Bill

On most business sites, a service page is a few paragraphs and a photo. On a law firm site, each practice area page has to explain a legal problem to a worried person who has never dealt with it before, accurately, in plain words. That is slow work, and it moves a quote more than almost anything else.

Page counts make the point. Kinex Media, a Canadian agency whose guide was last updated on September 23, 2026, describes a basic template site as 4 to 6 pages and a mid-range professional site as 15 to 30. It does not say which currency its dollar figures are in, so treat them as rough. The difference between those two sites is almost entirely practice area pages, attorney pages and the articles that support them.

Then comes the cost of filling each page. Kinex puts professional legal copywriting at $100 to $300 per page, and Timmermann Group gives $100 to $500 per page. Take a probate and estate planning practice that wants separate pages for wills, revocable trusts, probate administration, guardianship, powers of attorney and Medicaid planning. That is six pages of careful writing before the homepage.

Civille’s guide adds a useful warning. It says the number of pages matters less than most people think, and that if a vendor is offering 30 pages for $2,000, you should ask who is writing the content. For any quote, ask whether the writing is included, who does it, and whether a lawyer at the firm will have time to correct it.

03Attorney Bios, Photos and a Second Office

People hire a lawyer, not a logo, so the attorney pages on a law firm site get read closely. Each one needs a real photograph, education and bar admissions, the kinds of matters the lawyer handles, and a way to reach that person. Civille’s guide notes that more attorneys on the team tends to mean a bigger site, because each one needs an individual page.

Photography is its own line. Timmermann Group lists legal photography at $500 to $2,000. A firm with consistent, recent portraits saves that money. A firm whose headshots were taken in three different decades usually ends up paying for a session, because mismatched portraits are the first thing a visitor notices on a team page.

Offices add pages too. Timmermann Group says a firm with multiple locations needs separate pages for each one, with specific content about that area. For a firm with offices in Sarasota and Lakewood Ranch, that means two pages with different addresses, parking directions, hours and the names of the attorneys who actually sit there, not one page copied twice with the town name swapped.

04Intake: From a Contact Form to a Connected System

Intake is the part of a law firm site with the widest spread between simple and elaborate. At the simple end is a contact form that sends an email to the front desk. At the other end is a form that asks different questions for a probate matter than for a real estate closing, offers a consultation time, and creates a record in the firm’s practice management software without anyone retyping it.

The published figures follow that spread. Kinex lists online intake forms at $300 to $1,500 and a client portal with a secure login at $1,500 to $5,000 or more. Timmermann Group puts customer relationship software integration at $500 to $1,500 on a basic agency project and $1,000 to $3,000 with marketing automation on a full-service one. Its guide also explains why the work costs what it does: intake forms and automation seem simple but require careful development and testing, and scheduling has to work with the firm’s existing calendar and practice software.

There is some evidence that intake matters. An article by Patrick Allen Wright in the American Bar Association’s Law Practice magazine, in its March and April 2025 issue, cites a secret shopper study from Clio’s 2024 Legal Trends Report in which only 40 percent of law firms answered phone calls, down from 56 percent in 2019. A form that works at nine at night catches some of the people a ringing phone would lose.

Clio’s own summary of its 2025 report for solo and small firms says firms using online intake forms saw shorter hiring times, by 9 percent for solos and 5 percent for small firms. Clio sells this kind of software, so read that as a vendor’s finding. The same summary says 59 percent of solo and small firms report that referrals are their highest source of leads, which is a good reason not to overbuild. A referred client mostly wants to confirm that the lawyer is real and find the phone number.

A sensible middle path for a small firm is a short form that collects a name, a way to reach the person and the general type of matter, with a plain sentence saying that sending the form does not create an attorney-client relationship and asking people not to include confidential details. The exact wording is the firm’s decision, not the designer’s.

05Compliance Review: Hours, Not a Fee

A law firm website in Florida is advertising, and it has to follow the Bar’s advertising rules. What it does not have to do is go through the Bar’s filing process. Rule 4-7.20(g), as reproduced on floridajustice.com with a note that it was last amended effective July 14, 2025, exempts information contained on a lawyer’s own website from the filing requirement.

So there is usually no filing fee attached to the site itself. If a firm wants the Bar’s view on something specific, Rule 4-7.19(d) allows a lawyer to ask for an advisory opinion on a particular page, statement or photograph, though not on an entire website. The current rule text says the fee is set by the Bar’s executive director with the approval of the Board of Governors, so check the Bar’s site for the amount before you budget for it.

The real compliance cost is time. Somebody at the firm, normally a partner, has to read every headline, bio, testimonial and case description before the site goes live, and then the designer has to make the changes. A firm that plans for it finishes on schedule. A firm that does not may find the site sitting finished and unpublished, waiting for an hour nobody has.

None of the four cost guides we read for this post mentions bar advertising review at all, which may be why it surprises people. What the rules actually require of a site, from office locations to testimonials, is covered in our post on law firm website design and the Florida Bar advertising rules. We are a design studio, not a law firm, and none of this is legal advice.

06How to Get a Quote You Can Compare

Two quotes for a law firm website are only comparable if they describe the same site. Before asking anyone for a price, write down a few facts and send the same list to everyone.

  • The practice areas that need their own page at launch, and the ones that can wait.
  • How many attorneys and staff need a bio, and whether current photographs exist.
  • How many offices, and whether any pages need a second language.
  • What should happen when someone fills out the form: an email, a booked consultation, or a record in the software the firm already uses.
  • Who writes the text, and which attorney reviews every page before launch.

Then ask each company the same questions back. Is the writing included, and for how many pages? Who owns the domain, the site and the content if the firm leaves? What does the site cost each month after launch, and what does that cover? Civille’s guide, for one, pairs a templated agency site with $400 to $1,500 per month for hosting, maintenance, SEO and marketing, so the build price alone tells you little.

Be wary of any quote that includes a promised position in search results. No one can guarantee where a firm appears on Google, and a lawyer who is careful about promising case outcomes should expect the same care from the people building the site.
